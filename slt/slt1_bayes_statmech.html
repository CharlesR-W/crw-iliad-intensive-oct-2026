Question: what does Bayes do with n samples, and how much does it generalize?

Bayesian inference over the parameters of a model has the same form as the Boltzmann distribution of statistical mechanics, with the training loss as the energy and the sample size n as the inverse temperature. This notebook sets up that dictionary, says where it is only formal, and then uses it to answer the question above for regular models, the case covered by classical statistics: the posterior shrinks onto the best parameter like n^{-1/2} (§1.2), the free energy is the best fit plus \tfrac d2\log n (§1.3), and the Bayes predictor generalizes with error d/2n, where d is the number of parameters (§1.4). Notebook 2 asks what replaces d/2 when the model is not regular.

Bayesian Interpolation, MacKay (Neural Computation 1992) Importance ●●●●● Length ●●●○○

The evidence Z_n as the quantity Bayes uses to compare models, and the Occam factor: the evidence is the best-fit likelihood times the fraction of the prior volume the data leave standing, so a model with more parameters pays for them by volume (§1.3). Sections 1–4 are what matters here. The same text is chapter 2 of MacKay’s thesis, free as a PDF.

Route. Each section opens with its question and restates the notation it uses.

  • 1.2 Posterior concentration: for large n the posterior is a Gaussian of width n^{-1/2} around the maximum-likelihood estimate.

  • 1.4 Bayes generalization error: d/2n for the Bayes predictor, d/n for a posterior sample, and how the maximum-likelihood fit compares when the model is wrong.

Setup used throughout. Data x_1,\dots,x_n are drawn independently from an unknown distribution with density q(x). (For supervised learning x is an input–output pair and the model is a conditional density; nothing below changes.) A model is a family of densities p(x\mid w) indexed by a parameter w\in\mathbb R^d, and a prior \varphi(w) is a smooth positive density on \mathbb R^d. The per-sample loss is the negative log-likelihood \ell(x,w)=-\log p(x\mid w), and

L_n(w)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(x_i,w)\quad\text{(training loss)},\qquad L(w)=\mathbb E_{x\sim q}[\ell(x,w)]\quad\text{(population loss)}.

Writing S=-\mathbb E_q[\log q(x)] for the entropy of the data distribution, L(w)=S+\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p_w), so minimizing the population loss means finding the model closest to q in Kullback–Leibler divergence. Let w_0 be a minimizer of L and define the excess losses K(w)=L(w)-L(w_0)\ge0 and K_n(w)=L_n(w)-L_n(w_0). The model is well specified if q=p_{w_0} for some w_0, so that K is the KL divergence to the truth; otherwise it is misspecified.

def log_trapz(logf, x):
    # log of the trapezoid-rule integral of exp(logf) over the 1-D grid x,
    # computed in log space so that very small integrands do not underflow
    m = np.logaddexp(logf[:-1], logf[1:]) - np.log(2)
    return logsumexp(m + np.log(np.diff(x)))

1.1 The dictionary

Question: what does Bayes’ rule look like when written in the language of statistical mechanics, and which parts of the correspondence are more than notation?

Notation: p(x\mid w) model, \varphi(w) prior, \ell(x,w)=-\log p(x\mid w), L_n training loss, L population loss, K=L-L(w_0) (see the setup above).

Bayes’ rule multiplies the prior by the likelihood of the data and normalizes:

\pi_n(w)=\frac{\varphi(w)\prod_{i=1}^np(x_i\mid w)}{Z_n}, \qquad Z_n=\int\varphi(w)\prod_{i=1}^np(x_i\mid w)\,dw .

Since \prod_ip(x_i\mid w)=e^{-nL_n(w)}, the posterior is \pi_n(w)=\varphi(w)e^{-nL_n(w)}/Z_n: a Boltzmann distribution with energy nL_n(w) at inverse temperature 1, or equally with energy L_n(w) at inverse temperature n. The normalizer Z_n is the probability of the data under the model as a whole (the evidence, or marginal likelihood), and in the dictionary it is the partition function. Its negative logarithm F_n=-\log Z_n is the free energy; lower free energy means the model, with its prior, gave the data higher probability.

It is useful to add a knob. For \beta>0 define the tempered posterior \pi_{n,\beta}(w)\propto\varphi(w)e^{-\beta nL_n(w)} with partition function Z_n(\beta) and free energy F_n(\beta)=-\log Z_n(\beta). Only \beta=1 is Bayes; the other values are a mathematical device that Notebook 3 will need. It is also convenient to factor out the best achievable fit: with K_n=L_n-L_n(w_0),

Z_n=e^{-nL_n(w_0)}\,\bar Z_n,\qquad \bar Z_n=\int\varphi(w)e^{-nK_n(w)}\,dw, \qquad F_n=nL_n(w_0)+\bar F_n .

The first term is the loss of the best parameter on this data set; everything interesting about the shape of the posterior is in \bar F_n=-\log\bar Z_n.

Where the dictionary is only formal. Several things a physicist would take for granted do not hold. The “energy” nL_n(w) depends on the random sample, so the system has quenched disorder: every statement about F_n is a statement about a random variable. The sample size n is not a system size: there is no thermodynamic limit in which d and n grow together with intensive quantities fixed, and F_n is not extensive in anything. Temperature is not a physical knob: \beta\ne1 is not Bayesian inference, and nothing here says what happens if one trains a network at some learning rate. The posterior is a static object; there are no dynamics, no equilibration, and no claim that any training procedure samples it (whether stochastic gradient descent does is a separate question, not treated in these notebooks). Finally the prior is a weight on parameters, not a count of states at a given energy; the true analogue of the density of states is the volume of parameters at a given loss, which Notebook 2 introduces. What does hold exactly is the algebra: identities of the form “derivative of \log Z equals a posterior average” are the same identities as in statistical mechanics, and the exercises below are the first of them.

Exercise 1.1 (Posterior as a Boltzmann distribution). Let U\subseteq\mathbb R^d be a region of parameter space, and define the local partition function and free energy Z_n(U)=\int_U\varphi(w)e^{-nL_n(w)}dw and F_n(U)=-\log Z_n(U).

  1. Show that \pi_n(w)=\varphi(w)e^{-nL_n(w)}/Z_n with Z_n=\int\varphi e^{-nL_n}, i.e. that the likelihood is e^{-nL_n}.
  2. Show that the posterior mass of U is \pi_n(U)=Z_n(U)/Z_n, and that for two regions \log\frac{\pi_n(U)}{\pi_n(V)}=F_n(V)-F_n(U): posterior odds are exponential in free-energy differences.
  3. If U_1,\dots,U_k partition parameter space, show F_n=-\log\sum_je^{-F_n(U_j)}, and that if F_n(U_1) is the smallest then F_n(U_1)-\log k<F_n<F_n(U_1). So the total free energy is set by the region of lowest free energy, up to \log k.
  4. Show that \partial_\beta F_n(\beta)=n\,\mathbb E_{\pi_{n,\beta}}[L_n(w)] and \partial^2_\beta F_n(\beta)=-n^2\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{n,\beta}}[L_n(w)]. (In the dictionary: the derivative of the free energy with respect to inverse temperature is the internal energy, and F_n(\beta) is concave.)
Solution

1. \prod_ip(x_i\mid w)=\exp\sum_i\log p(x_i\mid w)=\exp(-\sum_i\ell(x_i,w))=e^{-nL_n(w)}. Substituting into Bayes’ rule gives the stated form, and Z_n is whatever makes \pi_n integrate to one.

2. \pi_n(U)=\int_U\pi_n=\frac1{Z_n}\int_U\varphi e^{-nL_n}=Z_n(U)/Z_n. Then \pi_n(U)/\pi_n(V)=Z_n(U)/Z_n(V)=e^{-F_n(U)}/e^{-F_n(V)}, and taking logs gives F_n(V)-F_n(U).

3. The integral over a disjoint union is the sum of the integrals, so Z_n=\sum_jZ_n(U_j)=\sum_je^{-F_n(U_j)}; take -\log. For the bounds: the sum is at least its largest term e^{-F_n(U_1)} and at most k times it, so e^{-F_n(U_1)}\le Z_n\le ke^{-F_n(U_1)}, which after -\log is the stated sandwich. (The lower inequality is strict as soon as another region has nonzero mass.)

4. Z_n(\beta)=\int\varphi e^{-\beta nL_n}, so \partial_\beta Z_n=-\int nL_n\,\varphi e^{-\beta nL_n} and \partial_\beta F_n=-\partial_\beta Z_n/Z_n=n\,\mathbb E_{\pi_{n,\beta}}[L_n]. Differentiating once more, \partial_\beta\mathbb E_\beta[nL_n]=\partial_\beta\frac{\int nL_n\varphi e^{-\beta nL_n}}{\int\varphi e^{-\beta nL_n}}=-\mathbb E_\beta[(nL_n)^2]+\mathbb E_\beta[nL_n]^2=-n^2\mathrm{Var}_\beta[L_n]. A variance is nonnegative, so F_n(\beta) is concave in \beta. These are the usual relations \langle E\rangle=\partial_\beta(\beta F) and \mathrm{Var}(E)=-\partial_\beta\langle E\rangle; Notebook 3 turns the first one into an estimator.

1.2 Posterior concentration

Question: what does the posterior look like when n is large?

Notation: \hat w is the maximum-likelihood estimate, a minimizer of the training loss L_n; w_0 minimizes the population loss L; H=\nabla^2L(w_0) and H_n=\nabla^2L_n(\hat w) are Hessians (matrices of second derivatives); C=\mathrm{Cov}_{x\sim q}[\nabla_w\ell(x,w_0)] is the covariance of the per-sample gradient at w_0. A model is regular at w_0 if w_0 is the unique minimizer of L and H is positive definite. This section assumes that.

Two facts drive everything. First, the posterior density is \varphi(w)e^{-nL_n(w)} up to normalization, and nL_n grows linearly in n while \varphi does not, so for large n the exponential wins: the posterior is squeezed onto the minimizer \hat w of L_n, and only the quadratic term of L_n around \hat w survives. Second, \hat w is itself random, since L_n is built from a random sample, and the central limit theorem says how it scatters around w_0. The result, known as the Bernstein–von Mises theorem, is

\pi_n(w)\approx\mathcal N\!\big(\hat w,\,(nH_n)^{-1}\big), \qquad \hat w-w_0\approx\mathcal N\!\big(0,\,\tfrac1n H^{-1}CH^{-1}\big).

Both widths scale as n^{-1/2}. The first is what the posterior believes; the second is how the estimate actually fluctuates. In a well-specified model C=H and the two covariances agree; in a misspecified model they do not, and the posterior is overconfident or underconfident depending on H^{-1}C.

You may use the central limit theorem in this form: the average of n independent copies of a random vector with mean zero and covariance C is approximately \mathcal N(0,C/n) for large n.

Exercise 1.2 (The Gaussian posterior).

  1. Expand nL_n(w) to second order about \hat w (note \nabla L_n(\hat w)=0) and show that, if the prior is replaced by the constant \varphi(\hat w), the posterior is exactly \mathcal N(\hat w,(nH_n)^{-1}).
  2. Why can the prior be replaced by a constant, and why does the cubic term of the Taylor expansion not matter, for large n? Argue by scaling: write w=\hat w+u/\sqrt n and look at the size of each term.
  3. Show that \hat w-w_0\approx-H^{-1}\nabla L_n(w_0), and that \nabla L_n(w_0) is an average of n independent mean-zero vectors with covariance C. Conclude the second display above. (Why is the mean zero?)
  4. (The information identity.) Suppose the model is well specified, q=p_{w_0}. Show that C=H.
    HintStart from \int p(x\mid w)\,dx=1 for every w and differentiate twice with respect to w; use \nabla^2p=p\,(\nabla^2\log p+\nabla\log p\,\nabla\log p^\top).
Solution

1. nL_n(w)=nL_n(\hat w)+\tfrac n2(w-\hat w)^\top H_n(w-\hat w)+R, with R cubic and higher. Dropping R and setting \varphi\equiv\varphi(\hat w), the posterior is proportional to \exp[-\tfrac12(w-\hat w)^\top(nH_n)(w-\hat w)], which is the density of \mathcal N(\hat w,(nH_n)^{-1}) up to its normalizing constant.

2. With w=\hat w+u/\sqrt n the quadratic term is \tfrac12u^\top H_nu, of order one, so u ranges over an O(1) region: the posterior has width n^{-1/2} in w. The cubic term is n\cdot O(|w-\hat w|^3)=O(n\cdot n^{-3/2})=O(n^{-1/2}) on that region, and higher terms are smaller still. A smooth prior changes by O(n^{-1/2}) across a region of width n^{-1/2}. So both corrections vanish as n\to\infty, at rate n^{-1/2}. (This is the regular case: if H_n had a zero eigenvalue the quadratic term would not confine u in that direction, and the higher terms would decide the width. That is Notebook 2.)

3. 0=\nabla L_n(\hat w)\approx\nabla L_n(w_0)+\nabla^2L_n(w_0)(\hat w-w_0), and \nabla^2L_n(w_0)\to H by the law of large numbers, so \hat w-w_0\approx-H^{-1}\nabla L_n(w_0). Now \nabla L_n(w_0)=\frac1n\sum_i\nabla\ell(x_i,w_0) is an average of independent copies of the vector g=\nabla\ell(x,w_0). Its mean is \mathbb E_q[\nabla\ell(x,w_0)]=\nabla L(w_0)=0 because w_0 minimizes L (interchange of gradient and expectation), and its covariance is C by definition. The central limit theorem gives \nabla L_n(w_0)\approx\mathcal N(0,C/n), and a linear image -H^{-1}(\cdot) of a Gaussian is Gaussian with covariance H^{-1}(C/n)H^{-1}.

4. Differentiate \int p(x\mid w)dx=1 once: \int\nabla p=0, i.e. \mathbb E_{p_w}[\nabla\log p(x\mid w)]=0. Differentiate again: \int\nabla^2p=0, and with the hint \mathbb E_{p_w}[\nabla^2\log p]+\mathbb E_{p_w}[\nabla\log p\,\nabla\log p^\top]=0. Evaluate at w=w_0 with q=p_{w_0}. Since \ell=-\log p, the first term is -\mathbb E_q[\nabla^2\ell(x,w_0)]=-H and the second is \mathbb E_q[\nabla\ell\nabla\ell^\top], which equals C because the mean of \nabla\ell(x,w_0) is zero. Hence H=C. Under misspecification the two derivatives are taken under p_{w_0} but the expectations defining H and C are under q, and the argument fails; in general H\ne C.

1.3 Laplace and the BIC

Question: what is the free energy F_n=-\log Z_n for large n, and what does it reward and penalize?

Notation: as in §1.2: \hat w minimizes L_n, H_n=\nabla^2L_n(\hat w) is positive definite, d is the number of parameters.

Laplace’s method evaluates an integral \int g(w)e^{-nf(w)}dw for large n by noting that it is dominated by the neighbourhood of the minimum of f, replacing f there by its quadratic expansion and g by its value at the minimum, and doing the resulting Gaussian integral. The one formula needed is the normalization of the multivariate normal distribution,

\int_{\mathbb R^d}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12u^\top Au\big)\,du=(2\pi)^{d/2}(\det A)^{-1/2} \qquad(A\text{ positive definite}).

Applied to Z_n=\int\varphi(w)e^{-nL_n(w)}dw this gives

Z_n\approx\varphi(\hat w)\,e^{-nL_n(\hat w)}\Big(\frac{2\pi}n\Big)^{d/2}(\det H_n)^{-1/2}, \qquad F_n=nL_n(\hat w)+\frac d2\log n+\frac12\log\det H_n-\log\varphi(\hat w)-\frac d2\log2\pi+o(1).

The first two terms are the Bayesian information criterion (BIC): the free energy is the best achievable training loss plus half a \log n per parameter. The remaining terms are O(1) and are what model comparison at finite n actually hinges on, but they do not grow with n.

MacKay’s reading of the same formula: Z_n=(\text{best-fit likelihood})\times (\text{Occam factor}), where the Occam factor \varphi(\hat w)(2\pi/n)^{d/2}(\det H_n)^{-1/2} is, for a prior spread evenly over some volume, the ratio of the posterior’s volume to the prior’s. A model with more parameters can reach a better fit, but each extra parameter has a prior width that the data shrink to n^{-1/2}, and the evidence is charged for that shrinkage. Nothing in the argument involves held-out data; the penalty comes from volume alone.

Exercise 1.3 (Laplace’s method and the Occam factor).

  1. Derive the two displayed formulas for Z_n and F_n from the Gaussian integral.
  2. Suppose the prior is uniform over a box of volume V_\varphi containing \hat w. Define the posterior volume V_{\rm post}=\int e^{-n(L_n(w)-L_n(\hat w))}dw (the volume of parameter space, weighted by how much worse than the best fit each point is). Show that Z_n=e^{-nL_n(\hat w)}\,V_{\rm post}/V_\varphi exactly, and that Laplace’s method gives V_{\rm post}\approx(2\pi/n)^{d/2}(\det H_n)^{-1/2}. This is the Occam factor as a volume ratio.
  3. Two models A and B have d_A<d_B parameters and best training losses L_n^A\ge L_n^B (the larger model fits at least as well). Using only the BIC terms, when does Bayes prefer the smaller model? Express the condition as a bound on the improvement in fit per sample.
  4. (A case where everything is exact.) Model p(x\mid w)=\mathcal N(x;w,1) with prior \varphi=\mathcal N(0,\tau^2). Compute Z_n exactly, write F_n in the form nL_n(\hat w)+\tfrac12\log n+(\text{rest}), and compare with the Laplace formula. Show that the two differ by O(1/n).
    Hint\sum_i(x_i-w)^2=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2+n(w-\bar x)^2, so nL_n(w)=nL_n(\bar x)+\tfrac n2(w-\bar x)^2 exactly; the integral against the Gaussian prior is then one Gaussian integral.
Solution

1. Expand nL_n(w)=nL_n(\hat w)+\tfrac12(w-\hat w)^\top(nH_n)(w-\hat w)+\dots (the gradient vanishes at \hat w), set \varphi(w)\approx\varphi(\hat w), and apply the Gaussian integral with A=nH_n: (2\pi)^{d/2}(\det nH_n)^{-1/2}=(2\pi/n)^{d/2}(\det H_n)^{-1/2}, using \det(nH_n)=n^d\det H_n. Taking -\log gives F_n. The o(1) collects the prior’s variation and the cubic terms, both O(n^{-1/2}) by Exercise 1.2.2, together with the O(1/n) corrections to the Gaussian integral itself.

2. With \varphi=1/V_\varphi on the box (and the box containing all the posterior mass that matters), Z_n=\frac1{V_\varphi}\int e^{-nL_n(w)}dw=\frac{e^{-nL_n(\hat w)}}{V_\varphi}\int e^{-n(L_n(w)-L_n(\hat w))}dw, which is the claim. Laplace’s method on V_{\rm post} is part 1 with \varphi\equiv1. So the evidence is the best-fit likelihood times V_{\rm post}/V_\varphi: the fraction of prior volume that remains compatible with the data. Each parameter contributes a factor (posterior width)/(prior width) \sim n^{-1/2}/\text{(prior width)}, which is where \tfrac d2\log n comes from.

3. F^A-F^B\approx n(L_n^A-L_n^B)-\frac{d_B-d_A}2\log n. Bayes prefers A (lower free energy) when L_n^A-L_n^B<\frac{d_B-d_A}{2n}\log n: the larger model must improve the per-sample fit by more than \frac{\log n}{2n} per extra parameter to be preferred. For fixed losses the right-hand side goes to zero, so any genuine improvement eventually wins; but a model that only fits noise improves the per-sample loss by O(1/n) (the fit improvement from \Delta d extra parameters is about \frac{\Delta d}{2n}, Exercise 1.4.3), which is smaller than \frac{\Delta d}{2n}\log n. That is the sense in which the BIC penalty rejects parameters that only fit noise.

4. By the hint, \prod_ip(x_i\mid w)=e^{-nL_n(\bar x)}e^{-\frac n2(w-\bar x)^2} with \hat w=\bar x and nL_n(\bar x)=\frac n2\log2\pi+\frac12\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2. Then Z_n=e^{-nL_n(\bar x)}\int\frac{e^{-w^2/2\tau^2}}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau^2}}e^{-\frac n2(w-\bar x)^2}dw =e^{-nL_n(\bar x)}\,\frac1{\sqrt{1+n\tau^2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{n\bar x^2}{2(1+n\tau^2)}\Big), (complete the square: the product of the two Gaussians in w has precision n+1/\tau^2, and the leftover constant is a Gaussian in \bar x with variance \tau^2+1/n). So F_n^{\rm exact}=nL_n(\bar x)+\tfrac12\log n+\tfrac12\log\!\big(\tau^2+\tfrac1n\big)+\frac{\bar x^2}{2(\tau^2+1/n)} . Laplace: H_n=1, d=1, -\log\varphi(\hat w)=\tfrac12\log(2\pi\tau^2)+\bar x^2/2\tau^2, so F_n^{\rm Lap}=nL_n(\bar x)+\tfrac12\log n+\tfrac12\log\tau^2+\frac{\bar x^2}{2\tau^2}. The difference is \tfrac12\log(1+\tfrac1{n\tau^2})+\frac{\bar x^2}2\big(\frac1{\tau^2+1/n}-\frac1{\tau^2}\big)=\frac1{2n\tau^2}-\frac{\bar x^2}{2\tau^2(n\tau^2+1)}+O(n^{-2})=O(1/n). Laplace is exact up to the 1/n corrections from the prior’s curvature, as it should be: here the likelihood is exactly Gaussian in w and the only approximation is \varphi\approx\varphi(\hat w).

    n = len(x)
    # n L_n(mu, s) = n s + n/2 log 2pi + exp(-2s) * sum (x_i - mu)^2 / 2
    ss = ((x[None, None, :] - MU[:, :, None]) ** 2).sum(-1) if n <= 300 else \
         n * ((MU - x.mean()) ** 2 + x.var())           # same thing, computed from sufficient statistics
    nL = n * S + 0.5 * n * np.log(2 * np.pi) + 0.5 * np.exp(-2 * S) * ss
    return -(logsumexp(log_prior - nL) + np.log(dmu * ds))

             + 0.5 * (mu_hat**2 + s_hat**2) + np.log(2 * np.pi) - np.log(2 * np.pi))
    return nL_hat, F_lap

1.4 The Bayes generalization error

Question: how well does the posterior predict the next sample, and how does that compare with using the maximum-likelihood fit?

Notation: q data distribution with entropy S; \pi_n posterior; \hat w maximum-likelihood estimate; w_0, H, C as in §1.2; L_{\min}=L(w_0)-S=\min_w\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_w), the smallest loss the model can reach, zero if well specified.

There are three natural predictors. The Bayes predictive distribution averages the model over the posterior, p^*(x)=\int p(x\mid w)\,\pi_n(w)\,dw. A Gibbs predictor draws one w\sim\pi_n and uses p(x\mid w). The maximum-likelihood predictor uses p(x\mid\hat w). Their generalization errors are the expected loss on a fresh sample, minus the entropy, which is the part of the loss no predictor can remove:

G_n^{\rm Bayes}=\mathbb E_{x\sim q}[-\log p^*(x)]-S=\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p^*),\qquad G_n^{\rm Gibbs}=\mathbb E_{w\sim\pi_n}[L(w)]-S,\qquad G_n^{\rm MLE}=L(\hat w)-S .

All three are random (they depend on the training sample); the statements below are about their expectations over the sample. The reason the free energy belongs in this story is an exact identity: appending one more sample x_{n+1} to the data set and recomputing the free energy,

-\log p^*(x_{n+1})=F_{n+1}-F_n , \qquad\text{so}\qquad \mathbb E[G_n^{\rm Bayes}]=\mathbb E[F_{n+1}]-\mathbb E[F_n]-S .

The Bayes generalization error is the free-energy cost of one more datum, minus the entropy. Every asymptotic statement about F_n therefore becomes a statement about generalization, and that is how Notebook 2’s volume exponent will enter generalization too. In the regular case the results are

predictor expected generalization error, regular model
Bayes predictive L_{\min}+\dfrac{d}{2n}
Gibbs (one posterior sample) L_{\min}+\dfrac{d+\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)}{2n}
maximum likelihood L_{\min}+\dfrac{\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)}{2n}

up to o(1/n). Well specified, C=H and \mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)=d: Bayes and maximum likelihood both pay d/2n and a single posterior sample pays twice that. Misspecified, the Bayes predictive still pays exactly d/2n above the best the model can do, while the other two pay \mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)/2n, which can be larger or smaller than d/2n. So “Bayes generalizes better than maximum likelihood” is not a theorem even for regular models; what is true is that averaging over the posterior removes the dependence on C.

Solution

1. Z_{n+1}=\int\varphi(w)\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}p(x_i\mid w)dw=\int p(x_{n+1}\mid w)\,\varphi(w)\prod_{i\le n}p(x_i\mid w)\,dw=Z_n\int p(x_{n+1}\mid w)\pi_n(w)dw=Z_np^*(x_{n+1}). Take -\log. Averaging over x_{n+1}\sim q and over the first n samples gives \mathbb E[-\log p^*(x_{n+1})]=\mathbb E[F_{n+1}]-\mathbb E[F_n], and subtracting S gives the Bayes generalization error.

2. From §1.3, F_n=nL_n(\hat w)+\tfrac d2\log n+\tfrac12\log\det H_n-\log\varphi(\hat w)-\tfrac d2\log2\pi+o(1). Expand L_n about w_0 instead of \hat w: with g_n=\nabla L_n(w_0) and \hat w-w_0\approx-H^{-1}g_n, nL_n(\hat w)\approx nL_n(w_0)+ng_n^\top(\hat w-w_0)+\tfrac n2(\hat w-w_0)^\top H(\hat w-w_0)=nL_n(w_0)-\tfrac n2g_n^\top H^{-1}g_n. Now \mathbb E[nL_n(w_0)]=nL(w_0) exactly, and \mathbb E[ng_n^\top H^{-1}g_n]=n\,\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}\mathbb E[g_ng_n^\top])=\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C). The remaining terms converge to constants, giving c. Then \mathbb E[F_{n+1}]-\mathbb E[F_n]=L(w_0)+\tfrac d2\log\frac{n+1}n+o(1)\text{-difference}=L(w_0)+\tfrac d{2n}+\dots, so \mathbb E[G_n^{\rm Bayes}]\approx L(w_0)-S+\tfrac d{2n}=L_{\min}+\tfrac d{2n}. The step of faith is that the difference of the o(1) remainders between n and n+1 is o(1/n); a term like \mathbb E[\log\det H_n] does change by O(1/n) between n and n+1, so this needs the direct computation of part 3 (or Watanabe’s theorems) to be trusted. Part 3 confirms the result.

3. Fluctuation of the fit. L(\hat w)-L(w_0)\approx\tfrac12(\hat w-w_0)^\top H(\hat w-w_0), and with \hat w-w_0\approx\mathcal N(0,H^{-1}CH^{-1}/n) its expectation is \tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(H\cdot H^{-1}CH^{-1}/n)=\tfrac1{2n}\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C). This is already the MLE row: \mathbb E[G^{\rm MLE}_n]=L(w_0)-S+\tfrac1{2n}\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C).

Gibbs. \mathbb E_{\pi_n}[L(w)]\approx L(\hat w)+\tfrac12\mathbb E[\delta^\top H\delta]=L(\hat w)+\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(H(nH)^{-1})=L(\hat w)+\tfrac d{2n}. Adding the fluctuation of the fit gives the Gibbs row, L_{\min}+\frac{d+\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)}{2n}.

Bayes. Write \log p(x\mid w)=-\ell(x,\hat w)+s^\top\delta+\tfrac12\delta^\top M\delta+\dots with s=-\nabla\ell(x,\hat w) and M=-\nabla^2\ell(x,\hat w). Then \log p^*(x)=-\ell(x,\hat w)+\log\mathbb E_\delta[e^{Y}] with Y=s^\top\delta+\tfrac12\delta^\top M\delta, and \log\mathbb E[e^Y]\approx\mathbb E[Y]+\tfrac12\mathrm{Var}[Y]=\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(M(nH)^{-1})+\tfrac12s^\top(nH)^{-1}s+O(n^{-2}). So -\log p^*(x)\approx\ell(x,\hat w)-\frac1{2n}\big[s^\top H^{-1}s+\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}M)\big]. Average over x\sim q (with \hat w\approx w_0 inside the 1/n terms): \mathbb E_q[ss^\top]=C and \mathbb E_q[M]=-H, giving \mathbb E_x[-\log p^*(x)]\approx L(\hat w)-\frac1{2n}[\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)-d]. Finally average over the training sample using the fluctuation of the fit: L(w_0)+\frac1{2n}\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)-\frac1{2n}\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)+\frac d{2n}. The C-dependence cancels and the Bayes row is L_{\min}+\frac d{2n}. Physically: the maximum-likelihood estimate is too far from w_0 by an amount set by C, and the posterior average, by spreading over a region of the same size as that error, happens to pay back exactly that amount.

4. With a flat prior the posterior is \mathcal N(\bar x,1/n) and the predictive is \mathcal N(\bar x,1+1/n). For x\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2), S=\tfrac12\log(2\pi e\sigma^2) and L(w)-S=\tfrac12(\sigma^2+w^2)-\tfrac12\sigma^2-\tfrac12=\tfrac12(w^2+\sigma^2-1), so L_{\min}=\tfrac12(\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2), which is \ge0 with equality only at \sigma^2=1. Then, writing v=1/n and G for the error minus L_{\min}: G^{\rm MLE}=\tfrac12\bar x^2,\qquad G^{\rm Gibbs}=\tfrac12\big(\bar x^2+v\big),\qquad G^{\rm Bayes}=\tfrac12\log(1+v)+\frac{\sigma^2+\bar x^2}{2(1+v)}-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}. (The Bayes value is \mathbb E_x[-\log\mathcal N(x;\bar x,1+v)]-S-L_{\min}.) Taking expectations with \mathbb E[\bar x^2]=\sigma^2/n: \mathbb E[G^{\rm MLE}]=\frac{\sigma^2}{2n}, \mathbb E[G^{\rm Gibbs}]=\frac{\sigma^2+1}{2n}, and \mathbb E[G^{\rm Bayes}]=\tfrac12\log(1+\tfrac1n)+\frac{\sigma^2(1+1/n)}{2(1+1/n)}-\frac{\sigma^2}2=\tfrac12\log(1+\tfrac1n)=\frac1{2n}+O(n^{-2}). Here d=1, H=1 (the Hessian of \tfrac12(x-w)^2) and C=\mathrm{Var}(x)=\sigma^2, so \mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}C)=\sigma^2 and all three rows of the table are reproduced exactly. The Bayes predictive beats maximum likelihood when \sigma^2>1 (the data are noisier than the model believes, and averaging hedges) and loses when \sigma^2<1 (the model is too cautious already, and hedging makes it worse). At \sigma^2=1 they tie, as the well-specified row says.

sigma2 = 2.0          # variance of the data; the model assumes 1
tau2 = 100.0          # prior variance (wide, so the prior barely matters)
ns = np.array([5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500])
trials = 4000
S = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e * sigma2)
L_min = 0.5 * (sigma2 - 1 - np.log(sigma2))

def errors(xbar, n):
    v = 1.0 / (n + 1.0 / tau2)
    m = v * n * xbar
    # expected loss of a Gaussian predictor N(m, s2) on x ~ N(0, sigma2): 1/2 log(2 pi s2) + (sigma2 + m^2) / (2 s2)
    bayes = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * (1 + v)) + (sigma2 + m**2) / (2 * (1 + v)) - S - L_min
    gibbs = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) + 0.5 * (sigma2 + m**2 + v) - S - L_min
    mle = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) + 0.5 * (sigma2 + xbar**2) - S - L_min
    return bayes, gibbs, mle

means = []
for n in ns:
    xbar = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(sigma2 / n), size=trials)
    means.append([np.mean(g) for g in errors(xbar, n)])
means = np.array(means)
