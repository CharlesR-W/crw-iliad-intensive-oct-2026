SLT 4: Applications

Singular learning theory day, Iliad Intensive 2026

What are the quantities from NB1–NB3 (the free energy F_n=-\log Z_n, the learning coefficient \lambda, covariances under the tempered posterior) used for on real networks? This page is a reading list with a few lines of set-up per section, and two short identities in §4.2–4.3; there is no code and there are no exercises, and nothing in NB1–NB3 depends on it. Keystone: Baker et al. (§4.2), which defines the susceptibility as a posterior covariance; §4.3 applies the same identity to single training samples.

4.1 Stagewise development and phase transitions

As n grows, which region of parameter space does the posterior favour? And separately, what does the learning coefficient show when it is measured at checkpoints along a training run? For a region W_\alpha of parameter space, the local free energy at \beta=1 is F_n(W_\alpha)=-\log\int_{W_\alpha}\varphi(w)e^{-nL_n(w)}dw\approx nL_n(w_\alpha)+\lambda_\alpha\log n, where w_\alpha minimises the population loss L on W_\alpha and \lambda_\alpha is the volume exponent there. The posterior concentrates on the region with the smallest F_n(W_\alpha), so a region with lower loss but larger \lambda can take over as n grows (NB2 §3). “Phase transition” here means that the dominant region changes at some critical n; with d finite and no thermodynamic limit the switch is a smooth crossover, not a singularity of F_n. The local learning coefficient (LLC) \lambda(w^*) is the volume exponent of the basin around one parameter w^*, estimated with stochastic-gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD) as in NB3 §3. Measuring it along a training run treats each checkpoint w^* as the centre of its own local posterior.

Loss landscape degeneracy and stagewise development in transformers, Hoogland, Wang, Farrugia-Roberts, Carroll, Wei & Murfet (TMLR 2025) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●●○○

Estimates the LLC at checkpoints along training for a two-layer attention-only language transformer and an in-context linear-regression transformer, splits training into stages at the plateaus of the LLC curve (§4–5), and matches most stages to changes in internal structure or input/output behaviour (§6–7).

Dynamical versus Bayesian phase transitions in a toy model of superposition, Chen, Lau, Mendel, Wei & Murfet (2023) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●●○○

In the toy model of superposition with two hidden dimensions, derives the local learning coefficients of a family of k-gon solutions, checks by MCMC that the posterior moves between them as n grows as their local free energies predict (§4), and compares these transitions with the plateaus and drops of SGD training (§5).

Differentiation and specialization of attention heads via the refined local learning coefficient, Wang, Hoogland, van Wingerden, Furman & Murfet (ICLR 2025) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●●○○

Defines refined LLCs, the volume exponent with only one component’s weights varying or with the loss taken on another data distribution (§3.2), and uses them to follow how the attention heads of a two-layer attention-only transformer differentiate during training (§4).

4.2 Susceptibilities

How does a posterior expectation respond when the data distribution is nudged? Mix the true distribution q with another, q_h=(1-h)q+hq', so that the population loss becomes L_h=(1-h)L+hL', and take the tempered posterior p_{\beta,h}(w)\propto\varphi(w)e^{-n\beta L_h(w)}, with prior \varphi (in practice L_h is replaced by its empirical estimate). Pick an observable O(w), for example the loss restricted to one attention head, with posterior mean \langle O\rangle_{\beta,h}. The susceptibility is

\chi=\frac{1}{n\beta}\,\frac{\partial\langle O\rangle_{\beta,h}}{\partial h}\Big|_{h=0} =-\mathrm{Cov}_\beta\big(O,\,\Delta L\big),\qquad \Delta L=L'-L.

The second equality only needs differentiating under the integral. It is the fluctuation–response identity behind the magnetic susceptibility in statistical mechanics, and here it holds exactly at finite n and d, with no thermodynamic limit. In practice the prior is replaced by a Gaussian centred at the trained weights w^* (localisation, as for the LLC) and the covariance is estimated from SGLD samples.

Structural inference: interpreting small language models with susceptibilities, Baker, Wang, Hoogland, Pathak & Murfet (ICLR 2026; also arXiv:2504.18274) Importance ●●●●● Length ●●●○○

Defines the susceptibility of a component’s observable to a shift in the data distribution and estimates it with local SGLD (§2–3); the components-by-data-sources matrix of susceptibilities then picks out modules such as induction heads in a 3M-parameter transformer (§4).

Towards spectroscopy: susceptibility clusters in language models, Gordon, Baker, Wang, Snell, van Wingerden & Murfet (2026) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●●○○

Upweights a single token y in a context x, shows that the resulting susceptibilities \chi_{xy} decompose as a sum over modes of the data distribution (§2), and clusters them for Pythia-14M into 510 clusters, about half of which match sparse-autoencoder features (§4).

4.3 Bayesian influence functions and the loss kernel

Which training samples does a quantity depend on, and which samples does the model treat alike? The classical influence function needs the inverse Hessian at the trained weights, which does not exist when the Hessian is degenerate. Instead give each training loss \ell_i(w)=-\log p(z_i\mid w), with nL_n=\sum_i\ell_i, its own inverse temperature \beta_i in the posterior, p(w\mid D_n)\propto\varphi(w)\exp\!\big(-\sum_i\beta_i\ell_i(w)\big), so that all \beta_i=1 is Bayes. The identity of §4.2, with one sample as the perturbation, gives the Bayesian influence function (BIF) of sample z_i on an observable O(w):

\mathrm{BIF}(z_i,O)=\frac{\partial\langle O\rangle}{\partial\beta_i}\Big|_{\beta_i=1} =-\mathrm{Cov}\big(\ell_i,O\big).

The loss kernel is the covariance matrix \mathcal K(z_i,z_j)=\mathrm{Cov}(\ell_i,\ell_j)=-\mathrm{BIF}(z_i,\ell_j), which is positive semi-definite. Both papers use the localised posterior: the prior is replaced by a Gaussian of precision \gamma centred at w^*, and the covariances are estimated from SGLD samples.

Bayesian influence functions for Hessian-free data attribution, Kreer, Wu, Adam, Furman & Hoogland (ICLR 2026) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●●○○

Defines the BIF and its localised version, shows that under a Laplace approximation it reduces to the classical (damped) influence function (§2, App. A), and estimates it with SGLD for networks up to billions of parameters, testing it against retraining experiments (§3–4).

The loss kernel: a geometric probe for deep learning interpretability, Adam, Furman & Hoogland (2025) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●○○○

Defines the loss kernel on the localised tempered posterior (§2), checks on a synthetic multitask problem that it separates inputs by task (§3), and applies it to Inception-v1 on ImageNet, where its structure follows the WordNet hierarchy (§4).

4.4 MDL and compressibility

How many nats does it cost to describe a model good enough for the data? A two-part code sends a parameter and then the data encoded with it. Its redundancy R_n is the excess code length over the code built from the true distribution q. Let K(w)=D_{\rm KL}(q\,\|\,p(\cdot\mid w)), which is L(w)-L(w_0) when q is realisable, and let \lambda and m be the exponent and multiplicity of the volume of \{K<t\}, V(t)\sim c\,t^{\lambda}(-\log t)^{m-1} as t\to0. For a realisable model on a finite alphabet, with every probability bounded below and the analytic conditions of their App. E.1, Urdshals et al. construct a two-part code with

R_n=\lambda\log n-(m-1)\log\log n+O_p(1),

the same leading terms as the averaged free energy \bar F_n in NB2 §1. A realisable regular model has \lambda=d/2 and m=1, which gives the BIC term \tfrac d2\log n of NB1 §3.

Compressibility measures complexity: minimum description length meets singular learning theory, Urdshals, Lau, Hoogland, van Wingerden & Murfet (2025) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●●○○

Proves the two-part code with redundancy \lambda\log n-(m-1)\log\log n+O_p(1) (§3, App. E) and, on the Pythia suite, compares how far quantisation, factorisation and other compressions can go before the loss rises with LLC estimates, finding close and sometimes linear correlation (§4–5).

4.5 Programs and singularities

Can a discrete program be treated as a point in a continuous parameter space, so that the volume exponent says something about the program? These papers embed Turing machine codes in a smooth space of noisy codes w, with a negative log-likelihood L(w) that has the machines among its critical points. The learning coefficient \lambda is then the volume exponent of the basin around a machine, and the posterior weighs machines by loss and \lambda as in §4.1.

Geometry of program synthesis, Clift, Murfet & Wallbridge (2021) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●○○○

Proposes viewing programs as singularities of analytic varieties, or equivalently as phases of the Bayesian posterior of a program-synthesis problem, and reports small experiments on synthesising simple Turing machines (§6–7).

Programs as singularities, Murfet & Troiani (2025) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●●○○

Embeds Turing machines in a smooth family of noisy codes and shows that two machines computing the same function can sit at critical points with different local geometry, which the posterior distinguishes (§1.1 for the overview, §2 for the model and its learning coefficients).

Readings for the whole day

These go with NB1–NB3, as tagged. Notation as on the other pages: F_n=-\log Z_n is the free energy, L_n the empirical negative log-likelihood, w_0 an optimal parameter, \lambda the learning coefficient.

Keeping neural networks simple by minimizing the description length of the weights, Hinton & van Camp (COLT 1993) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●○○○

With NB1. Adds adaptive Gaussian noise to a network’s weights and minimises the expected squared error plus the information the noisy weights carry, an early variational form of the free energy’s trade-off between fit and the volume of good parameters.

Recent advances in algebraic geometry and Bayesian statistics, Watanabe (Information Geometry, 2024) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●●○○

With NB1–NB2. Watanabe’s review: the Bayesian framework (§2), the asymptotic free energy and the relation between generalisation loss, cross-validation and WAIC (§5), with the algebraic geometry behind them in §3–4.

Singular learning theory: exercises, Furman (LessWrong, 2024) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●●●○

With NB1–NB2. Exercises from reparametrised normal models and volume scaling through a two-layer linear network, the free energy of two competing phases and the bound \lambda\ge r/2 from the Fisher rank r, to the cumulants of nL_n under the tempered posterior; starred ones are the core.

Deep learning is singular, and that’s good, Wei, Murfet, Gong, Li, Gell-Redman & Quella (IEEE TNNLS 2023) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●●○○

With NB2. Argues for the learning coefficient as the complexity measure for deep networks, comparing it as a volume dimension with effective degrees of freedom and flatness (§4) and relating it to generalisation error in experiments such as reduced-rank regression (§5).

A widely applicable Bayesian information criterion, Watanabe (JMLR 2013) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●●○○

With NB3. Defines WBIC, the posterior mean of nL_n at inverse temperature \beta=1/\log n, and proves, under conditions that cover singular and some unrealisable models, that it shares the leading terms nL_n(w_0)+\lambda\log n of the free energy F_n (Theorem 4).

For reference (books): Algebraic Geometry and Statistical Learning Theory, Watanabe (Cambridge University Press, 2009); Mathematical Theory of Bayesian Statistics, Watanabe (CRC Press, 2018).
