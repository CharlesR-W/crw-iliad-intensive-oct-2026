DA1 built one formula, the damped Gauss–Newton influence function, and asked which question it answers. This page tours the methods that answer the question differently, or answer it at scale. Read the capsule at the top of each stop, in order (about 30 minutes for all six). Then go deep where you like. Each stop names one or two papers and the sections worth reading, and most offer a walkthrough: our own annotated derivation, an alternative to working through the paper’s.

Conventions (as in DA1 §2):

  • The training objective is L(w;\beta)=\sum_i\beta_iL_i(w), with L_i the loss on example z_i; \beta=\mathbf 1 is the actual run.
  • A method says what training produces as a function of the data weights, \beta\mapsto\pi(\beta) (a stationary point, the last iterate, a posterior). The influence of z_i on a scalar observable \phi is \mathcal I(z_i,\phi)=\partial\phi(\pi(\beta))/\partial\beta_i at \beta=\mathbf 1.
  • Deleting z_i is \beta_i:1\to0, so to first order \Delta\phi\approx-\mathcal I.
  • An upweighting that lowers a query loss is helpful and has negative influence on that loss.
  • SGD written on a mean loss shows its 1/n (full batch) or 1/b (minibatch) explicitly.
  • The LDS (DA1 §5) is the rank correlation, over random training subsets, between predicted and actual \phi.
  • H and G are the Hessian and Gauss–Newton matrix of the summed loss. The damped IF is \mathcal I_\lambda(z_i,\phi)=-\nabla\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}\nabla L_i.

One lens. In their local or frozen-curvature approximations, several of these methods share a structure: a curvature matrix, a regularization of its small eigenvalues, a training gradient and a query gradient. But they answer different questions, and the damping-like quantity means something different in each. DA1 §6 tabulates the spectral filters with their assumptions. The exact BIF, general unrolling and datamodels have no such form.

The tour, with what each method takes \pi(\beta) to be.

  1. Influence functions at scale: EK-FAC makes the damped Gauss–Newton IF computable for language models. \pi: a stationary point.
  2. Unrolling: differentiate the actual training run, including its order and length. \pi: the last iterate.
  3. Bayesian influence functions: a covariance under a local posterior, with no Hessian inverse. \pi: a local posterior.
  4. Influence changes over training: attribution is a time series, and can change sign. \pi: the iterate at each time t.
  5. Datamodels and TRAK: fit the map from training subsets to outputs, or a linearized estimate of it. \pi: retraining from scratch on a subset.
  6. Open problems and uses.

1. Influence functions at scale

Question. How do we compute the damped Gauss–Newton IF, \mathcal I_\lambda(z_i,\phi)=-\nabla\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}\nabla L_i, when w has billions of entries? Here G is the Gauss–Newton matrix of the summed training loss, and \pi(\beta) is still the stationary point of DA1 §4’s proximal objective (the PBRF, proximal Bregman response function). The target is unchanged; only the estimator is new.

How. Never form G or its inverse. Keep one block of G per layer, and approximate each block by a Kronecker product S\otimes A of two small matrices: the second moments of the gradients at the layer’s outputs and of its inputs. A Kronecker product is inverted through the eigendecompositions of its two factors. EK-FAC keeps that eigenbasis but refits the eigenvalues to the data. Then each query costs one solve v=(G+\lambda I)^{-1}\nabla\phi, and each training example one gradient and one dot product, -v^\top\nabla L_i.

What it bought. Grosse et al. computed IFs for language models up to 52B parameters. For a model’s answer about being shut down, the most influential training sequences of the largest model are about self-preservation, related to the query at a high level of abstraction rather than by shared words. The distribution of influence is heavy-tailed, yet spread over many sequences. Caveat: each approximation (dropping cross-layer blocks, the Kronecker factorization, the eigenvalue refit, the choice of \lambda) changes the answer, and its agreement with the exact damped Gauss–Newton IF has to be measured. Code 1 measures it on a toy network.

Walkthrough 1: EK-FAC for one linear layer

Substitutes for Grosse et al. §2.2.2–2.2.3 and §3.1–3.2 (the method), not their findings (§5). Shared objective: say which matrix EK-FAC approximates, which independence assumption it makes, and how a damped inverse is applied without ever being formed.

Notation. One linear layer s=Wa inside a network: input a\in\mathbb R^d (append a 1 for a bias), weights W\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}, output (pre-activation) s\in\mathbb R^m. On input x with label y the loss is \ell(f(x),y), and g=\partial\ell/\partial s\in\mathbb R^m is the gradient at the layer’s output. The per-example weight gradient is \nabla_W\ell=g\,a^\top. Flatten matrices row by row, so \mathrm{vec}(g\,a^\top)=g\otimes a. Write \mathbb E_x for the average over the n training inputs. The Gauss–Newton matrix of the summed loss is G=n\bar G with \bar G an average; damping \lambda on G is damping \lambda/n on \bar G. We work with \bar G.

Step 1. The curvature target. IFs at scale use G rather than H (DA1 §4), and G must be built from gradients, the one thing backprop gives cheaply. The right gradients use labels sampled from the model, not the observed labels.

Exercise. For a classifier with logits z=f(x), probabilities p=\mathrm{softmax}(z) and \ell=-\log p_y:

  1. Show that for a label \hat y drawn from p, \mathbb E_{\hat y\sim p}\big[\nabla_z\ell(z,\hat y)\nabla_z\ell(z,\hat y)^\top\big]=\nabla_z^2\ell=\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^\top.
  2. Conclude that the layer’s block of \bar G is \bar G_{WW}=\mathbb E_x\mathbb E_{\hat y}\big[(g_{\hat y}g_{\hat y}^\top)\otimes(aa^\top)\big], where g_{\hat y} is the output gradient computed with label \hat y.
  3. What matrix do the observed labels give instead, and how does it compare with \bar G near the end of training?
Solution

1. \ell(z,\hat y)=-z_{\hat y}+\log\sum_ce^{z_c}, so \nabla_z\ell=p-e_{\hat y}, and \nabla^2_z\ell=\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^\top, independent of the label. Averaging over \hat y\sim p,

\mathbb E\big[(p-e_{\hat y})(p-e_{\hat y})^\top\big]=\sum_cp_c\,(e_c-p)(e_c-p)^\top =\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^\top ,

using \sum_cp_c=1 to expand.

2. With J=\partial z/\partial w, the Gauss–Newton contribution of one input is J^\top\nabla_z^2\ell\,J=\mathbb E_{\hat y}[J^\top\nabla_z\ell\,\nabla_z\ell^\top J] =\mathbb E_{\hat y}[\nabla_w\ell(\hat y)\nabla_w\ell(\hat y)^\top]. Restricted to the layer, \nabla_W\ell=g_{\hat y}a^\top, i.e. g_{\hat y}\otimes a after flattening, and the mixed-product rule gives (g\otimes a)(g\otimes a)^\top=(gg^\top)\otimes(aa^\top). Average over x. The input a does not depend on \hat y, so only g is averaged over labels.

3. The observed labels give the empirical Fisher F_{\rm emp}=\mathbb E_x[\nabla\ell(y)\nabla\ell(y)^\top]. Near interpolation p\approx e_y, so p-e_y=O(\varepsilon) with \varepsilon=1-p_y: F_{\rm emp}=O(\varepsilon^2) while \bar G=O(\varepsilon). The two shrink at different rates, and their spectra differ (TD §2.6 has the details). Grosse et al. and Martens and Grosse sample \hat y; one sample per input gives an unbiased estimate of \bar G.

Step 2. The Kronecker factorization. The block \bar G_{WW} is md\times md, and for a 4096\times4096 layer md\approx1.7\times10^7. K-FAC replaces the average of a product by the product of averages, which needs only an m\times m and a d\times d matrix. That is exact only if input and output gradient are uncorrelated in a specific sense.

Exercise. Let S=\mathbb E_x\mathbb E_{\hat y}[gg^\top] and A=\mathbb E_x[aa^\top]. K-FAC sets \bar G_{WW}\approx S\otimes A.

  1. Show this is exact when \mathbb E[g_ig_j\,a_ka_l]=\mathbb E[g_ig_j]\,\mathbb E[a_ka_l] for all indices, for example when g and a are independent.
  2. Compare the storage of \bar G_{WW} and of (S,A).
Solution

1. Entry ((i,k),(j,l)) of \bar G_{WW} is \mathbb E[g_ig_j\,a_ka_l]; the same entry of S\otimes A is S_{ij}A_{kl}=\mathbb E[g_ig_j]\,\mathbb E[a_ka_l]. They agree for all indices exactly when the fourth moments factorize. In a real network they do not in general: g depends on the input through the network’s prediction, and so does a. The code at the end of this stop measures the error.

2. \bar G_{WW} has md(md+1)/2 independent entries; S and A have m(m+1)/2+d(d+1)/2. For m=d=4096: about 1.4\times10^{14} against 1.7\times10^7.

Step 3. A damped inverse without forming it. We need v=(\bar G_{WW}+\bar\lambda I)^{-1}u for a query gradient u, once per query. In this step \bar\lambda is the damping on \bar G, so \lambda=n\bar\lambda on G. The eigendecomposition of S\otimes A comes from those of S and A, so the inverse costs two small eigendecompositions and four matrix products. The damping goes on the joint eigenvalues.

Exercise. Let S=Q_SD_SQ_S^\top and A=Q_AD_AQ_A^\top, with D_S=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma) and D_A=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha) holding the eigenvalues.

  1. Show S\otimes A=(Q_S\otimes Q_A)(D_S\otimes D_A)(Q_S\otimes Q_A)^\top, so its eigenvalues are the products \sigma_i\alpha_j.
  2. For u=\mathrm{vec}(U) with U\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}, show (S\otimes A+\bar\lambda I)^{-1}u=\mathrm{vec}\Big(Q_S\big[(Q_S^\top UQ_A)\oslash(\sigma\alpha^\top+\bar\lambda)\big]Q_A^\top\Big), where \oslash divides entrywise and (\sigma\alpha^\top+\bar\lambda)_{ij}=\sigma_i\alpha_j+\bar\lambda. Count the cost.
  3. K-FAC optimizers often damp each factor instead, using (S+\sqrt{\bar\lambda} I)\otimes(A+\sqrt{\bar\lambda} I). Why is that a different operator, and which directions does it damp more?
Solution

1. The mixed-product rule (B\otimes C)(D\otimes E)=BD\otimes CE gives (Q_S\otimes Q_A)(D_S\otimes D_A)(Q_S\otimes Q_A)^\top=(Q_SD_SQ_S^\top)\otimes(Q_AD_AQ_A^\top). Q_S\otimes Q_A is orthogonal, and D_S\otimes D_A is diagonal with entries \sigma_i\alpha_j.

2. With row-major flattening, (B\otimes C)\,\mathrm{vec}(U)=\mathrm{vec}(BUC^\top) (check it on U=ga^\top: (Bg)\otimes(Ca)=\mathrm{vec}(Bg\,a^\top C^\top)). So (Q_S\otimes Q_A)^\top u=\mathrm{vec}(Q_S^\top UQ_A): the coordinates of u in the eigenbasis. Dividing coordinate (i,j) by \sigma_i\alpha_j+\bar\lambda applies the inverse of the damped diagonal, and Q_S\otimes Q_A maps back. Cost: O(m^3+d^3) once per layer for the eigendecompositions, then O(md(m+d)) per vector, against O(m^3d^3) for a dense solve.

3. Its eigenvalues are (\sigma_i+\sqrt{\bar\lambda})(\alpha_j+\sqrt{\bar\lambda})=\sigma_i\alpha_j+\bar\lambda+\sqrt{\bar\lambda}(\sigma_i+\alpha_j). The extra \sqrt{\bar\lambda}(\sigma_i+\alpha_j) damps more wherever either factor is large, including directions with a small product. It is not \bar G+\bar\lambda I for any \bar G of the form S\otimes A, so the IF it gives is not the damped Gauss–Newton IF. Grosse et al. add the damping to the joint eigenvalues (their Eq. 21).

Step 4. The eigenvalue refit (EK-FAC). The Kronecker eigenbasis can be good even when the Kronecker eigenvalues \sigma_i\alpha_j are not. EK-FAC keeps the basis Q=Q_S\otimes Q_A and refits the eigenvalues as the second moments of the actual gradients in that basis. That is the best diagonal in the basis, and it removes the independence assumption along the diagonal.

Exercise. Here g is computed with \hat y\sim p, as in Step 1.

  1. Show that the diagonal \Lambda minimizing \|\bar G_{WW}-Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\Lambda)\,Q^\top\|_F is \Lambda_{ij}=\mathbb E\big[(Q_S^\top g)_i^2\,(Q_A^\top a)_j^2\big]. Deduce that EK-FAC is never further from \bar G_{WW} in Frobenius norm than K-FAC.
  2. Show that if g and a are independent, \Lambda_{ij}=\sigma_i\alpha_j: EK-FAC reduces to K-FAC.
  3. Write the EK-FAC damped IF for one layer, in summed-loss units.
Solution

1. The Frobenius norm is invariant under orthogonal conjugation: \|\bar G-Q\Lambda Q^\top\|_F=\|Q^\top\bar GQ-\Lambda\|_F. Over diagonal \Lambda this is minimized by copying the diagonal of Q^\top\bar GQ; the off-diagonal part is the irreducible error. The basis vector for index (i,j) is q_{ij}=(Q_S)_{:i}\otimes(Q_A)_{:j}, and q_{ij}^\top(g\otimes a)=(Q_S^\top g)_i\,(Q_A^\top a)_j, so \Lambda_{ij}=q_{ij}^\top\bar Gq_{ij}=\mathbb E[(Q_S^\top g)_i^2(Q_A^\top a)_j^2]. K-FAC is another diagonal in the same basis, so its error is at least as large.

2. If g and a are independent the expectation factorizes: \mathbb E[(Q_S^\top g)_i^2]\,\mathbb E[(Q_A^\top a)_j^2]=(Q_S^\top SQ_S)_{ii}(Q_A^\top AQ_A)_{jj}=\sigma_i\alpha_j.

3. For the summed loss the curvature is n\bar G, with eigenvalues n\Lambda_{ij} in the EK-FAC basis. With U_\phi and U_i the layer blocks of \nabla\phi and \nabla L_i (as m\times d matrices),

\mathcal I_\lambda(z_i,\phi)\approx-\Big\langle Q_S\big[(Q_S^\top U_\phi Q_A)\oslash(n\Lambda+\lambda)\big]Q_A^\top,\;U_i\Big\rangle ,

summed over layers (the cross-layer blocks of G are dropped). The bracket is computed once per query; each training example then costs one gradient and one inner product.

From one layer to a language model. Grosse et al. apply this to the MLP layers only. Each training sequence contributes one pseudo-gradient per token position: they fit S and A as if tokens were independent, then refit \Lambda on the gradients summed over the sequence (§3.1). Two engineering steps make the search feasible. TF-IDF filtering keeps about 10^4 candidate sequences per query, which they note biases the results. Query batching stores each query’s preconditioned gradient Q_S[\cdots]Q_A^\top as a rank-32 matrix per layer, so hundreds of queries share one pass over the training gradients (§3.2).

Scope. We derived the one-layer algebra of K-FAC and EK-FAC and the optimality of the refit. Not derived: dropping the cross-layer blocks, the token-independence fit for sequences, and the size of any approximation error, which is empirical.

Code 1: what each approximation costs

A two-layer tanh network (147 parameters) is trained briefly on three Gaussian classes (n=240; 200 Adam steps, training loss about 0.05). We build four curvature matrices: the exact Gauss–Newton \bar G (averaged over model-sampled labels exactly, not by sampling), its block-diagonal part, K-FAC and EK-FAC. We then compute damped IFs of every training example on the test losses of 12 held-out points, with the same \lambda for all four. The block-diagonal column isolates block omission; K-FAC and EK-FAC against block-diagonal isolate the Kronecker error. Look for:

  • EK-FAC’s Frobenius error is below K-FAC’s in every block, as Step 4 guarantees;
  • the Kronecker step costs far more than dropping the cross-layer blocks: rank correlation with the exact IF is about 0.65–0.75 for block-diagonal and 0.3–0.45 for K-FAC and EK-FAC;
  • EK-FAC’s better curvature fit raises the Pearson correlation a little and leaves the rank correlation flat;
  • larger damping raises the Pearson correlations, because it hides the small-eigenvalue directions where the approximations differ most.

    a1 = torch.cat([X, torch.ones(len(X), 1)], 1)
    hid = torch.tanh(a1 @ W1.T)
    a2 = torch.cat([hid, torch.ones(len(X), 1)], 1)
    return a2 @ W2.T, a1, a2, hid

    opt.step()

G = torch.einsum("ncp,ncd,ndq->pq", J, Hz, J) / n
blocks = [slice(0, sizes[0]), slice(sizes[0], P)]

W2 = th[sizes[0]:].reshape(shapes[1])[:, :h]
Sg2 = Hz
Sg1 = torch.einsum("nh,ch,ncd,dk,nk->nhk", 1 - hid**2, W2, Hz, W2, 1 - hid**2)

    G_bd[blk, blk] = G[blk, blk]
    S, A = Sg.mean(0), a.T @ a / n

2. Unrolling: differentiate the actual training run

Question. If example z_i had been weighted slightly more in this training run (same initialization, same data order, same optimizer), how would \phi(w_T) change? Here \pi(\beta)=w_T(\beta), the last iterate as a function of the data weights. Order, optimizer and training length become part of the answer: an example seen in the first minibatch and one seen in the last can have very different effects, which no endpoint formula can distinguish.

How. Training is a composition of steps, so its derivative is a product of step Jacobians, exactly as backprop differentiates a composition of layers (Walkthrough 2A). Each step that uses z_i injects its gradient, and the later steps propagate it. TracIn keeps only the injections, each paired with the query gradient at the same step: it allocates the decrease of the test loss along the trajectory to the examples that were used, a different estimand from the endpoint sensitivity. SOURCE freezes the curvature within a few training segments, which turns unrolling into an IF with a filter F(s) in place of 1/s on each curvature eigenvalue s (Walkthrough 2B; DA1 §6’s table). MAGIC computes the exact derivative by reverse mode through training.

Main result and caveat. For frozen positive-definite curvature, long training makes the unrolled response forget its history and converge to the IF (Walkthrough 2C). An exact derivative is exact only for infinitesimal reweighting of this run: MAGIC’s near-perfect LDS holds when about 1% of the data is dropped with the randomness fixed, and degrades for larger deletions. Even in the ridge regression of the code below, deleting one example moves \phi 30% more than the first-order prediction.

Walkthrough 2A: the unrolling formula

Substitutes for SOURCE §3.1 and the set-up of MAGIC §2. Shared objective: derive the influence of an example on the last iterate as a sum over the steps that used it, and know what changes for adaptive optimizers.

Unrolling is to a training run what backprop is to a forward pass:

Forward pass Training run
inputs weights w data weights \beta
intermediate states activations a_1,\dots,a_L iterates w_1,\dots,w_T
output loss of the logits observable \phi(w_T)
differentiation backprop unrolling

Notation. Minibatch SGD on the mean minibatch loss, with weights \beta: w_{t+1}=w_t-\frac{\eta_t}{b}\sum_{j\in B_t}\beta_j\nabla L_j(w_t), with fixed w_0 and a fixed batch sequence B_0,\dots,B_{T-1} of size b. Let \hat H_t=\frac1b\sum_{j\in B_t}\nabla^2L_j(w_t) be the minibatch Hessian, J_t=I-\eta_t\hat H_t the step Jacobian, and J_{(t+1):T}=J_{T-1}\cdots J_{t+1} the ordered product (I when t=T-1). All derivatives are at \beta=\mathbf 1.

Step A1. One step. \beta_i enters a step twice: directly, through its weight in the sum, and indirectly, through w_t, which already depends on \beta_i.

Exercise. Show that \dfrac{\partial w_{t+1}}{\partial\beta_i}=J_t\dfrac{\partial w_t}{\partial\beta_i}-\dfrac{\eta_t}{b}\,\mathbf 1[i\in B_t]\,\nabla L_i(w_t), with \partial w_0/\partial\beta_i=0.

Solution

Differentiate the update with the chain rule. The sum depends on \beta_i through the coefficient \beta_i (only if i\in B_t) and through w_t in every term:

\frac{\partial w_{t+1}}{\partial\beta_i}=\frac{\partial w_t}{\partial\beta_i} -\frac{\eta_t}{b}\sum_{j\in B_t}\nabla^2L_j(w_t)\,\frac{\partial w_t}{\partial\beta_i} -\frac{\eta_t}{b}\,\mathbf 1[i\in B_t]\,\nabla L_i(w_t),

using \beta_j=1. The first two terms combine to (I-\eta_t\hat H_t)\,\partial w_t/\partial\beta_i=J_t\,\partial w_t/\partial\beta_i. The initial point does not depend on the data weights.

Step A2. Unroll. Solving the recursion is substituting it into itself, as backprop does layer by layer.

Exercise. Show \frac{\partial w_T}{\partial\beta_i}=-\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\eta_t}{b}\,\mathbf 1[i\in B_t]\,J_{(t+1):T}\,\nabla L_i(w_t),\qquad \mathcal I(z_i,\phi)=\nabla\phi(w_T)^\top\frac{\partial w_T}{\partial\beta_i}. What do you get if you set every J=I and replace \nabla\phi(w_T) by \nabla\phi(w_t)?

Solution

Write r_t=\partial w_t/\partial\beta_i and c_t=-\frac{\eta_t}{b}\mathbf 1[i\in B_t]\nabla L_i(w_t), so r_{t+1}=J_tr_t+c_t and r_0=0. Then r_1=c_0, r_2=J_1c_0+c_1, and by induction r_T=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}J_{T-1}\cdots J_{t+1}\,c_t=\sum_tJ_{(t+1):T}c_t. The chain rule gives \mathcal I=\nabla\phi(w_T)^\top r_T. Each term is one appearance of z_i: its gradient at that step, carried forward by all later steps.

With every J=I and the query gradient taken at the same step, -\frac1b\sum_{t:\,i\in B_t}\eta_t\nabla\phi(w_t)^\top\nabla L_i(w_t). For \phi a test loss this is minus TracIn’s minibatch score (TracIn counts a loss decrease as positive). To first order in \eta_t, the step t changes \phi by -\eta_t\nabla\phi(w_t)^\top\hat g_t, and the part of \hat g_t due to z_i is \nabla L_i(w_t)/b. So TracIn allocates the decrease of \phi along the trajectory to the examples used at each step. It does not ask how the endpoint would move under reweighting, which is what the Jacobians propagate.

Step A3. Adaptive optimizers. Adam and natural-gradient methods multiply the gradient by a matrix that itself depends on the trajectory. Treating that matrix as fixed is the frozen-preconditioner approximation.

Exercise. Consider w_{t+1}=w_t-\frac{\eta_t}{b}P(w_t)\sum_{j\in B_t}\beta_j\nabla L_j(w_t), with P(w)\succ0 depending smoothly on w, and write \hat g_t=\frac1b\sum_{j\in B_t}\nabla L_j(w_t).

  1. Show that the step Jacobian acts as v\mapsto v-\eta_t\big[P(w_t)\hat H_tv+\big(DP(w_t)[v]\big)\hat g_t\big].
  2. In the frozen-preconditioner approximation one drops the DP term and treats P_t=P(w_t) as fixed. Show that the unrolled formula becomes -\sum_t\frac{\eta_t}{b}\mathbf 1[i\in B_t]\,J^{(P)}_{(t+1):T}P_t\nabla L_i(w_t) with J^{(P)}_t=I-\eta_tP_t\hat H_t.
  3. Adam’s preconditioner is built from a running average v_t of squared minibatch gradients, which depends on \beta too. How do you differentiate it exactly?
Solution

1. The step is w\mapsto w-\eta_tP(w)\hat g(w). Its derivative in direction v is v-\eta_t\big[DP(w)[v]\,\hat g(w)+P(w)\,D\hat g(w)[v]\big], and D\hat g(w)[v]=\hat H_tv. The DP term vanishes where \hat g_t=0, which minibatch gradients almost never are.

2. With P_t fixed, Step A1 goes through with \hat H_t\to P_t\hat H_t in the Jacobian and \nabla L_i\to P_t\nabla L_i in the direct term; unrolling as in Step A2 gives the formula.

3. Make the whole optimizer state the state: s_t=(w_t,m_t,v_t) with update s_{t+1}=h_t(s_t;\beta). Then \partial\phi(s_T)/\partial\beta=\sum_t A_{t+1}\,\partial h_t/\partial\beta, with A_T=\partial\phi/\partial s_T and A_t=A_{t+1}\,\partial h_t/\partial s_t: backprop through the training loop, over the full state. This captures that z_i’s gradient enters v_t and so rescales every later step for all data. MAGIC computes exactly this, replaying training from saved checkpoints to regenerate the states.

Scope. Steps A1–A2 are exact for SGD with the randomness held fixed; for any differentiable update the same chain rule holds over the full optimizer state (Step A3). What is expensive is the Jacobian product: SOURCE approximates it, MAGIC computes it.

Walkthrough 2B: the SOURCE filter

Substitutes for SOURCE §§3.2–3.3. Shared objective: see how freezing the curvature within a training segment turns the unrolled sum into an IF with a filter F(s) in place of 1/s, and what the crossover at s\sim1/(\eta K) does and does not mean.

Notation. One segment of K steps of minibatch SGD on the mean minibatch loss, w_{t+1}=w_t-\frac{\eta_t}{b}\sum_{j\in B_t}\beta_j\nabla L_j(w_t) (n examples, batch size b, batches drawn uniformly, so \Pr[i\in B_t]=b/n). SOURCE’s stationarity approximation: within the segment the minibatch Hessian, the gradient of z_i and the step size are constant, \hat H_t\approx\bar H, \nabla L_i(w_t)\approx\bar g_i, \eta_t\approx\bar\eta, and the indicators \mathbf 1[i\in B_t] are replaced by their mean b/n. \bar H is a mean-loss Hessian, so its eigenvalues s do not grow with n.

Step B1. The segment response. With everything frozen, the sum over steps is a matrix geometric series.

Exercise. Show that the segment’s contribution to \partial w/\partial\beta_i at its end is -\frac{\bar\eta}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}(I-\bar\eta\bar H)^k\,\bar g_i =-\frac1n\big[I-(I-\bar\eta\bar H)^K\big]\bar H^{-1}\bar g_i \approx-\frac1n\big(I-e^{-\bar\eta K\bar H}\big)\bar H^{-1}\bar g_i , so in an eigenbasis of \bar H, with eigenvalue s, it acts on \bar g_i as -\frac1nF(s) with F(s)=\big(1-e^{-\bar\eta Ks}\big)/s. When is the last step accurate? (In summed-loss units, eigenvalue ns and step \bar\eta/n, the same response is -F with no 1/n: DA1 §6’s SOURCE row.)

Solution

Within the segment, the step t term of Walkthrough 2A carries \frac{\eta_t}{b}\mathbf 1[i\in B_t]\to\frac{\bar\eta}{b}\cdot\frac bn=\frac{\bar\eta}n, and is propagated to the segment’s end by K-1-k frozen Jacobians (I-\bar\eta\bar H). Relabelling, the sum is -\frac{\bar\eta}n\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}M^k\bar g_i with M=I-\bar\eta\bar H. The geometric series \sum_{k<K}M^k=(I-M^K)(I-M)^{-1} and I-M=\bar\eta\bar H give the middle form. In the eigenbasis, (1-\bar\eta s)^K=e^{K\log(1-\bar\eta s)}\approx e^{-\bar\eta Ks}, accurate when \bar\eta s\ll1. The exponent is off by about K\bar\eta^2s^2/2, which matters only where e^{-\bar\eta Ks} is not already negligible.

Step B2. Reading the filter. F plays the role of 1/s in the IF. Its behaviour for small, large and negative s is what “unrolling supplies its own damping” means, and where that phrase is loose.

Exercise.

  1. Show F(s)\to1/s for s\gg1/(\bar\eta K) and F(0)=\bar\eta K.
  2. Compare with the damped IF filter 1/(s+\lambda) at \lambda=1/(\bar\eta K): both are \approx\bar\eta K at s=0 and \approx1/s for large s. Evaluate both at the crossover s=1/(\bar\eta K). How do they approach 1/s?
  3. What does F do for a negative eigenvalue?
Solution

1. For \bar\eta Ks\gg1, e^{-\bar\eta Ks}\to0 and F\to1/s. For s\to0, 1-e^{-\bar\eta Ks}=\bar\eta Ks+O(s^2), so F(0)=\bar\eta K: a direction with no curvature moves linearly for K steps, by \bar\eta K per unit force, and no further.

2. At s=1/(\bar\eta K): F=(1-e^{-1})\,\bar\eta K\approx0.632\,\bar\eta K, while 1/(s+\lambda)=\bar\eta K/2. For large s, F=1/s-e^{-\bar\eta Ks}/s approaches 1/s exponentially fast, while 1/(s+\lambda)=1/s-\lambda/s^2+\dots approaches it only algebraically. So 1/(\bar\eta K) sets the crossover scale, as damping does, but F is not a ridge penalty.

3. For s<0, F(s)=\big(e^{\bar\eta K|s|}-1\big)/|s|>0, growing exponentially in K: along negative curvature (a saddle direction) a push grows exponentially over the segment. It stays finite for every s, unlike 1/(s+\lambda) at s=-\lambda.

Step B3. Several segments. SOURCE splits training into L segments (it uses L=3), each with its own \bar H_\ell, \bar g_{i,\ell}, \bar\eta_\ell and K_\ell. A segment’s response is carried through the later segments by their frozen Jacobians \bar S_\ell=e^{-\bar\eta_\ell K_\ell\bar H_\ell}: \frac{\partial w_T}{\partial\beta_i}\approx-\frac1n\sum_{\ell=1}^L\Big(\prod_{\ell'=\ell+1}^{L}\bar S_{\ell'}\Big)\big(I-e^{-\bar\eta_\ell K_\ell\bar H_\ell}\big)\bar H_\ell^{-1}\bar g_{i,\ell}, with later segments on the left. Once the \bar H_\ell differ, this is not one filter applied to one gradient.

Scope. We derived the one-segment filter under stationarity, the exponential approximation and averaged batch membership. These hide changing gradients, ordered non-commuting Jacobians across segments, and any correlation between when z_i appears and the curvature at that time.

Walkthrough 2C: from unrolling to the influence function

Covers the deterministic mechanism behind Mlodozeniec et al. §4.1 (Theorem 2); the stochastic-approximation proof is not reproduced. Shared objective: prove that for frozen positive-definite curvature and full-batch GD the unrolled response forgets its history and converges to the IF, and see exactly what goes wrong along flat directions.

Notation. Full-batch GD on the mean loss, with z_k downweighted: w_{t+1}=w_t-\frac\eta n\sum_j\beta_j\nabla L_j(w_t),\qquad \beta=\mathbf 1-\varepsilon e_k . So \beta_k=1-\varepsilon, and deleting z_k is \varepsilon=1. The response is r_t=\partial w_t/\partial\varepsilon at \varepsilon=0, which is -\partial w_t/\partial\beta_k. \bar H_t=\frac1n\sum_j\nabla^2L_j(w_t) is the mean-loss Hessian; the summed-loss Hessian is H=n\bar H.

Step C1. The response recursion. This is Walkthrough 2A with b=n and the sign of the perturbation flipped.

Exercise. Show r_{t+1}=(I-\eta\bar H_t)\,r_t+\frac\eta n\nabla L_k(w_t), with r_0=0.

Solution

By Step A1 with b=n and B_t=\{1,\dots,n\}, \partial w_{t+1}/\partial\beta_k=(I-\eta\bar H_t)\,\partial w_t/\partial\beta_k-\frac\eta n\nabla L_k(w_t). Since \beta_k=1-\varepsilon, r_t=-\partial w_t/\partial\beta_k; multiply by -1. Downweighting pushes w against the descent direction of L_k, hence the plus sign.

Step C2. A converged window. Suppose training has reached a stationary point w^* before the last K steps, so that on T-K\le t<T we have w_t=w^*, \bar H_t=\bar H\succ0 and \nabla L_k(w_t)=g_k. The response entering the window, r_{T-K}, is whatever the earlier history produced.

Exercise. Show r_T=(I-\eta\bar H)^K\,r_{T-K}+\frac1n\big[I-(I-\eta\bar H)^K\big]\bar H^{-1}g_k . For 0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}(\bar H), take K\to\infty. What does the limit not depend on, and how does it compare with the IF?

Solution

Iterating the frozen recursion K times, r_T=M^Kr_{T-K}+\frac\eta n\sum_{s=0}^{K-1}M^sg_k with M=I-\eta\bar H, and \sum_{s<K}M^s=(I-M^K)(\eta\bar H)^{-1} gives the formula. If 0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}, every eigenvalue 1-\eta s of M has modulus below 1 (here s>0), so M^K\to0 and r_\infty=\frac1n\bar H^{-1}g_k=H^{-1}g_k . The limit does not depend on r_{T-K}: the inherited response decays, and the run forgets how it got to w^*. H^{-1}g_k is minus the upweighting IF -H^{-1}g_k (DA1 §3), as it should be for a downweighting, and deletion (\varepsilon=1) moves w by H^{-1}g_k to first order. Dropping r_{T-K} from the formula is only correct once (I-\eta\bar H)^K is negligible.

Step C3. Flat directions. Now let \bar H\succeq0 have a null space N, as at a degenerate minimum, with orthogonal projector P_0 onto N.

Exercise. With the same frozen window, show that P_0r_T=P_0r_{T-K}+\frac{K\eta}nP_0g_k, while the component in the range of \bar H still converges to \frac1n\bar H^+g_k. When does the response converge? Check that in linear regression it always does.

Solution

P_0 commutes with \bar H and P_0\bar H=0, so P_0r_{t+1}=P_0r_t+\frac\eta nP_0g_k; summing K steps gives the linear growth. On the range of \bar H the argument of Step C2 applies with \bar H^+. So r_t converges if and only if P_0g_k=0, that is g_k\in\mathrm{Range}(\bar H): the example must not push along a direction the total loss is flat in. In linear regression g_k=-x_k(y_k-x_k^\top w^*) lies in the span of the inputs, which is \mathrm{Range}(X^\top X), so there is no drift.

Scope. Proved: full-batch GD with frozen, positive-definite curvature, including the inherited response, and the drift along flat directions. For SGD, Theorem 2 of Mlodozeniec et al. (§4.1) gives the same limit H^+g_k plus a null-space part that need not converge, under regularity, bounded (w_t,r_t), g_k\in\mathrm{Range}(H), eigen-gap and uniform-convergence assumptions (A1–A6). Convergence of w_t alone is not enough: Step C3 with \eta_t in place of \eta drifts without bound, since \sum_t\eta_t=\infty.

Ridge regression (n=60, d=8, input scales spread over a factor 30, so the mean-loss Hessian’s eigenvalues span 0.002 to 0.7), trained by full-batch GD from w_0=0 with \eta=1/\lambda_{\max}. Observable: a query loss. We downweight one high-residual example and compare, as a function of the number of steps K:

  • the frozen-curvature filter: the parameter response \frac1n[I-(I-\eta\bar H)^K]\bar H^{-1}g_k with g_k=\nabla L_k(w^*), read with the current query gradient \nabla\phi(w_K);
  • the IF, \frac1n\bar H^{-1}g_k, read with \nabla\phi(w^*).

The Hessian of ridge regression is constant, so the frozen filter is wrong early only because the training gradient \nabla L_k(w_t) is not yet g_k. Look for:

  • the response building up over about 1/(\eta s) steps for each eigenvalue s (the slowest takes about 344 steps);
  • the inherited term of Step C2: dropping it from a 200-step window gets about half of r_T wrong;
  • deleting the example (printed) moves \phi about 30% more than the IF predicts: first order is not deletion, even here.

t0 = time.time()
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, d, mu_ridge = 60, 8, 1e-3
X = rng.standard_normal((n, d)) * np.geomspace(1, 0.03, d)
w_true = rng.standard_normal(d)
y = X @ w_true + 0.3 * rng.standard_normal(n)
Hbar = X.T @ X / n + mu_ridge * np.eye(d)                 # mean-loss Hessian (constant)
evals, Q = np.linalg.eigh(Hbar)
eta = 1.0 / evals.max()
w_star = np.linalg.solve(Hbar, X.T @ y / n)
k = int(np.argmax(np.abs((y - X @ w_star) * X[:, -1])))   # pushes along the flattest input direction
xq = rng.standard_normal(d)
yq = xq @ w_true
phi = lambda w: 0.5 * (yq - xq @ w) ** 2
grad_phi = lambda w: -(yq - xq @ w) * xq
grad_Lk = lambda w: -(y[k] - X[k] @ w) * X[k]
Kmax = 10000


def train(eps):
    # full-batch GD on (1/n) sum_j beta_j L_j + ridge, with beta_k = 1 - eps; returns all iterates
    beta = np.ones(n)
    beta[k] -= eps
    w, W = np.zeros(d), np.empty((Kmax + 1, d))
    for t in range(Kmax + 1):
        W[t] = w
        w = w - eta * (X.T @ (beta * (X @ w - y)) / n + mu_ridge * w)
    return W


W0, Wp, Wm = train(0.0), train(1e-4), train(-1e-4)

3. Bayesian influence functions

Question. What if training produced a distribution rather than a point? Take the local posterior, or Gibbs measure, p_\beta(w)\propto\exp\!\big(-\sum_j\beta_jL_j(w)-\frac\gamma2\|w-w^*\|^2\big): the Gaussian factor localizes around the checkpoint w^* and takes the place of a prior (a Gaussian prior of precision \alpha would just add \alpha to \gamma). Then \pi(\beta) is that local posterior, the observable is its average \mathbb E_{p_\beta}\phi, and the Bayesian influence function is \mathrm{BIF}(z_i,\phi)=\partial\,\mathbb E_{p_\beta}[\phi]/\partial\beta_i at \beta=\mathbf 1.

How. The derivative of a Gibbs average is a covariance, \mathrm{BIF}(z_i,\phi)=-\mathrm{Cov}_p(L_i,\phi) (Walkthrough 3A). No Hessian is formed or inverted: sample w from the local posterior, usually with stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD), and compute sample covariances for all training examples at once.

Result. In a Gaussian approximation of the posterior, the leading term of the local BIF is the damped IF, with the localization \gamma as the damping (Walkthrough 3B). Beyond that order the BIF sees non-quadratic geometry, such as curved or degenerate valleys, that the IF cannot: in the code below, a curved two-parameter valley makes the local BIF twice the damped IF at small \gamma. Kreer et al. report LDS on CIFAR-10 comparable to EK-FAC. Caveat: the estimate is only as good as the sampler. SGLD mixing, its step-size bias and the choice of \gamma are the practical risks. Langevin dynamics and Gibbs laws are in TD2.2 §3.

Walkthrough 3A: the BIF is a covariance

Substitutes for Kreer et al. §2.2–2.3. Shared objective: derive \mathrm{BIF}=-\mathrm{Cov}, and see that Bayesian linear regression gives the damped IF exactly, with the damping in stated units.

Notation. p_\beta(w)=e^{-\sum_j\beta_jL_j(w)}\rho(w)/Z(\beta) with Z(\beta)=\int e^{-\sum_j\beta_jL_j}\rho\,dw; \mathbb E and \mathrm{Cov} are under p=p_{\mathbf 1}. A localization factor e^{-\frac\gamma2\|w-w^*\|^2} can be absorbed into \rho.

Step A1. Differentiate a Gibbs average. \beta_i appears in the weight of every w and in the normalization.

Exercise. Show \partial_{\beta_i}\log Z=-\mathbb E[L_i] and \partial_{\beta_i}\mathbb E_{p_\beta}[\phi]\big|_{\beta=\mathbf 1}=-\mathrm{Cov}_p(L_i,\phi).

Solution

\partial_{\beta_i}Z=\int(-L_i)\,e^{-\sum_j\beta_jL_j}\rho\,dw=-Z\,\mathbb E_{p_\beta}[L_i]. Then, by the quotient rule,

\partial_{\beta_i}\frac{\int\phi\,e^{-\sum_j\beta_jL_j}\rho}{Z} =\frac{\int\phi\,(-L_i)\,e^{-\sum_j\beta_jL_j}\rho}{Z}-\frac{\partial_{\beta_i}Z}{Z}\,\mathbb E_{p_\beta}[\phi] =-\mathbb E[\phi L_i]+\mathbb E[L_i]\,\mathbb E[\phi]=-\mathrm{Cov}(L_i,\phi).

This is a fluctuation–response relation: the response of an average to the field \beta_i equals minus the covariance with the conjugate energy L_i. The localization factor does not involve \beta, so the local BIF is the same covariance under the local posterior.

Step A2. Bayesian linear regression. The one case where everything is Gaussian. It fixes the units of the damping.

Exercise. Let y_j=x_j^\top w+\text{noise}, noise \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2), prior \mathcal N(0,\tau^2I), and L_j(w)=(y_j-x_j^\top w)^2/(2\sigma^2). The posterior is \mathcal N(\mu,V) with A=\sum_jx_jx_j^\top, V=(A/\sigma^2+I/\tau^2)^{-1} and \mu=V\sum_jx_jy_j/\sigma^2. Let e_j=y_j-x_j^\top\mu.

  1. Show \mathrm{BIF}(z_j,w)=Vx_je_j/\sigma^2=(A+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}I)^{-1}x_je_j (the observable is \phi(w)=w, one entry at a time).
  2. Write this as a damped IF in two systems of units: unscaled squared errors \ell_j=\frac12(y_j-x_j^\top w)^2, and the likelihood losses L_j.
Solution

1. Write w=\mu+\Delta with \Delta\sim\mathcal N(0,V). Then L_j=\big(e_j^2-2e_jx_j^\top\Delta+(x_j^\top\Delta)^2\big)/(2\sigma^2). Constants drop out of a covariance, and \mathrm{Cov}\big((x_j^\top\Delta)^2,\Delta\big) is a third central moment of a Gaussian, so zero. Hence \mathrm{Cov}(L_j,w)=-\frac{e_j}{\sigma^2}\mathrm{Cov}(x_j^\top\Delta,\Delta)=-\frac{e_j}{\sigma^2}Vx_j, and \mathrm{BIF}=Vx_je_j/\sigma^2. Multiplying V^{-1}=(A+\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}I)/\sigma^2 out gives the second form.

2. In squared-error units, \nabla\ell_j(\mu)=-x_je_j and the curvature is A, so \mathrm{BIF}=-(A+\lambda I)^{-1}\nabla\ell_j(\mu) with \lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2: a damped IF at the posterior mean, damping noise variance over prior variance. In likelihood units, \nabla L_j(\mu)=-x_je_j/\sigma^2 and H=A/\sigma^2, so \mathrm{BIF}=-(H+\tau^{-2}I)^{-1}\nabla L_j(\mu): the damping is the prior precision \tau^{-2}. So what counts as “the damping” depends on the loss scale: \sigma^2/\tau^2 for squared errors, 1/\tau^2 for likelihood losses.

Step A3. Why it is exact here. Three things made Step A2 exact: a Gaussian posterior, quadratic L_j, and a linear \phi. Here we break the last one.

Exercise. Keep the Gaussian posterior of Step A2 but take a quadratic observable \phi(w)=\frac12w^\top H_\phi w. Show that the BIF now has an extra term -\frac12\mathrm{tr}(H_\phi VH_jV) with H_j=x_jx_j^\top/\sigma^2, which the damped IF misses.

Solution

With \Delta\sim\mathcal N(0,V), \phi=\phi(\mu)+\mu^\top H_\phi\Delta+\frac12\Delta^\top H_\phi\Delta and L_j=L_j(\mu)+\nabla L_j(\mu)^\top\Delta+\frac12\Delta^\top H_j\Delta. Covariances of a linear and a quadratic term are odd moments, so zero. The linear–linear term gives the damped IF with \nabla\phi(\mu)=H_\phi\mu. The quadratic–quadratic term is, by Isserlis’ theorem, \mathrm{Cov}(\frac12\Delta^\top H_\phi\Delta,\frac12\Delta^\top H_j\Delta)=\frac12\mathrm{tr}(H_\phi VH_jV). So the BIF equals the damped IF exactly when the posterior is Gaussian (conjugacy) and the covariance has no higher terms, which here needs L_j quadratic and \phi linear.

Scope. \mathrm{BIF}=-\mathrm{Cov} holds whenever we may differentiate under the integral. Exact agreement with the damped IF is special to Gaussian posteriors with quadratic losses and linear observables. Nothing here says that a sampler reaches the posterior.

Walkthrough 3B: the Gaussian-surrogate expansion

Substitutes for Kreer et al. Appendix A. Shared objective: see the damped IF as the leading term of the local BIF, and see what enters at the next order.

Notation. w^* is a local minimum of L=\sum_jL_j with H=\nabla^2L(w^*)\succ0. We take the dimension fixed and every eigenvalue of H of order n (cnI\preceq H\preceq CnI, as when each example contributes comparable curvature), with derivatives of order 3 and 4 also O(n). Let \Delta=w-w^*, and write g_\phi,H_\phi and g_i,H_i for the gradients and Hessians of \phi and L_i at w^* (all O(1)). T_k[f] is the order-k term of the Taylor series of f at w^*. The Gaussian surrogate is q=\mathcal N(w^*,\Sigma) with \Sigma=H^{-1}, or \Sigma_\gamma=(H+\gamma I)^{-1} for the local posterior. Here the posterior is p(w)\propto e^{-L(w)}\rho(w) with a smooth prior \rho, and the local posterior is p_\gamma(w)\propto e^{-L(w)-\frac\gamma2\|w-w^*\|^2} (the localization replaces the prior, as in Kreer et al.), with \mathrm{BIF}_\gamma=-\mathrm{Cov}_{p_\gamma}(L_i,\phi).

Step B1. The surrogate. Expanding \log p=-L(w^*)-\frac12\Delta^\top H\Delta+O(n|\Delta|^3)+\log\rho and keeping the quadratic term gives q. The posterior has width |\Delta|\sim n^{-1/2}, so the cubic term is O(n^{-1/2}) there and the prior’s gradient is O(1) against the O(n) curvature: both are corrections. For the local posterior, \log\rho is replaced by -\frac\gamma2|\Delta|^2, which adds \gamma I to the quadratic exactly.

Step B2. Odd terms vanish, and order counting. Under q, expand both functions and use bilinearity of the covariance.

Exercise. Show \mathrm{Cov}_q(\phi,L_i)=\sum_{k,m\ge1}\mathrm{Cov}_q(T_k[\phi],T_m[L_i]), that terms with k+m odd vanish, and that the (k,m) term is O(n^{-(k+m)/2}).

Solution

Constant terms drop out of covariances, and \mathrm{Cov} is bilinear. T_k[\phi] is a degree-k polynomial in \Delta, so \mathrm{Cov}(T_k,T_m)=\mathbb E[T_kT_m]-\mathbb E[T_k]\mathbb E[T_m] involves moments of \Delta of total degree k+m. A centered Gaussian has zero odd moments. If k+m is odd, then \mathbb E[T_kT_m]=0, and one of k,m is odd, so \mathbb E[T_k]\mathbb E[T_m]=0. By Isserlis’ theorem a degree-(k+m) moment is a sum of products of (k+m)/2 entries of \Sigma=O(n^{-1}).

Step B3. The leading term. The only O(n^{-1}) term is (1,1).

Exercise. Show \mathrm{Cov}_q(g_\phi^\top\Delta,g_i^\top\Delta)=g_\phi^\top\Sigma g_i. Conclude \mathrm{BIF}(z_i,\phi)\approx-g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i,\qquad \mathrm{BIF}_\gamma(z_i,\phi)\approx-g_\phi^\top(H+\gamma I)^{-1}g_i , the classical and the damped IF, with \gamma in the same (summed-loss) units as \lambda.

Solution

\mathrm{Cov}(g_\phi^\top\Delta,g_i^\top\Delta)=g_\phi^\top\mathbb E[\Delta\Delta^\top]g_i=g_\phi^\top\Sigma g_i. With the minus sign of Walkthrough 3A this is -\nabla\phi^\top H^{-1}\nabla L_i, the IF of DA1 §3, and with \Sigma_\gamma the damped IF. The “\approx” covers both the higher terms of Step B4 and the difference between q and the true posterior.

Step B4. The next order. All terms with k+m=4 are O(n^{-2}), so the next order is not one term.

Exercise. Show that under q (2,2)=\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(H_\phi\Sigma H_i\Sigma),\qquad (1,3)=\tfrac12\,\nabla^3L_i[\Sigma g_\phi,\Sigma],\qquad (3,1)=\tfrac12\,\nabla^3\phi[\Sigma g_i,\Sigma], where \nabla^3f[u,\Sigma]=\sum_{abc}(\nabla^3f)_{abc}u_a\Sigma_{bc}. Which of them survive for a linear observable?

Solution

(2,2): Isserlis gives three pairings of \mathbb E[\Delta_a\Delta_b\Delta_c\Delta_d]. The pairing \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{cd} reproduces \mathbb E[T_2[\phi]]\,\mathbb E[T_2[L_i]] and cancels; the other two give \frac14\mathrm{tr}(H_\phi\Sigma H_i\Sigma) each.

(1,3): T_3[L_i]=\frac16\sum(\nabla^3L_i)_{abc}\Delta_a\Delta_b\Delta_c and \mathbb E[T_3]=0. In \mathbb E[(g_\phi^\top\Delta)\Delta_a\Delta_b\Delta_c] the index of g_\phi pairs with any of the three (3 ways), each giving (\Sigma g_\phi)_a\Sigma_{bc} by symmetry of \nabla^3L_i; so \frac36=\frac12. (3,1) is the same with roles swapped.

For linear \phi, H_\phi=0 and \nabla^3\phi=0, so (2,2) and (3,1) vanish but (1,3) does not, unless L_i is quadratic. A linear observable does not remove the corrections.

The true posterior at this order. The surrogate drops the cubic part of L and the prior. These skew and shift the true posterior, and their effect on the covariance is also O(n^{-2}). Example: one parameter, L=n\big(\frac12w^2+aw^3+bw^4\big), \phi=w, L_i=\frac12(w-c)^2. Every next-order surrogate term vanishes (\phi is linear, L_i quadratic), yet a cumulant expansion of the true posterior gives \mathrm{Cov}(\phi,L_i)=-\frac cn-\frac{6a+c(36a^2-12b)}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}): the skewness (a) and the quartic term (b) of L enter at the same order as the surrogate terms. So the next-order BIF needs the true posterior’s corrections, not only the surrogate terms.

When the surrogate fails. If H is singular the unlocalized surrogate does not exist, but the BIF is still defined. The localized one, \mathcal N\big(w^*,(H+\gamma I)^{-1}\big), exists but can be badly off at weak localization: 35 times too large at \gamma=1 in Code 3.

Scope. We expanded the covariance under a Gaussian surrogate at a nondegenerate minimum with all curvatures of order n, to second order. Not derived: the true posterior’s corrections, which enter at the same order as the (1,3), (2,2) and (3,1) terms, and anything for singular H.

Walkthrough 3C (advanced): influence functions as optimal transport

Substitutes for Mlodozeniec et al. §4.2 (Theorem 3), nondegenerate case. Shared objective: show that shifting each sample of the low-temperature Gibbs distribution by the IF is the KL-optimal first-order way to reach the downweighted distribution, and see why the partition function matters. Long; it uses Laplace’s method.

Notation. \kappa>0 is an inverse temperature (\beta stays the data weights). p_0(w)=\frac{e^{-\kappa L(w)}}{Z_0},\qquad p_\varepsilon(w)=\frac{e^{-\kappa(L(w)-\varepsilon L_k(w))}}{Z_\varepsilon}: z_k downweighted by \varepsilon. w^* is the unique global minimum of L, with H=\nabla^2L(w^*)\succ0 and g_k=\nabla L_k(w^*). A smooth, bounded vector field r defines the transport T_\varepsilon(w)=w+\varepsilon r(w), assumed to be a diffeomorphism for small \varepsilon (bounded \nabla r suffices); q_\varepsilon is the distribution of T_\varepsilon(w) for w\sim p_0. The cost is \mathcal F(r,\varepsilon)=\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\frac1\kappa\mathrm{KL}(q_\varepsilon\,\|\,p_\varepsilon).

Step C1. KL in terms of energies. Change variables back to w\sim p_0.

Exercise. Show \frac1\kappa\mathrm{KL}(q_\varepsilon\|p_\varepsilon)=\mathbb E_{p_0}\big[L(w+\varepsilon r)-\varepsilon L_k(w+\varepsilon r)-L(w)\big]+\frac1\kappa\log\frac{Z_\varepsilon}{Z_0}-\frac1\kappa\mathbb E_{p_0}\log\det(I+\varepsilon\nabla r).

Solution

For a diffeomorphism T, q(T(w))=p_0(w)/|\det\nabla T(w)|. So \mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\varepsilon)=\mathbb E_{\tilde w\sim q}[\log q(\tilde w)-\log p_\varepsilon(\tilde w)] =\mathbb E_{w\sim p_0}\big[\log p_0(w)-\log\det(I+\varepsilon\nabla r)-\log p_\varepsilon(w+\varepsilon r)\big] (\det>0 for small \varepsilon). Insert \log p_0=-\kappa L-\log Z_0 and \log p_\varepsilon=-\kappa(L-\varepsilon L_k)-\log Z_\varepsilon, and divide by \kappa.

Step C2. Low temperature. As \kappa\to\infty, p_0 concentrates at w^*, the log-determinant is O(1), and Laplace’s method gives \frac1\kappa\log Z_\varepsilon\to-\inf_w\big(L-\varepsilon L_k\big).

Exercise. Show \inf_w(L-\varepsilon L_k)=L(w^*)-\varepsilon L_k(w^*)-\frac{\varepsilon^2}2g_k^\top H^{-1}g_k+O(\varepsilon^3), so the partition-function term tends to \varepsilon L_k(w^*)+\frac{\varepsilon^2}2g_k^\top H^{-1}g_k+O(\varepsilon^3), which is not zero.

Solution

Near w^*, L(w^*+\delta)-\varepsilon L_k(w^*+\delta)=L(w^*)-\varepsilon L_k(w^*)+\frac12\delta^\top H\delta-\varepsilon g_k^\top\delta+O(|\delta|^3+\varepsilon|\delta|^2). The minimizer is \delta^*=\varepsilon H^{-1}g_k+O(\varepsilon^2), and the minimum value is L(w^*)-\varepsilon L_k(w^*)-\frac{\varepsilon^2}2g_k^\top H^{-1}g_k+O(\varepsilon^3). For small \varepsilon the global infimum is this local one, because w^* is the unique global minimum. Then \frac1\kappa\log(Z_\varepsilon/Z_0)\to\inf L-\inf(L-\varepsilon L_k)=\varepsilon L_k(w^*)+\frac{\varepsilon^2}2g_k^\top H^{-1}g_k+O(\varepsilon^3).

Step C3. Complete the square.

Exercise. Combine Steps C1–C2 to show \mathcal F(r,\varepsilon)=\frac{\varepsilon^2}2\big(r(w^*)-H^{-1}g_k\big)^\top H\big(r(w^*)-H^{-1}g_k\big)+O(\varepsilon^3). What would go wrong without the partition-function term?

Solution

The expectation concentrates at w^*, where \nabla L=0. With r=r(w^*): L(w^*+\varepsilon r)-L(w^*)=\frac{\varepsilon^2}2r^\top Hr+O(\varepsilon^3) and \varepsilon L_k(w^*+\varepsilon r)=\varepsilon L_k(w^*)+\varepsilon^2g_k^\top r+O(\varepsilon^3). Adding the partition term, the \varepsilon L_k(w^*) terms cancel and

\mathcal F=\frac{\varepsilon^2}2\big(r^\top Hr-2g_k^\top r+g_k^\top H^{-1}g_k\big)+O(\varepsilon^3) =\frac{\varepsilon^2}2(r-H^{-1}g_k)^\top H(r-H^{-1}g_k)+O(\varepsilon^3).

Without the partition term, \mathcal F would contain -\varepsilon L_k(w^*) at first order and, even ignoring that, its minimum over r would be -\frac{\varepsilon^2}2g_k^\top H^{-1}g_k<0: impossible for a KL. The partition function supplies exactly the constant that completes the square.

Step C4. What is determined. The optimum is r(w^*)=H^{-1}g_k: the first-order displacement of the minimizer under downweighting, which is minus the upweighting parameter IF. The cost depends on r only through its value at w^*, so any smooth field with that value is optimal at this order. “The IF is the optimal transport” means the optimal value at the minimum, not a unique vector field.

Step C5. A finite-temperature check. Bayesian linear regression at \kappa=1: L_j=(y_j-x_j^\top w)^2/(2\sigma^2), prior \mathcal N(0,\tau^2I), posterior exactly \mathcal N(\mu,V) with V^{-1}=\sum_jx_jx_j^\top/\sigma^2+I/\tau^2, residuals e_j=y_j-x_j^\top\mu, and (Walkthrough 3A) \mathrm{BIF}(z_k,w)=Vx_ke_k/\sigma^2.

Exercise. Downweighting z_k by \varepsilon gives \mathcal N(\mu_\varepsilon,V_\varepsilon) with V_\varepsilon^{-1}=V^{-1}-\varepsilon x_kx_k^\top/\sigma^2. A constant shift sends \mathcal N(\mu,V) to \mathcal N(\mu+\varepsilon r,V).

  1. Show \mu_\varepsilon=\mu-\varepsilon\,\mathrm{BIF}(z_k,w)+O(\varepsilon^2).
  2. With M=x_kx_k^\top V/\sigma^2, show \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal N(\mu+\varepsilon r,V)\,\|\,\mathcal N(\mu_\varepsilon,V_\varepsilon)\big)=\frac{\varepsilon^2}2(r+\mathrm{BIF})^\top V^{-1}(r+\mathrm{BIF})+\frac{\varepsilon^2}4\mathrm{tr}(M^2)+O(\varepsilon^3).
  3. Check consistency with Step C3: V^{-1} is the Hessian of the energy, and V^{-1}(-\mathrm{BIF})=\nabla L_k(\mu).
Solution

1. By definition \mathrm{BIF}=\partial\mu/\partial\beta_k (Walkthrough 3A), and downweighting is \beta_k=1-\varepsilon.

2. For Gaussians, \mathrm{KL}(\mathcal N(m_0,V)\|\mathcal N(m_1,V_\varepsilon))=\frac12\big[\mathrm{tr}(V_\varepsilon^{-1}V)-d+(m_1-m_0)^\top V_\varepsilon^{-1}(m_1-m_0)+\log\frac{\det V_\varepsilon}{\det V}\big]. Here V_\varepsilon^{-1}V=I-\varepsilon M, so \mathrm{tr}(V_\varepsilon^{-1}V)-d=-\varepsilon\,\mathrm{tr}M and \log\frac{\det V_\varepsilon}{\det V}=-\log\det(I-\varepsilon M)=\varepsilon\,\mathrm{tr}M+\frac{\varepsilon^2}2\mathrm{tr}M^2+O(\varepsilon^3). The covariance part is \frac{\varepsilon^2}4\mathrm{tr}M^2. The mean part is \frac12\varepsilon^2(r+\mathrm{BIF})^\top V^{-1}(r+\mathrm{BIF})+O(\varepsilon^3), using m_1-m_0=-\varepsilon(\mathrm{BIF}+r)+O(\varepsilon^2) and V_\varepsilon^{-1}=V^{-1}+O(\varepsilon). So the best constant shift is r=-\mathrm{BIF}, as in Step C4, but the minimal cost is \frac{\varepsilon^2}4\mathrm{tr}M^2\neq0: a shift cannot reproduce the change of covariance. At low temperature this term is O(1) in KL against the O(\kappa) energy term, which is why it disappears from \mathcal F.

3. The energy is \sum_jL_j+\|w\|^2/(2\tau^2), with Hessian A/\sigma^2+I/\tau^2=V^{-1}. And V^{-1}(-\mathrm{BIF})=V^{-1}(-Vx_ke_k/\sigma^2)=-x_ke_k/\sigma^2=\nabla L_k(\mu), matching r=H^{-1}g_k.

Step C6. Degenerate minima. If H is singular, Step C2 needs care. If g_k has a component along a flat direction, L-\varepsilon L_k can decrease faster than \varepsilon^2 along it: for L=w^4 and L_k=w, \inf(L-\varepsilon L_k)=-3(\varepsilon/4)^{4/3}. The completed square, with H^+ in place of H^{-1}, needs g_k\in\mathrm{Range}(H).

Scope. Proved: a nondegenerate unique minimum and the low-temperature normalized KL. At finite temperature (Step C5) the covariance change costs O(\varepsilon^2) that no shift removes. Theorem 3 of Mlodozeniec et al. treats sets of minima; degenerate directions need at least g_k\in\mathrm{Range}(H) (Step C6), and there the optimal field is not unique.

Code 3: SGLD-BIF against quadrature

Part 1: calibrate the covariance estimator. In Bayesian linear regression (Walkthrough 3A, Step A2, with n=30, d=3) the BIF is known exactly. We draw exact posterior samples, with no SGLD, so any error is Monte Carlo error, and check that the error bars computed from 20 batches are honest: about 95% of the |z|-scores should be below 2, and the error should fall like N^{-1/2}.

Code (27 lines) 
t0 = time.time()
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_b, d_b, sig, tau = 30, 3, 0.5, 1.3
Xb = rng.standard_normal((n_b, d_b))
yb = Xb @ rng.standard_normal(d_b) + sig * rng.standard_normal(n_b)
V = np.linalg.inv(Xb.T @ Xb / sig**2 + np.eye(d_b) / tau**2)
mu_b = V @ Xb.T @ yb / sig**2
bif_exact = (V @ (Xb * (yb - Xb @ mu_b)[:, None]).T / sig**2).T     # (n, d): V x_j e_j / sigma^2
def bif_estimate(N, G=20):
    # -Cov(L_j, w) for all j from N exact posterior draws, with a standard error from G batches
    Wb = rng.multivariate_normal(mu_b, V, size=N)
    Lb = (yb[None, :] - Wb @ Xb.T) ** 2 / (2 * sig**2)                # (N, n) per-example losses
    batches = []
    for B in np.array_split(np.arange(N), G):
        Lc, Wc = Lb[B] - Lb[B].mean(0), Wb[B] - Wb[B].mean(0)
        batches.append(-(Lc.T @ Wc) / (len(B) - 1))
    batches = np.array(batches)
    return batches.mean(0), batches.std(0, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(G)


for N in [10**3, 10**4, 10**5]:
    est, se = bif_estimate(N)
    print(f"N = {N:>6}: rms error {np.sqrt(((est - bif_exact) ** 2).mean()):.1e}, median standard error {np.median(se):.1e}")
zs = np.concatenate([((lambda e, s: (e - bif_exact) / s)(*bif_estimate(10**4))).ravel() for _ in range(30)])
print(f"30 replicates at N = 10^4: fraction of |z| < 2 = {(np.abs(zs) < 2).mean():.3f} "
      f"(t with 19 d.o.f.: 0.940), rms z = {np.sqrt((zs**2).mean()):.2f}")
print(f"({time.time() - t0:.1f}s)")

The observable is the loss on a query z_q=\bar z+0.6; we report the BIF of the training point farthest from \bar z. For each \gamma we compare:

  • quadrature: the exact local BIF, by summing over a fine grid in (a,b);
  • SGLD (here plain, full-batch Langevin) at step sizes h and h/2, with 256 chains started at w^*, run for 64 relaxation times along the valley (a quarter discarded as burn-in), and the Monte Carlo error from 16 groups of chains;
  • the damped IF -\nabla\phi^\top(H+\gamma I)^{-1}\nabla L_i at w^*;
  • the Gaussian surrogate: the same covariance under \mathcal N(w^*,(H+\gamma I)^{-1}), by quadrature.

Look for where the local BIF departs from the damped IF, whether SGLD tracks quadrature within its error bars, and whether halving h moves the estimate.

def cov_on_grid(gamma, density):
    half = 7 / np.sqrt(gamma) + 1.0
    ax_ = np.linspace(-half, half, 801)
    A, B = np.meshgrid(w_star[0] + ax_, w_star[1] + ax_, indexing="ij")
    p = density(A, B)
    p = p / p.sum()
    Li, ph = L_i(A, B), phi(A, B)
    return -((p * Li * ph).sum() - (p * Li).sum() * (p * ph).sum())


def local_posterior(gamma):
    def dens(A, B):
        U = 0.5 * ((z[:, None, None] - A * B) ** 2).sum(0) + 0.5 * gamma * ((A - w_star[0]) ** 2 + (B - w_star[1]) ** 2)
        return np.exp(-(U - U.min()))
    return dens


def surrogate(gamma):
    Prec = H + gamma * np.eye(2)
    return lambda A, B: np.exp(-0.5 * (Prec[0, 0] * (A - w_star[0]) ** 2 + 2 * Prec[0, 1] * (A - w_star[0]) * (B - w_star[1])
                                       + Prec[1, 1] * (B - w_star[1]) ** 2))


def sgld(gamma, h, chains=256, seed=1):
    # Langevin on U = sum_j L_j + gamma/2 |w - w*|^2: w <- w - (h/2) grad U + sqrt(h) xi
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    w = np.tile(w_star, (chains, 1))
    steps = int(128 / (h * gamma))     # the slowest relaxation (along the valley) takes ~2/(h gamma) steps
    draws = []
    for t in range(steps):
        a, b = w[:, 0], w[:, 1]
        res = ((a * b)[:, None] - z[None, :]).sum(1)
        grad_U = np.stack([res * b, res * a], 1) + gamma * (w - w_star)
        w = w - 0.5 * h * grad_U + np.sqrt(h) * r.standard_normal(w.shape)
        if t >= steps // 4 and t % 4 == 0:
            draws.append(w.copy())
    W = np.array(draws)                                      # (draws, chains, 2)
    groups = []
    for cs in np.array_split(np.arange(chains), 16):
        a, b = W[:, cs, 0].ravel(), W[:, cs, 1].ravel()
        groups.append(-np.cov(L_i(a, b), phi(a, b))[0, 1])
    return np.mean(groups), np.std(groups, ddof=1) / 4, steps

4. Influence changes over training

Question. Ask the attribution question at every checkpoint: how does upweighting z_p change a query loss \ell_q(w_t), as a function of t? Here \pi(\beta)=w_t(\beta) for each t, as in unrolling, and we watch the answer evolve.

Result. Attribution is a time series, not a number. Networks learn coarse structure before fine structure (TD NB1). In a deep linear network learning a small hierarchy, an example’s influence on a related query is helpful while their shared coarse structure is learned, harmful on the plateau before the fine structure is learned, and zero at the end (Walkthrough 4, Code 4). Lee et al. report the same kind of sign flips in a toy hierarchy and in a small language model, using the BIF at each checkpoint. Caveat: an endpoint influence function, however exact, sees none of this. In the walkthrough’s dog/cat example (realizable data) it is exactly zero.

Substitutes for the derivation in Lee et al. Appendix C.6. Shared objective: derive how upweighting one example changes a two-layer linear network along its trajectory, separate a “speed” channel from a “rotation” channel, and explain a sign flip. Needs the mode solution of TD NB1 §1, restated here.

Notation. A two-layer linear network f(x)=W_2W_1x with product W=W_2W_1 (all matrices d\times d). Data (x_j,y_j), j=1,\dots,n, with weights \beta_j; mean loss L(W;\beta)=\frac1n\sum_j\beta_j\frac12\|y_j-Wx_j\|^2. With \Sigma_{xx}(\beta)=\frac1n\sum_j\beta_jx_jx_j^\top and \Sigma_{yx}(\beta)=\frac1n\sum_j\beta_jy_jx_j^\top, gradient flow is \tau\dot W_1=W_2^\top E,\qquad \tau\dot W_2=EW_1^\top,\qquad E=\Sigma_{yx}-W\Sigma_{xx}, with \tau=1/\eta, so t/\tau=\eta\times(\text{number of steps}). At \beta=\mathbf 1 the inputs are whitened, \Sigma_{xx}=I, and \Sigma_{yx}=USV^\top with singular values s_1>s_2>\dots>0. From the aligned, balanced, small initialization W_1(0)=\sqrt{a_0}\,V^\top, W_2(0)=\sqrt{a_0}\,U, W(t)=U\,\mathrm{diag}\big(a_k(t)\big)V^\top,\qquad \tau\dot a_k=2a_k(s_k-a_k),\qquad a_k(t)=\frac{s_ke^{2s_kt/\tau}}{e^{2s_kt/\tau}-1+s_k/a_0}. Mode k rises around t_k^*\approx\frac{\tau}{2s_k}\ln(s_k/a_0), strongest first. We work in the mode basis (x\to V^\top x, y\to U^\top y, W\to U^\top WV), which amounts to U=V=I.

We upweight example p, \beta_p=1+\varepsilon, keeping the initialization fixed. Write \delta W(t)=\partial W(t)/\partial\beta_p at \beta=\mathbf 1. The influence on the query loss \ell_q=\frac12\|y_q-Wx_q\|^2 is \mathcal I_q(t)=-r_q(t)^\top\,\delta W(t)\,x_q,\qquad r_q(t)=y_q-W(t)x_q .

Step 1. An example acts through its residual. Reweighting changes both covariances. Together they enter as a forcing proportional to the example’s current residual, so an example the network already fits exerts no force.

Exercise. Show that at \beta=\mathbf 1, \partial E/\partial\beta_p=F(t)-\delta W with F(t)=\frac1nr_p(t)x_p^\top and r_p=y_p-W(t)x_p. Write the linear equations for \delta W_1,\delta W_2 and their initial condition.

Solution

\partial\Sigma_{yx}/\partial\beta_p=\frac1ny_px_p^\top and \partial\Sigma_{xx}/\partial\beta_p=\frac1nx_px_p^\top. So \partial E/\partial\beta_p=\frac1ny_px_p^\top-\delta W\,\Sigma_{xx}-W\frac1nx_px_p^\top=\frac1n(y_p-Wx_p)x_p^\top-\delta W, using \Sigma_{xx}=I. Differentiating the flow,

\tau\,\delta\dot W_1=\delta W_2^\top E+W_2^\top\delta E,\qquad \tau\,\delta\dot W_2=\delta E\,W_1^\top+E\,\delta W_1^\top,\qquad \delta W=\delta W_2W_1+W_2\delta W_1,

with \delta E=F-\delta W and \delta W_1(0)=\delta W_2(0)=0, since the initialization is fixed. These are exact at linear order in \varepsilon. Perturbing only \Sigma_{yx} would give the forcing \frac1ny_px_p^\top instead, which keeps pushing even when p is fit perfectly.

Step 2. Two channels. Along the aligned path, W_1=W_2=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{a_k}) and E=\mathrm{diag}(s_k-a_k) are diagonal, so the linear equations split. The diagonal entries of \delta W (mode strengths: the speed channel) obey closed scalar equations. Each off-diagonal pair (j,k),(k,j) (mode mixing: the rotation channel) couples only to itself.

Exercise. Write P=\delta W_1, Q=\delta W_2, d_k=\sqrt{a_k}, e_k=s_k-a_k. Show that \delta a_k:=\delta W_{kk} satisfies \tau\,\delta\dot a_k=2(s_k-2a_k)\,\delta a_k+2a_k\,F_{kk}(t), and that the entries P_{jk},Q_{jk},P_{kj},Q_{kj} for j\ne k form a closed system.

Solution

With D=\mathrm{diag}(d_k) and E=\mathrm{diag}(e_k): (Q^\top E)_{jk}=Q_{kj}e_k, (D\,\delta E)_{jk}=d_j\delta E_{jk}, (\delta E\,D)_{jk}=\delta E_{jk}d_k and (EP^\top)_{jk}=e_jP_{kj}. So

\tau\dot P_{jk}=e_kQ_{kj}+d_j\,\delta E_{jk},\qquad \tau\dot Q_{jk}=d_k\,\delta E_{jk}+e_jP_{kj},\qquad \delta W_{jk}=d_jP_{jk}+d_kQ_{jk}.

For j\ne k these involve only the four entries listed. For j=k, let \sigma=P_{kk}+Q_{kk}, so \delta a_k=d_k\sigma and \tau\dot\sigma=e_k\sigma+2d_k(F_{kk}-d_k\sigma); also P_{kk}-Q_{kk} obeys \tau\frac{d}{dt}(P_{kk}-Q_{kk})=-e_k(P_{kk}-Q_{kk}) and stays zero. A balanced mode has \tau\dot d_k=d_ke_k. Then \tau\,\delta\dot a_k=\tau\dot d_k\sigma+d_k\tau\dot\sigma=2e_k\delta a_k-2a_k\delta a_k+2a_kF_{kk}=2(s_k-2a_k)\delta a_k+2a_kF_{kk}.

Step 3. The speed channel, solved. Shifting time along the trajectory solves the unforced equation: \dot a_k satisfies it. So the forced equation is solved by variation of parameters.

Exercise. Show \delta a_k(t)=\dot a_k(t)\int_0^t\frac{F_{kk}(t')}{s_k-a_k(t')}\,dt' .

  1. A cross-covariance intervention: replace the forcing F of Step 1 by a constant change \delta\Sigma_{yx} with diagonal entry \delta s, keeping \Sigma_{xx}=I, so F_{kk}=\delta s. Show \delta a_k=(\partial a_k/\partial s_k)\,\delta s, and that \partial a_k/\partial s_k\to1 after mode k saturates.
  2. Realizable data: y_j=Sx_j for every j. Show \delta a_k(t)=\frac{(x_p)_k^2}n\,t\,\dot a_k(t): upweighting p runs mode k’s clock faster by the factor 1+\varepsilon(x_p)_k^2/n.
Solution

Differentiating \tau\dot a=2a(s-a) gives \tau\ddot a=2(s-2a)\dot a. With \delta a=\dot a\,I(t) and I(t)=\int_0^tF/(s-a): \tau\,\delta\dot a=\tau\ddot aI+\tau\dot a\,F/(s-a)=2(s-2a)\delta a+2a(s-a)F/(s-a), which is the equation, and \delta a(0)=0.

1. Differentiate \tau\dot a=2a(s-a) with respect to s: \tau\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial a}{\partial s}=2(s-2a)\frac{\partial a}{\partial s}+2a, with \partial a/\partial s=0 at t=0 (a_0 does not depend on s). That is the equation with F=1, so \delta a=(\partial a/\partial s)\,\delta s. After saturation a_k\to s_k, so \partial a_k/\partial s_k\to1: the change in the learned strength persists.

2. (r_p)_k=(s_k-a_k)(x_p)_k, so F_{kk}/(s_k-a_k)=(x_p)_k^2/n is constant and \delta a_k=\dot a_k\,t\,(x_p)_k^2/n. That is the first-order term of a_k\big((1+\varepsilon c)t\big) with c=(x_p)_k^2/n. With realizable data the target is unchanged; only the speed is.

Step 4. When is influence localized in time? Through the speed channel the query sees -(r_q)_k(x_q)_k\,\delta a_k, the product of the mode-k query residual and \delta a_k.

Exercise.

  1. For realizable data and a realizable query (y_q=Sx_q), show that the speed contribution is localized around t_k^* and is \le0: helpful.
  2. For the cross-covariance intervention, \delta a_k\to\delta s\ne0 after saturation. What then makes the contribution to \mathcal I_q localized?
Solution

1. \dot a_k=2a_k(s_k-a_k)/\tau is exponentially small before the rise (a_k\approx a_0e^{2s_kt/\tau}) and after it (s_k-a_k decays exponentially), with its peak at a_k=s_k/2, near t_k^*; the factor t does not change that. Also (r_q)_k(x_q)_k=(s_k-a_k)(x_q)_k^2\ge0 and \delta a_k\ge0, so the contribution is \le0. Speeding up the learning of true structure helps every query.

2. The factor (r_q)_k=(y_q)_k-a_k(x_q)_k, which tends to zero for a query the network ends up fitting. What is localized is the product of the mode-k residual and the sensitivity, not the sensitivity \partial a_k/\partial s_k, which tends to 1.

Step 5. The derivative of a truncated SVD. Between rises the network sits near a partial reconstruction: modes 1,\dots,r learned (a_k\approx s_k), the rest near zero. Such a point is a critical point of the loss, and near it the reweighting acts like a change \delta\Sigma_{yx}=F of the cross-covariance, with F=\frac1nr_px_p^\top from Step 1. If the network follows the moving critical point, its rotation channel is the derivative of the truncated singular value decomposition. This step computes that derivative.

During mode 2’s rise the speed channel is negative again and the tilt relaxes. After mode 2 is learned everything decays to the endpoint value, zero.

Scope. Exact linear response at fixed aligned, balanced initialization; the speed channel in closed form; the rotation channel in closed form on plateaus only. The data are whitened and realizable; with label noise the endpoint influence can be nonzero, and both channels contribute to it.

5. Learning the map: datamodels and TRAK

Question. Can we skip the derivative? Fix a distribution over training subsets (for example random halves), retrain many times, and fit a predictor of \phi from the subset’s indicator vector \mathbf 1_S\in\{0,1\}^n. A linear datamodel \phi(S)\approx\theta_0+\sum_i\theta_i\mathbf 1[i\in S] has coefficients \theta_i that are attributions for that subset distribution. Here \pi(\beta) is retraining from scratch on the subset with indicator \beta, and the datamodel is a linear fit to \phi(\pi(\beta)) over random subsets.

Cost, and the shortcut. Datamodels needs a very large number of retrainings: 300,000 ResNet-9 models for CIFAR-10 at 50% subsets. TRAK gets close without fitting to retraining outcomes. It linearizes the model around its trained weights, randomly projects per-example gradients to a few thousand dimensions, applies a one-step Gauss–Newton influence estimate in that space, and averages over a few models trained on random subsets. Both are scored with the LDS of DA1 §5, the rank correlation between predicted and actual \phi over random subsets, which is the datamodel target.

Caveat. A high LDS at 50% subsets says how well a method predicts large random deletions. A method can compute its local derivative exactly and still score a low LDS at 50% subsets, and the reverse (DA1 §5).

6. Open problems and uses

Where the methods above stop working, and what attribution is used for.

  • Entangled latent concepts. An example carries many concepts at once, and attribution to examples mixes them all. The question we usually care about is narrower: which data taught the model this concept? Attributing to concepts rather than examples is open.
  • Behaviours rather than losses. The observable is usually a loss or a log-probability. Alignment questions are about behaviours, such as role-play or refusals, which first need a scalar \phi that measures them. Grosse et al.’s role-play queries (§5.3.5) are one way to set this up.
  • Fine-tuning data selection. Scores can be used to choose data instead of explaining it, which tests attribution by its downstream effect.
  • Unlearning. Attribution predicts the effect of removing data; unlearning tries to produce that effect without retraining. Whether an IF-style update achieves real removal is again a question of target versus estimator.
  • Distributional attribution. Training is random (seed, data order). A well-posed target averages over that randomness; Mlodozeniec et al. (Stop 3) call this distributional attribution. Single-run unrolling and fixed-seed LDS measure something else.
  • Scale. Exact methods cost retraining or replay; scalable ones approximate curvature or sample posteriors. Validating the scalable ones at LLM scale needs ground truth that is itself affordable.
