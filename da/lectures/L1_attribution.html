The question

Which training examples made the model do this?

Let it sit. Everyone has an intuition for the answer; the lecture is about why the intuition needs a formalism.

An example

Which pretraining texts make this response likely?

Query: a dialogue in which an AI assistant says it does not consent to being shut down.

810M model: top sequences

Share words with the query ("continue", "existing"), but not its meaning.

52B model: top sequences

Are about wanting to keep existing: an AI pleading with its crew; a person surviving in a desert.

These scores concern the response's likelihood under smaller pretrained models, not why some assistant chose it. Grosse et al. (2023), Fig. 1 and §5.3: influence functions (EK-FAC, which is DA2 stop 1) on pretrained models of two sizes. The influential sequences get more abstract with scale.

This is the kind of answer people want from attribution: "this behaviour came from imitating these kinds of documents". It is also exactly the kind of claim this morning teaches you to check. Don't over-read it: Grosse et al. present it as consistent with role-play by imitation, at a high level of abstraction. Nobody retrained the 52B model without those documents.

Ablating a layer costs a forward pass. Ablating an example costs a training run.

Mechanistic interpretability asks which components of a forward pass caused an output, and tests it by ablating, many times. Data attribution asks which examples of a training run caused a behaviour of the final model.

Retraining costs as much as training, so attribution can rarely afford even one ablation. Most of its methods are ways to predict retraining without doing it. And all the conceptual difficulties of causal reasoning come along.

§1 · Counterfactuals

"Example $i$ caused it if deleting $i$ and retraining changes it."

What could go wrong?

This is leave-one-out (LOO): Lewis's counterfactual dependence, applied to training data. Ask the room for a failure before showing the next slide.

§1 · Overdetermination

Two causes, each sufficient

butteryeastcroissants
premiumpremiumfluffy
standardpremiumfluffy
premiumstandardfluffy
standardstandardflat

Leave-one-out credits neither. In training: a behaviour taught by many redundant examples.

The bakery: premium butter makes more steam, premium yeast makes more gas, and either alone is enough for fluffy croissants. Downgrading only one changes nothing, so LOO says neither caused the fluffiness, though both did.

Picked up in: DA1 §1 (Mueller 2024 is the reading, §4; written about interpretability, and the same failures apply).

Non-transitivity (cut from the slides; tell it if there is time): a boulder falls toward a hiker, she ducks, she survives. Ducking depends on the boulder and survival on ducking, but survival does not depend on the boulder: without it she survives anyway. Dependence does not chain (Ned Hall's example, as told by Hitchcock 2001; Mueller 2024 §3). In training: an example can be necessary for a feature that a capability uses, yet deleting it changes no output.

§1 · Shapley values

Average over every subset the example could join

$$\varphi_i=\mathbb E_{\text{random order}}\big[v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)\big],\quad S=\text{examples before }i$$

Croissants: butter $\tfrac12$, yeast $\tfrac12$. The price: each $v(S)$ is a retraining, for up to $2^{n-1}$ subsets.

$v(S)$: the behaviour of a model trained on subset $S$. Shuffle the $n$ examples uniformly at random and add them one by one; $\varphi_i$ is the expected jump in $v$ when $i$ arrives. Equivalently (DA1 §1), $\varphi_i=\frac1n\sum_{S\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}}\binom{n-1}{|S|}^{-1}[v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)]$: average over subsets of each size, then over the sizes. In the bakery $v(S)=1$ if $S$ contains butter or yeast; LOO gives 0 to everything, Shapley gives butter and yeast ½ each, flour and sugar 0.

Shapley attributes over finite coalitions: it is not the derivative at the full training set that the rest of the day computes. Practical versions sample permutations (Ghorbani & Zou 2019). It still splits credit between sufficient causes, where one could argue each deserves all of it.

§1 · Takeaway

"The influence of example $i$" is ambiguous until you say which counterfactual.

§2 · The formalism

How do you make "delete example $i$" something you can differentiate?

Deletion is discrete. Calculus needs a continuous knob.

§2 · Data weights

Give every example a weight

$$L(w;\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\beta_i\,L_i(w)$$

$\beta=\mathbf 1$ is the real run; $\beta_i:1\to0$ deletes example $i$.

$w$: parameters. $L_i$: the loss on training example $z_i$. Note the summed loss, not the mean: curvatures and damping constants are in summed-loss units all day. For an argmin it doesn't matter (the minimizer of $cL$ is that of $L$); for SGD we write the $1/b$ explicitly.

Picked up in: DA1 §2.

§2 · The object of study

Weights → trained model → behaviour

$$\beta\;\xrightarrow{\ \ \pi\ \ }\;\pi(\beta)\;\xrightarrow{\ \ \phi\ \ }\;\phi\big(\pi(\beta)\big)$$

$\pi$, "the trained model", is a modelling choice. $\phi$ is the behaviour: a test loss, a logit.

$\phi(w)$: a scalar observable of the trained parameters; we also call it (or its test input) the query. $\pi(\beta)$: what training on weights $\beta$ produces. Is it the argmin? This seed's last iterate? A posterior? Methods differ mainly in this choice (the map on slide 57).

§2 · Influence

The influence of example $i$ on $\phi$

$$\mathcal I_i=\frac{\partial\,\phi(\pi(\beta))}{\partial\beta_i}\bigg|_{\beta=\mathbf 1}$$

Deleting $i$ changes $\phi$ by about $-\mathcal I_i$. On a test loss, a helpful example has $\mathcal I_i<0$.

Conventions, said once: summed loss; upweighting derivative; deletion is $\beta_i:1\to0$; helpful has negative influence on a test loss. Koh and Liang upweight by $\varepsilon$ on the mean loss, so their $\mathcal I_{\rm up}$ is $n$ times ours (DA1 Exercise 2.1). TracIn, TRAK and SOURCE report helpful as positive.

§2 · Three targets

Three questions, at three distances from the real run

The derivative; leave-one-out; deleting a large random subset.

Local derivative: $\mathcal I_i$. Leave-one-out: $\phi(\pi(\mathbf 1-e_i))-\phi(\pi(\mathbf 1))$, which equals $-\mathcal I_i$ only to first order. Subset deletion: $\phi(\pi(\mathbf 1_S))$ for a random $S$, typically half the data; first-order prediction $\phi(\pi(\mathbf 1))-\sum_{j\notin S}\mathcal I_j$.

§2 · A puzzle for later

The derivative and the deletion can disagree

§2 · Takeaway

Influence is the derivative of a behaviour in one example's weight, through a chosen $\pi$.

§3 · The classical influence function

If training lands at a minimum, where does the minimum go when you reweight one example?

Here $\pi$ is "the minimizer". Start with the smallest possible model.

§3 · Warm-up

The influence of a point on a mean

$$\begin{aligned} L(\mu;\beta)&=\tfrac12\textstyle\sum_j\beta_j(z_j-\mu)^2\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{\mu(\beta)}&\htmlClass{fragment}{=\frac{\sum_j\beta_jz_j}{\sum_j\beta_j}}\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial\beta_i}\Big|_{\mathbf 1}}&\htmlClass{fragment}{=\frac{z_i-\bar z}{n}} \end{aligned}$$

A point's pull on the mean is its distance from the mean, over $n$.

Quotient rule: $\partial\mu/\partial\beta_i=\big(z_i\sum_j\beta_j-\sum_j\beta_jz_j\big)/(\sum_j\beta_j)^2$, at $\beta=\mathbf 1$ equal to $(nz_i-n\bar z)/n^2$.

Robust statistics asked this first (Hampel 1974): move a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the mass to $z$ and the mean moves at rate $z-\mu$. Keep $(z_i-\bar z)/n$ in mind: it comes back in §4.

§3 · The general case

The trained model is defined by an equation

$$\nabla_wL\big(w^*(\beta);\beta\big)=0\quad\text{for all }\beta\text{ near }\mathbf 1$$

Implicit function theorem: if $H=\nabla^2_wL$ is invertible at $w^*$, this defines a smooth $w^*(\beta)$.

So differentiate the equation, not the training algorithm.

$w^*=w^*(\mathbf 1)$ is the trained model. We are assuming a branch $w^*(\beta)$ of stationary points exists and is smooth; the implicit function theorem (on the slide) says when.

§3 · Differentiate in $\beta_i$

Two places $\beta_i$ enters

$$\nabla_wL(w;\beta)=\textstyle\sum_j\beta_j\nabla L_j(w)$$
$$\underbrace{\nabla^2_wL\;\frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\beta_i}}_{\text{through }w^*}\;+\;\htmlClass{fragment}{\underbrace{\nabla L_i(w^*)}_{\text{explicitly}}}\;=\;0$$

At $\beta=\mathbf 1$: $H=\nabla^2_wL(w^*;\mathbf 1)$ is the Hessian, $g_i=\nabla L_i(w^*)$ is $i$'s gradient.

Chain rule on $F(w,\beta)=\nabla_wL(w;\beta)=\sum_j\beta_j\nabla L_j(w)$: the $w$-dependence gives $\partial F/\partial w\cdot\partial w^*/\partial\beta_i$, and $\partial F/\partial\beta_i=\nabla L_i$.

§3 · Solve

The influence function

$$\frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\beta_i}=-H^{-1}g_i\qquad\htmlClass{fragment}{\boxed{\;\mathcal I_i=-\,g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i\;}}$$

$g_\phi=\nabla\phi(w^*)$; the chain rule through $\phi$. Needs $H$ invertible.

This is Koh and Liang (2017) §2, in our convention. Deleting $i$ changes $\phi$ by about $-\mathcal I_i=g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i$.

In practice: solve $Hu=g_\phi$ once per query, then $\mathcal I_i=-u^\top g_i$ for every training example. L2 is about doing that solve when $H$ has $10^{18}$ entries.

Picked up in: DA1 §3.

§3 · Check

It recovers the warm-up

$$H=n,\quad g_i=-(z_i-\bar z)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad -H^{-1}g_i=\frac{z_i-\bar z}{n}$$

For the mean, $L_i=\tfrac12(z_i-\mu)^2$.

$\partial L_i/\partial\mu=-(z_i-\mu)$, second derivative 1 per example, so $H=n$ in summed units.

§3 · Read it right to left

$H^{-1}$ turns a pull into a move

Flat directions move most. Then $g_\phi$ reads off what $\phi$ cares about.

Three factors of $-g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i$: $g_i$ is how example $i$ pulls on the parameters (its descent direction is $-g_i$); $H^{-1}$ converts force to displacement, a lot along small eigenvalues and little along large ones; $g_\phi$ projects the displacement onto the observable.

This is why the gradient dot product $-g_\phi^\top g_i$ (the IF with $H$ replaced by $I$) can be badly wrong: it ignores the rotation. §5 shows it.

§3 · What the theorem gives

A local branch, not "the retrained model"

The IF follows the nearby minimum; retraining from scratch may land in another basin.

The implicit function theorem needs only $H$ invertible, and gives the unique smooth branch of stationary points through $w^*$. To call that branch "the retrained model" also needs $w^*$ to be a strict minimum and retraining to stay in this basin. Both hold automatically for a strictly convex loss.

The tilt in the figure is exaggerated. DA1 Exercise 3.3 has a version with numbers: $L_1=(w^2-1)^2$, $L_2=0.10w$, $L_3=-0.11w$; lowering $\beta_3$ to 0.8 moves the global minimum to the other well.

§3 · Takeaway

One linear solve per query: $\mathcal I_i=-g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i$.

§3 · How good is it?

How well does a derivative predict deleting a whole example?

Back to the 5.7× puzzle. Linear regression is the one model where we can do the deletion exactly, in closed form.

§3 · Least squares

Everything is explicit

$$L_j=\tfrac12(y_j-w^\top x_j)^2,\qquad A=\sum_jx_jx_j^\top$$ $$\htmlClass{fragment}{H=A,\qquad g_j=-x_j\,r_j,\qquad r_j=y_j-x_j^\top w^*}$$

The IF says deleting $j$ moves the fit by $-A^{-1}x_j\,r_j$.

$w^*=A^{-1}\sum_jx_jy_j$. The influence prediction of the deletion change: $-\partial w^*/\partial\beta_j=H^{-1}g_j=-A^{-1}x_jr_j$. Upweighting $j$ pulls the fit toward $j$, along $A^{-1}x_j$, in proportion to the residual.

Picked up in: DA1 Exercise 3.1, the required exercise of the morning. You may want to do it before looking at the next three slides.

§3 · Exact deletion, step 1

Subtract the two sets of normal equations

$$\begin{aligned} \text{all data:}&\quad A\,w^*=\textstyle\sum_ix_iy_i\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{\text{without }j\text{:}}&\quad\htmlClass{fragment}{(A-x_jx_j^\top)(w^*+\delta)=\textstyle\sum_ix_iy_i-x_jy_j}\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{\text{subtract:}}&\quad\htmlClass{fragment}{A\,\delta=x_j\,\big(\underbrace{x_j^\top\delta-r_j}_{=:\,c}\big)} \end{aligned}$$

$\delta=w^*_{(-j)}-w^*$ is the exact change. So $\delta=c\,A^{-1}x_j$: only the size $c$ is unknown.

$\delta=w^*_{(-j)}-w^*$. Expand the left side and use $Aw^*=\sum_ix_iy_i$: $A\delta-x_jx_j^\top w^*-x_jx_j^\top\delta=-x_jy_j$, then collect.

§3 · Exact deletion, step 2

Find the size from its own definition

$$\begin{aligned} x_j^\top\delta&=c\;\underbrace{x_j^\top A^{-1}x_j}_{h_j}\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{c}&\htmlClass{fragment}{=h_j\,c-r_j\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=-\frac{r_j}{1-h_j}} \end{aligned}$$

$h_j$ is the leverage of example $j$.

Take the inner product of $\delta=cA^{-1}x_j$ with $x_j$, then substitute into $c=x_j^\top\delta-r_j$.

§3 · The result

Exact deletion is the IF, magnified

$$w^*_{(-j)}-w^*=-\frac{A^{-1}x_j\,r_j}{1-h_j}$$
$$\frac1{1-h_j}=\underbrace{1}_{\text{IF}}+\,h_j+h_j^2+\cdots$$

The influence function keeps the leading 1. The higher terms are the higher-order Taylor terms of the response in $\beta_j$, which linear regression happens to sum in closed form (Sherman–Morrison; DA1 Exercise 3.2 gives the whole response curve, the black line of slide 15).

§3 · Leverage

How far a point sticks out, in the data's own metric

$$h_j=x_j^\top A^{-1}x_j\in[0,1],\qquad\sum_jh_j=p$$

$p$: the number of parameters. At $h_j=1$ the point alone pins down a direction.

At $h_j=1$, deleting the point leaves the fit undetermined: $(A-x_jx_j^\top)A^{-1}x_j=x_j(1-h_j)=0$, so the reduced design is singular.

$h_j$ is the diagonal of the hat matrix $X A^{-1}X^\top$, an orthogonal projection of rank $p$ (the number of parameters): hence $0\le h_j\le1$ and the trace is $p$, so the average leverage is $p/n$.

With $\hat\Sigma=A/n$, $h_j=\frac1n x_j^\top\hat\Sigma^{-1}x_j$: a squared length in the metric set by the data. With an intercept, it is $\frac1n$ plus $\frac1n$ times the squared Mahalanobis distance of the other features from their mean.

§3 · The puzzle, solved

Low leverage: close. High leverage: far too small.

Point B has leverage $h=0.825$, so $1/(1-h)=5.7$: the puzzle from slide 15.

Left: removing A ($h=0.068$, blue) or B ($h=0.825$, red), with the exact refit (solid) and the IF prediction (dotted); for A both are visually close to the full fit (its correction factor is 1.07). Right: every point's ratio of exact to predicted change in slope lies on $1/(1-h)$. 15 points, $p=2$, so the leverages sum to 2.

§3 · Takeaway

The IF is least reliable for exactly the points you most want to ask about. And this model is convex and converged.

§4 · Neural networks

At a network checkpoint, which assumptions survive?

  • $H$ invertible? No: flat directions.
  • $H$ positive? No: negative curvature.
  • $\nabla L=0$? No: training stopped early.

Three standard modifications turn $-g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i$ into the formula used in practice, $-g_\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i$. The theme: each fix changes the question being answered. Bae et al. (2022) §§1–4 is the full argument.

Picked up in: DA1 §4.

§4 · Fix 1 · Flat directions

Symmetries make valleys of minimizers

A toy: predict $ab$, losses $L_i=\tfrac12(z_i-ab)^2$. Along the valley, $H$ has a zero eigenvalue.

Networks have continuous families of minimizers: rescaling symmetries, unused units, more parameters than constraints. The $a\cdot b$ model is the smallest example: minimizers are the hyperbola $ab=\bar z$; rescaling $(a,b)\to(\alpha a,b/\alpha)$ moves along it. At a minimizer, $H=n\,vv^\top$ with $v=(b^*,a^*)$: rank one. $H^{-1}$ does not exist.

The SLT day uses the same model.

§4 · Fix 1 · Damping

Damping is exact for a proximal question

$$L(w;\beta)+\frac\lambda2\|w-w^*\|^2\quad\htmlClass{fragment}{\xrightarrow{\ \text{Hessian at }w^*\ }\quad H+\lambda I}$$
$$\mathcal I_i^{\lambda}=-\,g_\phi^\top(H+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i$$

Not a numerical patch: reweight $i$, while paying to move away from $w^*$.

At a stationary $w^*$ the proximal term has zero gradient, so $w^*$ is still stationary for the new objective at $\beta=\mathbf 1$, and the derivation of §3 goes through with $H+\lambda I$. For $H\succeq0$, increasing $\lambda$ shrinks the parameter response; the scalar influence can still grow or change sign.

§4 · Fix 1 · The toy, by hand

The gradient points across the valley

$$H=n\,vv^\top,\quad g_i=-(z_i-\bar z)\,v\quad\htmlClass{fragment}{\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal I^\lambda_i=\frac{(z_i-\bar z)\;g_\phi^\top v}{n\|v\|^2+\lambda}}$$

$g_i$ has no flat component, so $\lambda\to0$ is finite. For $\phi=ab$ the limit is $(z_i-\bar z)/n$: the mean again.

In general, in $H$'s eigenbasis $(s_k,u_k)$, $(H+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i=\sum_kc_ku_k/(s_k+\lambda)$ with $g_i=\sum_kc_ku_k$. On a flat direction ($s_k=0$) the factor is $1/\lambda$, which matters only if $c_k\neq0$; then the response grows like $1/\lambda$. If $g_i$ has no flat component, the $\lambda\to0$ limit is the pseudoinverse answer $-H^+g_i$.

$v=(b^*,a^*)$ is an eigenvector of $H$ with eigenvalue $n\|v\|^2$, so $(H+\lambda I)^{-1}v=v/(n\|v\|^2+\lambda)$. $g_i$ is parallel to $v$: no flat component, so nothing diverges.

For $\phi=ab$ (what the model computes, constant along the valley), $g_\phi^\top v=\|v\|^2$ and the limit $(z_i-\bar z)/n$ is the same at every minimizer. For $\phi=a^2$, $g_\phi=(2a^*,0)$, $g_\phi^\top v=2a^*b^*$, and the limit $2\bar z(z_i-\bar z)/(n\|v\|^2)$ depends on the minimizer.

Picked up in: DA1 Exercise 4.1.

§4 · Fix 1 · Where you stand matters

Finite limits, but which one?

$\phi=a^2$ changes along the valley: its answer depends on the minimizer.

Each curve: plateau for $\lambda\ll n\|v\|^2$, then $1/\lambda$ decay. Circles: the derivative of retraining by gradient flow warm-started at that minimizer, by finite differences; they match the plateaus because gradient flow conserves $a^2-b^2$ and moves along $v$. Retraining from a random init could land anywhere on the new hyperbola, so for $a^2$ there is no retraining-independent answer, and damping is what picks one.

§4 · Fix 2 · Negative curvature

Split the Hessian through the outputs

$$L=\sum_i\ell_i\big(f_i(w)\big),\qquad\nabla L=\sum_iJ_i^\top\,\nabla_f\ell_i$$
$$H=\htmlClass{fragment}{\underbrace{\sum_iJ_i^\top H_{y,i}\,J_i}_{G\text{, Gauss–Newton}}}\;\htmlClass{fragment}{+\;\underbrace{\sum_{i,c}\frac{\partial\ell_i}{\partial f_{ic}}\,\nabla^2_wf_{ic}}_{\text{residual term}}}$$

$f_{ic}$: output $c$ on example $i$. $J_i=\partial f_i/\partial w$. $H_{y,i}=\nabla^2_f\ell_i$. Product rule: two terms.

$f_i(w)\in\mathbb R^C$: the network's outputs on example $i$. Differentiate twice: once gives $\sum_iJ_i^\top\nabla_f\ell_i$; the second derivative hits either the $J_i$ (giving the residual term) or the $\nabla_f\ell_i$ (giving $G$).

The residual term vanishes when the output gradients do (squared error at interpolation) or when the model is linear in its parameters.

Picked up in: TD §6.1 (same decomposition) and L2 (how to multiply by $G$).

§4 · Fix 2 · Why $G$

$G$ can't have negative eigenvalues

$$\ell_i\text{ convex in }f\ \Longrightarrow\ G\succeq0\ \Longrightarrow\ G+\lambda I\succ0\ \text{ for every }\lambda>0$$

With $H$, an eigenvalue $s<0$ makes $1/(s+\lambda)$ blow up at $\lambda=-s$.

Squared error and softmax cross-entropy are both convex in the outputs. Near $\lambda=-s$ the damped Hessian IF gives huge responses of either sign.

A caution (cut from the slides): $G\neq H$ even at a strict minimum. One parameter, $f(w)=w^2$, target $-1$, squared error: $L=\frac12(w^2+1)^2$ has a strict minimum at $w=0$, where $J=f'(0)=0$ so $G=0$, while the residual $(f+1)f''=2$ gives all of $H=2$. So the Gauss–Newton influence need not equal the Hessian influence even at a converged minimum.

§4 · Fix 3 · Non-convergence

The checkpoint need not minimize the training loss.

So what is $-g_\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i$ the derivative of?

At a non-stationary $w^s$, the implicit-function derivation has nothing to start from. Bae et al. (2022): "If influence functions are the answer, then what is the question?" Construct an objective whose optimum is the checkpoint by design.

§4 · Fix 3 · A tool

The Bregman divergence: the gap above the tangent

$f^s$: the checkpoint's outputs. $D(f,f^s)=\ell(f)-\ell(f^s)-\nabla\ell(f^s)^\top(f-f^s)$: zero gradient at $f^s$, Hessian $H_y$.

For squared error, $D=\frac12\|f-f^s\|^2$. In general it measures how far the outputs moved, in the loss's own geometry, and is non-negative for convex $\ell$.

§4 · Fix 3 · The PBRF

Reweight, paying for every move

$$Q(w;\beta)=\underbrace{\sum_j(\beta_j-1)\,L_j(w)}_{\text{the reweighting}}\htmlClass{fragment}{+\underbrace{\sum_jD_j\big(f_j(w),f_j(w^s)\big)}_{\text{output cost}}}\htmlClass{fragment}{+\underbrace{\tfrac\lambda2\|w-w^s\|^2}_{\text{weight cost}}}$$

$w^s$: the saved checkpoint. The minimizer of $Q$ is the proximal Bregman response function, PBRF$(\beta)$.

Bae et al. (2022) §5. $w^s$: the saved checkpoint, which need not be stationary. The first term is zero at $\beta=\mathbf 1$ and adds or removes example $j$'s loss as $\beta_j$ moves.

§4 · Fix 3 · Apply §3 to $Q$

The checkpoint is optimal by construction

$$\begin{aligned} Q(w;\mathbf 1)&=\textstyle\sum_jD_j+\frac\lambda2\|w-w^s\|^2\ \ge0,\ \text{ with equality at }w^s\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{\nabla^2_wQ(w^s)}&\htmlClass{fragment}{=\textstyle\sum_jJ_j^\top H_{y,j}J_j+\lambda I=G+\lambda I}\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{\partial_{\beta_i}\nabla_wQ}&\htmlClass{fragment}{=\nabla L_i(w^s)=g_i} \end{aligned}$$

No residual term: $D_j$ has zero gradient in $f$ at $f^s$.

The Hessian in $w$ of $D_j(f_j(w),f_j^s)$ at $w^s$ is $J_j^\top H_{y,j}J_j$ plus $\sum_c(\partial D_j/\partial f_c)\nabla^2_wf_{jc}$, and the second sum is zero because $\nabla_fD_j=0$ at $f^s$. That is where $G$ comes from: not an approximation, an exact Hessian of a different objective.

§4 · Fix 3 · The result

The practical formula, and its question

$$\frac{\partial\,\phi\big(\mathrm{PBRF}(\beta)\big)}{\partial\beta_i}\bigg|_{\beta=\mathbf 1}=-\,g_\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i$$

Exact, for a question that isn't retraining. Slide 3's LLM results used an approximation of it.

In words: how would $\phi$ change if $i$ were reweighted, while moving the outputs on the training set and moving the parameters were both penalized? This is the formula DA2 stop 1 computes at LLM scale (with EK-FAC for $G$).

§4 · The proximity gap, measured

The damped IF answers its own question

A 25-parameter MLP: it ranks proximal retraining almost perfectly, plain retraining hardly at all.

Note: this experiment uses the damped Hessian IF of slide 36, $-g_\phi^\top(H+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i$, not Gauss–Newton. The training objective includes a fixed ridge $\frac{10^{-3}}2\|w\|^2$ in every refit, and $H$ includes it. Spearman for proximal retraining is 0.995.

This is one of five gaps Bae et al. identify between the IF and leave-one-out retraining. Changes of question: warm start (retraining starts from $w^s$, not a fresh init), proximity (moving the parameters is penalized), non-convergence (the Bregman term stands in for the loss). Errors: linearization, the approximate solve. For networks the changes of question dominate (their Fig. 4).

One hidden layer of 8 tanh units, 20 noisy points of $\sin2x$, trained to a minimum by Newton's method; $\phi$ is the squared error at one test input; $\lambda$ is 1% of the top Hessian eigenvalue. Deleting each point: damped-IF prediction vs proximal retraining (the IF's own target, made finite) vs plain retraining from $w^*$.

The Hessian's eigenvalues run from $5\times10^{-4}$ to 106: without the proximal term, a deletion moves the parameters far along nearly flat directions (across deletions, the vector of plain-retraining changes has 49 times the norm of the IF predictions). Which one you want is a choice of question, not a matter of accuracy.

Picked up in: DA1 §4 extension.

§4 · Takeaway

Each fix changes the question. The damped Gauss–Newton IF answers the PBRF question exactly.

§5 · Checking

How would you know an attribution is right?

Every method outputs a number per training example. Which question do you test it against?

§5 · Two separate things

Target and estimator

  • Target: the question. A choice of $\pi$, and of one of the three targets.
  • Estimator: how you compute the answer.
  • They fail separately, so they need separate checks.

We have now met two targets for one formula: the damped IF is the derivative of the proximal (PBRF) response, not of retraining. The table on slide 52 makes it concrete: one method computes its own target to seven digits and still predicts leave-one-out worse than another.

§5 · Three checks

From least to most demanding

  1. Finite differences of the method's own target.
  2. Leave-one-out retraining.
  3. Retraining on random half-subsets.

1 tests the estimator only: reweight by $\pm\varepsilon$, recompute $\pi$, difference $\phi$. 2 tests one finite deletion. 3 tests large deletions, far from $\beta=\mathbf 1$: it rewards estimators that extrapolate, which need not be those that differentiate exactly.

For networks: retraining with a new seed moves $\phi$ too. Single-model targets (this run, this seed: unrolling) differ from distributional ones (averaged over seeds: datamodels, TRAK's protocol).

§5 · The linear datamodeling score

Do summed scores order the subsets?

$$\mathrm{LDS}=\mathrm{Spearman}\Big(\big\{\phi(S_k)\big\},\ \Big\{\textstyle\sum_{i\in S_k}\mathcal I_i\Big\}\Big)$$

$S_k$: a random half of the data; retrain on it and measure $\phi(S_k)$. It checks the ranking, not the size.

$S_1,\dots,S_m$: random subsets, typically half the data. $\phi(S_k)$: the behaviour after retraining on $S_k$. To first order, $\phi(S)-\phi(\text{all})=-\sum_{j\notin S}\mathcal I_j$, which is $\sum_{i\in S}\mathcal I_i$ up to a constant. Averaged over test points. Park et al. (2023) Definition 2.4.

Multiplying every score by 10, or any increasing transform of the predictions, leaves the LDS unchanged.

Different checks rank methods differently

Both IFs nail their own target; LOO prefers the exact IF; LDS can't tell them apart.

Logistic regression, $n=80$: pick the check that matches your question. DA1 §5's setup: $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression, 80 examples in 10 dimensions with feature scales spanning 1.5 decades, 10 test losses as observables; Newton solver, so every check is deterministic. Damping $\lambda$ = median Hessian eigenvalue.

The damped IF is off by 48% against the plain local derivative, but matches its own proximal target to $3\times10^{-7}$: a different target, not a defect. It is worse on LOO and ties on LDS. Calibration slopes on the half-subsets are 12 to 35, far from 1: a high LDS doesn't license "deleting these changes the loss by this much".

The gradient dot product ignores the $H^{-1}$ rotation of slide 23; the Hessian's eigenvalues here span a factor of about 360.

§6 · Another $\pi$

What if $\pi$ is this training run, not an argmin?

The simplest case of unrolling, and a lens on the afternoon's methods. If short of time, skip to the map (slide 57) and say: the methods differ in $\pi$.

§6 · A lens

Many estimators filter the curvature spectrum

$$\frac{\partial w}{\partial\beta_i}=-F(M)\,g_i=-\sum_kF(s_k)\,c_k\,u_k$$

Classical IF: $F(s)=1/s$. Damped: $1/(s+\lambda)$. Now one more.

$M$: a curvature matrix with eigenpairs $(s_k,u_k)$; $g_i=\sum_kc_ku_k$. Methods differ in $F$ and in which matrix $M$ is ($H$, $G$, a segment's average Hessian).

Picked up in: DA1 §6, with a table of filters.

§6 · Unroll gradient descent

Differentiate $K$ steps instead of an argmin

$$\begin{aligned} r_t&=\partial w_t/\partial\beta_i,\qquad w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\nabla_wL(w_t;\beta)\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{r_{t+1}}&\htmlClass{fragment}{=(I-\eta H)\,r_t-\eta\,g_i,\qquad r_0=0}\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{r_K}&\htmlClass{fragment}{=-\eta\textstyle\sum_{k<K}(I-\eta H)^k\,g_i}\\[4pt] \htmlClass{fragment}{F(s)}&\htmlClass{fragment}{=\eta\textstyle\sum_{k<K}(1-\eta s)^k=\dfrac{1-(1-\eta s)^K}{s},\qquad F(0)=\eta K} \end{aligned}$$

Constant curvature and gradient give a geometric series; per eigenvalue, a filter.

Assume $H$ and $g_i$ constant over the $K$ steps. On the range of $H$ the sum is $r_K=-[I-(I-\eta H)^K]H^{-1}g_i$; on its null space, $r_K=-\eta K\,g_i$. For $0<\eta s<2$ the filter tends to $1/s$ as $K\to\infty$.

This is the simplest case of unrolling (DA2 stop 2). SOURCE's filter $(1-e^{-\eta Ks})/s$ is its continuous-time cousin.

§6 · The filters

Same shape, different meanings

$\lambda$ is a proximal penalty; $\eta K$ is training time. Both tame the flat directions.

Plotted with $\lambda=1$ and $\eta K=1$. Every filter but the classical one is finite at $s=0$: $F(0)=1/\lambda$, $\eta K$, $1/\gamma$ (local Bayesian IF at Gaussian leading order; $\gamma$ a localization strength) or $\tau^2/\sigma_{\rm noise}^2$ (Bayesian linear regression in $X^\top X$ units: prior over noise variance). For GD, directions with $s\ll1/(\eta K)$ haven't responded yet: influence depends on how long you train.

What the lens hides: general unrolling has changing curvature and non-commuting step Jacobians; the Bayesian IF beyond leading order sees non-quadratic structure; datamodels have no such form. Shapes are similar; questions are not.
