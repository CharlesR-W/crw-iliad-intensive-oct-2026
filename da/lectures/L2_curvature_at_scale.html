Data attribution · Lecture 2

Curvature at scale

From Hessian-vector products to the spectrum of a 100B-parameter model

jvp and vjp · Pearlmutter · finite differences · Lanczos · stochastic Lanczos quadrature · Granziol & Juarev (2026)

About 30 minutes (30–35 live); the 15-minute path is in the outline, L2_curvature_at_scale.md. Keys: N toggles these notes, S opens the speaker view.

Figures use a toy tanh MLP: 1,573 parameters, $C=5$ classes, $n=1000$, curvature of the mean loss (eigenvalues are $1/n$ of the summed-loss ones; ratios and fractions are unaffected).

Where we are

This morning gave the formula. Now we compute with the matrix inside it.

$$\mathcal I_i=-\nabla\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}\nabla L_i$$
  • Every method this afternoon approximates $(G+\lambda I)^{-1}$, replaces it by a filter of the same shape, or avoids it.
  • All of them need curvature information about a model with $10^8$ to $10^{11}$ parameters.
  • Every direct look at the true curvature uses one primitive: $v\mapsto Hv$.

Through-line: one primitive, $v\mapsto Hv$; what it buys; what trained spectra look like; how it was done at 120B; what that means for attribution.

Notation clashes to say out loud: the damping $\lambda$ against eigenvalues $\lambda_j$; Lanczos $\beta_k$ against data weights $\beta_i$. Eigenvalues carry an index; the damping never does.

Picked up in: DA1 §4 (where the formula comes from), DA2 stop 1 (computing it at scale).

§1 · Never form $H$

$H$ has $P^2$ entries: $10^{18}$ for a 1B model. Ask questions that need only $Hv$.

$v\ \mapsto\ Hv$
→
solve $(H+\lambda I)x=g$ · what an IF needs
→
top-$k$ eigenpairs · sharpness, IFs in a subspace
→
spectral density $\rho(s)=\frac1P\sum_j\delta(s-\lambda_j)$ · SLQ

In bf16, $10^{18}$ numbers are two exabytes. The toy spectra in TD 2.6 used nets with a few thousand parameters, where eigh still works; today's figures use such a net, so every estimate can be compared with the exact answer.

The trace is the density's first moment: $\operatorname{tr}H=P\int s\,\rho(s)\,ds$.

Plan: how to get $Hv$ (§2–3), then one arrow at a time (§4–6).

Picked up in: DA2 stop 1 ("never form $G$ or its inverse").

§2 · Forward and reverse mode

Autodiff gives two exact products, each for a few forward passes

For $f:\mathbb R^P\to\mathbb R^d$ with Jacobian $J$:

$$\textbf{jvp: }v\mapsto Jv\quad(\text{forward mode}),\qquad\textbf{vjp: }u\mapsto J^\top u\quad(\text{reverse mode})$$
  • jvp: carry a tangent next to every value. Forward-pass memory, nothing stored.
  • vjp: store activations, then one backward pass. Backprop is $\nabla L=J^\top 1$.
  • Code: jax.jvp, jax.vjp; torch.func.jvp, torch.func.vjp.

The only slide of autodiff theory. Both products are cheap and exact (to rounding), whatever $P$ and $d$ are, and neither forms $J$.

Picked up in: DA2 Code 1 uses torch.func; da/checks/L2_checks.py tests both products against an explicit Jacobian.

§3 · Hessian-vector products

$Hv$ is a derivative of the gradient, so autodiff gives it exactly

$$Hv=\nabla_w\big(\nabla L(w)^\top v\big)=\frac{d}{dr}\,\nabla L(w+rv)\Big|_{r=0}$$
  • Reverse-over-reverse ("double backward"): differentiate the scalar $\nabla L^\top v$. Keeps the backward graph.
  • Forward-over-reverse: a jvp of the gradient. Pearlmutter's (1994) $\mathcal R_v\{\cdot\}$ operator.
  • Cost: gradient plus $Hv$ is about 2–3× the gradient alone (Schraudolph 2002).

Pearlmutter: $\mathcal R_v\{f(w)\}=\frac{\partial}{\partial r}f(w+rv)|_{r=0}$, so $\mathcal R_v\{\nabla_w L\}=Hv$. He says it takes "about as much computation" as a gradient.

PyTorch: double backward is torch.autograd.grad(..., create_graph=True) twice; forward-over-reverse is torch.func.jvp(torch.func.grad(L), (w,), (v,)).

§3 · The third route: finite differences

Two ordinary gradients and a step size. The step size gives a V.

$$Hv\approx\frac{\nabla L(w+\epsilon v)-\nabla L(w-\epsilon v)}{2\epsilon}:\quad\text{truncation}\propto\epsilon^2,\ \ \text{round-off}\propto\frac{\epsilon_{\rm mach}}{\epsilon}\ \Rightarrow\ \epsilon^*\propto\epsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/3}$$

Toy MLP. Best $\epsilon\approx1.5\times10^{-2}$ in fp32 (error $3\times10^{-5}$); $0.2$ with bf16 gradients (error $3\times10^{-3}$). Best error $\propto\epsilon_{\rm mach}^{2/3}$.

Works inside any training loop, including a sharded one. Granziol & Juarev (2026), Thm 3.1, for unit $v$: error $\le\frac{\epsilon^2}{6}\|\nabla_w(D_v^3L)\|+O(\frac{\epsilon_{\rm mach}}{\epsilon}\|\nabla L\|)$, with $D_v^3L$ the third derivative of $L$ along $v$. That is leading order; a rigorous version takes the sup of the fourth derivative over the segment. Their Remark 3.2: fp32 $\epsilon^*\sim10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$, errors $\sim10^{-5}$; bf16 $\epsilon^*\sim10^{-1}$, errors $\sim10^{-2}$. The toy agrees.

Ask: where is the minimum for your model? The constants depend on the model. And $\epsilon$ is a step along a unit vector in $\mathbb R^P$: each weight moves by about $\epsilon/\sqrt P$ (about $4\times10^{-4}$ here; about $10^{-8}$ for $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ at 160M parameters). Step sizes are not comparable across models: sweep $\epsilon$ against an exact HVP on a probe. Hold that for §8.

The bf16 curve rounds only the two gradients. With bf16 weights, a $10^{-8}$ perturbation would round away, so it has to live in higher precision. Autodiff $Hv$ in fp32: $2\times10^{-7}$ (the horizontal line).

Identity: the gradient central difference is exactly the average of $H(w+tv)v$ over $t\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]$. A large $\epsilon$ gives curvature averaged along a segment, not a noisy $Hv$. (Their Thm 3.3 is the triangular-kernel version for the second difference of $L$.)

§3 · Gauss–Newton products

$Gv$ costs one jvp and one vjp

$$Gv=\sum_iJ_i^\top H_{y,i}\,(J_iv)$$
  • One jvp gives every $J_iv$; multiply by the small $C\times C$ matrix $H_{y,i}$; one vjp (Schraudolph 2002).
  • $G$ is the matrix of DA1 §4 and of EK-FAC.
  • $G\succeq0$ for convex output losses, so conjugate gradients on $(G+\lambda I)x=g$ converges.

$H-G$ need not vanish even at a minimum (DA1 §4's $f(w)=w^2$ example).

Picked up in: DA1 §4 (why $G$), TD 2.6 §6.1 (the decomposition $H=G+\text{residual}$).

§4 · Solves

Iterative IF solves can cost thousands of HVPs per query. Hence EK-FAC for IFs.

  • Solves: Koh & Liang (2017) used conjugate gradients and LiSSA. LiSSA "often" needs thousands of iterations, each at least a gradient (Grosse et al. 2023).
  • Power iteration: $v\leftarrow Hv/\|Hv\|$ finds the largest-magnitude eigenpair; error $\sim|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k$. Slow when the top eigenvalues are close.
  • Hutchinson: $\operatorname{tr}H=\mathbb E_v[v^\top Hv]$ for $\mathbb E[vv^\top]=I$. One HVP per probe.

Grosse et al. §2.2.1 also note that CG is a full-batch method; its iteration count depends on conditioning and tolerance. Every IF query needs its own solve. EK-FAC itself came from optimization (George et al. 2018); Grosse et al. applied it to IFs.

For i.i.d. $\pm1$ probes, $\operatorname{Var}(v^\top Hv)=2(\|H\|_F^2-\sum_jH_{jj}^2)$; Gaussian probes give $2\|H\|_F^2$. PyHessian packages power iteration, Hutchinson and SLQ for PyTorch.

Sharpness is the largest algebraic eigenvalue: use Lanczos targeting the top end, as TD 2.2 does.

Picked up in: DA2 stop 1 (EK-FAC: one cheap approximate solve per query).

§5 · Lanczos

$m$ HVPs compress $H$ into an $m\times m$ tridiagonal matrix

$$Hq_k=\beta_{k-1}q_{k-1}+\alpha_kq_k+\beta_kq_{k+1},\qquad Q_m^\top HQ_m=T_m=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1&\beta_1&\\ \beta_1&\alpha_2&\ddots\\ &\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}$$
  • Start from a unit $q_1=v$. $Q_m$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal K_m=\operatorname{span}\{v,Hv,\dots,H^{m-1}v\}$.
  • Cost: $m$ HVPs plus $O(mP)$ vector work.
  • Ritz values $\theta_k$ (eigenvalues of $T_m$) approximate eigenvalues of $H$.

Each new HVP is orthogonalized against only the two previous vectors; symmetry of $H$ makes the rest vanish in exact arithmetic.

§5 · Outside in

Ritz values converge to the extreme eigenvalues first

Top five eigenvalues converged by $m\approx12$; the bulk stays a smear. Without reorthogonalization, converged eigenvalues return as "ghost" copies: four copies of $\lambda_{\max}$ by $m=60$.

Why outside-in: Ritz values are optimal over polynomials of degree $m-1$, and a polynomial can single out an isolated eigenvalue far more easily than one inside a dense cluster. The Kaniel–Paige bounds (refined by Saad) use Chebyshev polynomials: the rate depends on the gap to the rest of the spectrum relative to its spread.

This asymmetry is the whole story of what SLQ can and cannot see.

Code you have seen: TD 2.2 §2.5's top_eig_lanczos calls scipy's eigsh (implicitly restarted Lanczos) on double-backward HVPs.

§5 · The memory wall

Keeping the basis orthogonal costs memory

  • In floating point the $q_k$ lose orthogonality once a Ritz value converges.
  • Full reorthogonalization keeps all of $Q_m$: $m$ vectors of length $P$.
  • At $P=10^{11}$ in bf16, one vector is 200 GB. That rules out reorthogonalization at LLM scale; without it, $m$ must stay small.
  • Eigenpairs route to IFs: Schioppa et al. (2022) use Arnoldi for the top eigenpairs, then IFs in that subspace (up to a 300M ViT).

Full reorthogonalization also adds $O(m^2P)$ work. Arnoldi is the version for non-symmetric matrices; Schioppa et al. run it on a data subset and invert $H$ in the top-eigenvector subspace: the "eigenpairs" arrow from slide 3, used for attribution.

§6 · The target

A probe sees the spectrum as a weighted measure

$$\int f\,\rho=\frac1P\operatorname{tr}f(H),\qquad v^\top f(H)\,v=\sum_j(v\cdot u_j)^2f(\lambda_j)=\int f\,d\mu_v$$
  • $\mu_v$: nodes at the eigenvalues $\lambda_j$, weights $(v\cdot u_j)^2$.
  • For $v=z/\sqrt P$ with i.i.d. $\pm1$ entries $z_j$: $\mathbb E[\mu_v]=\rho$ exactly.
  • So: approximate $\int f\,d\mu_v$ well for a few probes, and average.

$\mathbb E[(v\cdot u_j)^2]=u_j^\top\mathbb E[vv^\top]u_j=1/P$. Any probe with $\mathbb E[vv^\top]=I/P$ works; Ghorbani et al. used Gaussian probes.

§6 · Golub–Welsch

$T_m$ matches $2m$ moments of $\mu_v$: it is a Gauss rule

$$e_1^\top T_m^k e_1=v^\top H^kv,\quad k\le2m-1$$
$$v^\top f(H)v\approx\sum_{k=1}^m\tau_k^2f(\theta_k)$$
  • Nodes: Ritz values $\theta_k$.
  • Weights: $\tau_k^2=(e_1^\top y_k)^2$, from $T_my_k=\theta_ky_k$.

The figure: one probe; the cumulative weight of $\mu_v$ (1,573 steps) against the 10-node Gauss rule. The rule matches moments 0 to 19 of $\mu_v$ to rounding (checked numerically).

Reference: Golub & Meurant, Matrices, Moments and Quadrature; Wikipedia's Gaussian quadrature page has the Golub–Welsch construction.

Physicists may know this as the recursion method: the $\alpha_k,\beta_k$ are continued-fraction coefficients of a local density of states.

§6 · Stochastic Lanczos quadrature

Average the Gauss rules over a few probes, then smooth

$$\hat\rho(s)=\frac1{n_v}\sum_{\text{probes}}\ \sum_{k=1}^m\tau_k^2\,\delta(s-\theta_k)\ \ \xrightarrow{\ \text{Gaussian of width }\sigma\ }\ \text{plot}$$
  • Two error sources: quadrature ($m$ iterations) and Monte Carlo ($n_v$ probes).
  • Ubaru, Chen & Saad (2017); Chen, Trogdon & Ubaru (2021) for the error analysis.
  • Ghorbani et al. (2019): $m=90$. Granziol & Juarev (2026): $m=10$ at 120B.

Moments of degree up to $2m-1$ are exact in expectation, so their error is all Monte Carlo. In the toy, the first, second and fourth moments are off by $+1.7\%$, $-2.3\%$, $-5.4\%$ at every $m$ from 10 to 90 (same probes). More iterations do not fix Monte Carlo error.

In high dimension one probe already averages over many eigenvectors. For probes uniform on the sphere, the number of probes in Chen, Trogdon & Ubaru's Wasserstein bound falls roughly like $\log P/P$ at fixed accuracy (never below one). That is why huge models get away with few probes.

Code: google/spectral-density (Ghorbani et al., TF and JAX, archived) and PyHessian.

§6 · What SLQ sees

Outliers come out at once; the bulk needs more iterations

Same toy, $n_v=10$ probes. Left: $m=90$ and $m=30$ match. Right: $m=10$ has the outliers right and a lumpy bulk.

Dotted lines: the top $C=5$ eigenvalues. Log scale on the density: the bulk near zero holds 89% of the eigenvalues.

§6 · Resolution

SLQ resolves the outliers, not the fine structure near zero

  • Resolved: the outliers and the bulk's overall shape.
  • The mean eigenvalue: no quadrature error, but check its spread across probes.
  • Not resolved: individual bulk eigenvalues. Coarsest near zero, exactly where an IF's damping acts.
Toy: fraction of eigenvalues with $|s|<\sigma$ is 0.891 exactly, 0.890 by SLQ at $m=90$, 0.78 at $m=10$.

For a spectrum dominated by a few outliers, ten probes can leave the mean uncertain by tens of percent (e.g. rank one: relative s.d. $\approx\sqrt{2/n_v}$).

Picked up in: DA1 §6. The filters act eigenvalue by eigenvalue; the density says how many eigenvalues sit where.

§7 · Trained classifiers

A bulk near zero and about $C$ outliers

  • Sagun et al. (2017): on $k$-cluster data, the number of outliers above the gap equals the number of classes.
  • Ghorbani et al. (2019): SLQ up to ImageNet. Without BatchNorm, outliers appear fast and the gradient concentrates in their span; with it, both almost vanish.
  • Papyan (2018, 2020): outliers from class means in $G$: about $C$, with a weaker $C(C-1)$ layer below. The bulk: within-class variation and $H-G$.

Ghorbani et al.: ResNet-18 and Inception-V3 (27M parameters) on ImageNet. Large negative eigenvalues early in training vanish within the first 1% of steps.

Papers report $C$, $C-1$ or about $C^2$ outliers, depending on what they count, the cutoff, the loss, normalization and the stage of training. Our toy ($C=5$), relative to the largest: 1, 0.97, 0.68, 0.42, 0.35, then 0.23. A real gap, not a dramatic one.

Picked up in: TD 2.6 §6.3 (the class-mean mechanism) and §6.4 (the gradient lives in the top subspace).

§7 · Language models

For a language model, $C$ is the vocabulary

  • "About $C$ outliers" means tens of thousands, not a handful.
  • SLQ with $m=10$ nodes per probe cannot resolve $10^4$–$10^5$ outliers one by one.
  • Transformers are also uneven by block: Zhang et al. (2024) ran SLQ per parameter block and found very different spectra across blocks, unlike CNNs.

Zhang, Chen, Ding, Li, Sun & Luo (NeurIPS 2024), "block heterogeneity", §3.1; a Claude-suggested extra.

The LLM spectra in §8 are among the first direct looks.

§8 · Granziol & Juarev (2026)

Under sharding, use the finite difference, shard by shard

  • FSDP (ZeRO-3): each GPU stores a slice of every parameter tensor. The authors: naive HVPs force expensive all-gathers.
  • Their move: perturb $w\mapsto w\pm\epsilon v$ in place on each shard; two ordinary gradient passes over the same data; restore.
  • Only scalar all-reduces extra. $T_{Hv}\approx2\,T_{\rm grad}$.
  • The gain is compatibility with the training stack, not FLOPs. Basis memory is handled by small $m$.

arXiv 2602.00816, §3.1 and Algorithm 1. Cost (§5.1): Qwen-0.6B on one node, 1 → 8 GPUs gives 5.74× per HVP (Table 3); Lanczos bookkeeping adds 1.3–1.8% over the HVPs (Table 2); on a 32B model, Table 5 reports about 4× over HessFormer (89.6 s at batch 32 against 22.2 s at batch 80). Multi-node scaling is shown on 32B (Table 4).

HessFormer (Granziol 2025), the previous record: Pearlmutter HVPs on one 8-GPU node, SLQ on a 70B DeepSeek distilled model.

§8 · Reading the paper

Five things that will otherwise trip you up

  • "Exact" means the true $H$, not a structured approximation. The finite difference still has truncation error.
  • Figs 2–8 plot Ritz values against weights ("Gamma", "Ritz weights"): our $\theta_k$, $\tau_k^2$. Not smoothed densities.
  • "Smoothing parameter $\epsilon$" is the finite-difference step, not the plotting width $\sigma$.
  • Their $\theta$ is the parameters. Pearlmutter's method is called "reverse-over-forward" there.
  • Their loss is a dataset average: eigenvalues are $1/n$ of summed-loss ones.

The name for the order of the two passes varies between communities; what matters is that one pass differentiates the other.

§4's Lanczos error theorem (Thm 4.1) assumes the finite-difference errors are unbiased and independent across iterations; Thm 3.1 gives an $O(\epsilon^2)$ bias, so at least the unbiasedness assumption is optimistic. Read §4 as heuristic (it is optional reading).

§8 · Finding 1

The step size $\epsilon$ can fake a spectrum

  • Pythia-160M: at $\epsilon=10^{-5}$ and at $10^{-2}$, a random-matrix-like bulk with no separated outliers. At $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$, clear outliers (Figs 3–6).
  • The authors attribute the failures to round-off and to averaging along the segment.
  • But the "good" $\epsilon$ is the one that shows the expected outliers. The check from slide 6, against an exact HVP on a probe, is not reported.
  • They find Pythia-1.4B more stable in $\epsilon$ (Fig 8). One pair of models.

Tie back to the V figure: the same two failure modes, now in a picture that looks like physics. The exact-HVP check would be cheap at 160M.

Their Remark 3.2 puts the fp32 $\epsilon^*$ at $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$, yet their sweet spot is $10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}$ fails. Same lesson as slide 6: the constants, and $\sqrt P$, move the optimum.

The fp32/bf16 labels in their figures refer to how the Lanczos basis vectors are stored, not the gradients; they conclude bf16 storage with fp32 scalar arithmetic is enough. 30× more data (a 30% split instead of 1%) leaves the spectrum essentially unchanged at fixed $\epsilon$ (Fig 7). Fig 8's panels: $\epsilon=10^{-2}$, $3\times10^{-3}$, $10^{-3}$, $3\times10^{-4}$; the text claims consistency for $10^{-4}\le\epsilon\le10^{-3}$.

§8 · Finding 2

gpt-oss-120b: large negative eigenvalues, from 10 iterations

  • Density on wikitext-2, sequence length 64, $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ (§7, Fig 2).
  • 10 Lanczos iterations, no reorthogonalization, $\epsilon$ not swept at this size.
  • The authors call the plot "only indicative". Why 10: without reorthogonalization, ghosts appear.
  • Three readings: not memorised (authors); not near a minimum of this loss; numerics. The plot cannot separate them.

HessFormer's paper: it is prudent to stay below 20 iterations without reorthogonalization, and it often uses 10. Reorthogonalizing at 120B would need 240 GB per bf16 basis vector.

Three readings of the negative eigenvalues, side by side. The authors': the data, as tokenized, has not been memorised. Another: a pretrained checkpoint need not be near a minimum of this particular loss (wikitext-2, sequence length 64). A numerical one: no $\epsilon$ sweep, 10 iterations, no reorthogonalization. The figure cannot separate them.

Negative eigenvalues live in $H-G$ only, since $G\succeq0$: the IF's $G$ has none.

The paper gives neither the number of probes nor the hardware for this run, and no code; HessFormer's code is public.

§8 · Finding 3

Block-diagonal curvature can be wrong by $O(1)$

Per transformer layer $b$, true $(Hv)_b$ against the block product $H_{bb}v_b$, unit Gaussian $v$:

$$\text{rel. err.}=\frac{\|(Hv)_b-H_{bb}v_b\|}{\|(Hv)_b\|}\qquad(\text{predicting }0\text{ gives exactly }1)$$
  • Qwen-0.6B, 28 layers: $0.94\pm0.06$; cosine $0.31\pm0.12$ (Table 6).
  • DeepSeek-Coder 1.3B after fine-tuning, 24 layers: $1.21\pm0.15$; cosine $0.00\pm0.13$ (Table 7).

Most of $(Hv)_b$ comes from the other layers. For the DeepSeek-Coder model they report a substantially higher cosine in the final block (§6.2).

The caveat that changes a conclusion: this is $H$ with random probes. EK-FAC approximates $G$, and an IF applies $(G+\lambda I)^{-1}$ to gradients, which may live in a different subspace. A warning, not a verdict on IFs. In the other direction: EK-FAC's blocks are single weight matrices, finer than a whole transformer layer, so if anything the test is generous to the block assumption.

DA2 Code 1 measures the IF-level error on a 147-parameter net: rank correlation with the exact damped GN IF is 0.65–0.73 for block-diagonal $G$, 0.26–0.45 for K-FAC and EK-FAC, across $\lambda$ from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}$ times the mean eigenvalue.

§9 · Back to attribution

Attribution wants the spectrum of $G$. It shows where $\lambda$ sits.

  • $Gv$ is the same primitive; the 2026 paper measured $H$.
  • SLQ sees the outliers well; where $0.1\times$mean falls inside the bulk is what it resolves least well.
  • Grosse et al.: $\lambda=0.1\times$ mean eigenvalue. Toy: 78% of eigenvalues below it. Between bulk and outliers: 99.7%.

The toy's $G$ (mean loss); log axis, so the bulk spans about seven decades. Grosse et al. apply the rule layer by layer to the EK-FAC eigenvalues; here it is applied to the exact $G$.

On directions with $s_k\ll\lambda$, $(G+\lambda I)^{-1}$ acts as $\lambda^{-1}$. At scale both choices of $\lambda$ are computable: the outliers come out well, and the mean has no quadrature error (check its spread across probes).

The mean eigenvalue is $\operatorname{tr}G/P$: Hutchinson gives it from a few Gauss–Newton products $Gv$ (with $Hv$ it would give $\operatorname{tr}H$, a different number). DA2's Code 1 sweeps $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}$ times the mean eigenvalue; DA1 §5's exercise uses the median Hessian eigenvalue.

$\lambda$ also has a meaning: DA1 §4's proximal penalty. Choosing it from the spectrum is a statement about where you trust the quadratic model; choosing it from the proximal target is a statement about the question.

§9 · What the IF is made of

Far below $\lambda$: a scaled gradient dot product. Near or above: curvature-weighted.

$$\mathcal I_i=-\sum_k\frac{(u_k\cdot\nabla\phi)(u_k\cdot\nabla L_i)}{s_k+\lambda}\approx-\frac1\lambda\!\sum_{s_k\ll\lambda}\!(u_k\cdot\nabla\phi)(u_k\cdot\nabla L_i)-\!\sum_{s_k\gtrsim\lambda}\!\frac{(u_k\cdot\nabla\phi)(u_k\cdot\nabla L_i)}{s_k+\lambda}$$
  • Gradient-similarity scores differ from the IF on directions with $s_k$ near or above $\lambda$.
  • Which directions matter depends on the projections $u_k\cdot\nabla\phi$, $u_k\cdot\nabla L_i$ and the weights; the density gives neither projection.
  • Auditing EK-FAC (blocks + a Kronecker-factored eigenbasis): blocks cost $O(1)$ in $Hv$ (2026); the Kronecker step costs more at the IF level (DA2 Code 1). Measurements, not theorems.

$(s_k,u_k)$: eigenpairs of $G$, as in DA1 §6. The first sum is the TracIn-like part, scaled by $1/\lambda$.

Contributions depend on both projections and on the weights $1/(s_k+\lambda)$. TD 2.6 §6.4 finds the gradient concentrated in the top Hessian subspace, where the weights are smallest, so concentration alone does not say which part dominates.

Picked up in: DA1 §6, DA2 stop 1, DA2 Code 1.

Summary

One primitive, three questions, one picture, one warning

  • Primitive: $v\mapsto Hv$ or $Gv$. Exact by autodiff, or two gradients and a step size.
  • Questions: solve, top eigenpairs, density. Lanczos finds eigenpairs outside in; the same $T_m$ is a Gauss rule; SLQ averages it.
  • Picture: a bulk near zero and about $C$ outliers.
  • Warning: an approximation or a step size can give a plausible wrong answer. Check against an exact HVP on a probe.
