Data attribution · Lecture 2

Curvature at scale

From Hessian-vector products to the spectrum of a 100B-parameter model

About 30 minutes, 51 slides; many are 20-second slides. The 15-minute path is in the outline (L2_curvature_at_scale.md). Keys: N toggles these notes, S opens the speaker view; arrows step through the build-ups.

Figures use a toy tanh MLP: 1,573 parameters, $C=5$ classes, $n=1000$, curvature of the mean loss (eigenvalues are $1/n$ of summed-loss ones; ratios and fractions are unaffected).

Where we are

This morning gave the formula

$$\mathcal I_i=-\nabla\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}\nabla L_i$$

For a 1B-parameter model, the matrix inside has $10^{18}$ entries.

Point at $(G+\lambda I)^{-1}$. Every method this afternoon approximates it, replaces it by a filter of the same shape (DA1 §6), or avoids it. In bf16, $10^{18}$ numbers are two exabytes.

Through-line: every direct look at the true curvature uses one primitive, $v\mapsto Hv$. Then what it buys, what trained spectra look like, how it was done at 120B, and what it means for attribution.

Notation clashes to say out loud: damping $\lambda$ against eigenvalues $\lambda_j$; Lanczos $\beta_k$ against data weights $\beta_i$. Eigenvalues carry an index; the damping never does.

Picked up in: DA1 §4, DA2 stop 1.

The plan

Three questions need only products $v\mapsto Hv$

$v\ \mapsto\ Hv$
→
solve $(H+\lambda I)x=g$
→
top eigenpairs
→
spectral density $\rho(s)$: where the eigenvalues sit

$H$: the $P\times P$ Hessian of the training loss (or $G$, its Gauss–Newton part).

$P$ is the number of parameters. Solve: what an IF needs. Top eigenpairs: sharpness, IFs in a subspace. Density $\rho(s)=\frac1P\sum_j\delta(s-\lambda_j)$: what stochastic Lanczos quadrature (SLQ) estimates; the trace is its first moment, $\operatorname{tr}H=P\int s\rho(s)\,ds$.

Plan: how to get $Hv$ (slides 4–15), then one arrow at a time.

§1 · The primitive

How do you multiply by a Jacobian you can't even store?

Let the room answer for ten seconds. Then: autodiff never forms $J$; it only ever multiplies by it.

§1 · Two directions

Forward and reverse mode

Forward mode pushes a tangent through; reverse mode pulls a covector back.

For $f:\mathbb R^P\to\mathbb R^d$ with Jacobian $J=J_2J_1$ (chain rule). Forward mode multiplies the layer Jacobians onto $v$ as it goes; reverse mode multiplies their transposes onto $u$ on the way back.

§1 · The two products

jvp and vjp, for a Jacobian $J$

$$\underbrace{v\mapsto Jv}_{\text{jvp}}\qquad\htmlClass{fragment}{\underbrace{u\mapsto J^\top u}_{\text{vjp}}}$$

Each costs a few forward passes, at any size; backprop is a vjp.

jvp: one forward pass with a tangent next to every value; forward-pass memory, nothing stored. vjp: one forward pass storing activations, then one backward pass. Backprop: $\nabla L=J^\top 1$ for scalar $L$.

Code: jax.jvp, jax.vjp; torch.func.jvp, torch.func.vjp. Both exact to rounding; neither forms $J$.

Picked up in: DA2 Code 1 uses torch.func.

§1 · A tiny example

Forward mode is arithmetic with dual numbers

$$\begin{aligned} &f(x_1,x_2)=x_1x_2+\sin x_1,\qquad x_1=1+\varepsilon,\ \ x_2=2,\ \ \varepsilon^2=0\\[6pt] &\htmlClass{fragment}{f=(2+\sin 1)+(2+\cos 1)\,\varepsilon} \end{aligned}$$

The $\varepsilon$-coefficient is $\partial f/\partial x_1$: a jvp with $v=(1,0)$.

On the board: $x_1x_2=2+2\varepsilon$ and $\sin x_1=\sin1+(\cos1)\varepsilon$, so $f=2.841+2.540\,\varepsilon$; checked against torch.func.jvp in da/checks/L2_checks.py. One pass, exact, and the tangent rides along with the value.

§1 · From gradients to Hv

Move along $v$ and watch the gradient

$$\nabla L(w+rv)=\nabla L(w)+r\,Hv+O(r^2)$$

The gradient changes along $v$ at rate $Hv$.

Taylor expansion of the gradient. Nothing new yet; the point is that $Hv$ is hiding in something we can already compute.

§1 · From gradients to Hv

So $Hv$ is a directional derivative of the gradient

$$Hv=\frac{d}{dr}\,\nabla L(w+rv)\Big|_{r=0}$$

And a directional derivative is exactly what a jvp computes.

Pearlmutter (1994) calls this operator $\mathcal R_v\{\cdot\}=\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(\cdot)(w+rv)|_{r=0}$, with $\mathcal R_v\{\nabla L\}=Hv$.

§1 · Pearlmutter's trick

$Hv$ is a jvp of the gradient function

$$Hv=\operatorname{jvp}\big(\nabla L;\ w,\ v\big)\qquad\htmlClass{fragment}{=\ \nabla_w\big(\nabla L^\top v\big)}$$

Exact, for about two to three gradients' worth of work.

First form: forward-over-reverse, a jvp of the gradient, Pearlmutter (1994). Second form: reverse-over-reverse ("double backward"): differentiate the scalar $\nabla L^\top v$; keeps the backward graph.

Cost: Pearlmutter says "about as much computation" as a gradient; Schraudolph (2002) puts gradient plus product at 2–3× the gradient alone.

PyTorch: torch.func.jvp(torch.func.grad(L), (w,), (v,)), or torch.autograd.grad twice with create_graph=True.

§1 · Gauss–Newton

The Gauss–Newton product, piece by piece

$$Gv=\sum_i\ \underbrace{J_i^\top}_{\text{vjp}}\ \underbrace{H_{y,i}}_{\substack{\text{loss Hessian}\\ \text{in the outputs}}}\ \underbrace{(J_iv)}_{\text{jvp}}$$

$J_i$: the Jacobian of the outputs on example $i$. One jvp, a small multiply, one vjp.

$G$ is the matrix of DA1 §4 and of EK-FAC; $H_{y,i}$ is $C\times C$ for $C$ outputs. Schraudolph (2002). $G\succeq0$ for convex output losses, so conjugate gradients on $(G+\lambda I)x=g$ converges. $H-G$ need not vanish even at a minimum (DA1 §4's $f(w)=w^2$ example).

Picked up in: DA1 §4 (why $G$), TD 2.6 §6.1 ($H=G+$ residual).

§1 · Without double differentiation

Or just take two ordinary gradients

$$Hv\approx\frac{\nabla L(w+\epsilon v)-\nabla L(w-\epsilon v)}{2\epsilon}$$

Works inside any training loop. But which $\epsilon$?

Unit $v$. This is the route the 2026 paper takes under sharded training (§5).

§1 · Two errors

Truncation versus round-off

Shrink $\epsilon$ and round-off grows; enlarge it and truncation grows.

Granziol & Juarev (2026), Thm 3.1, for unit $v$: error $\le\frac{\epsilon^2}{6}\|\nabla_w(D_v^3L)\|+O(\frac{\epsilon_{\rm mach}}{\epsilon}\|\nabla L\|)$, with $D_v^3L$ the third derivative of $L$ along $v$. Leading order; a rigorous version takes the sup of that fourth derivative over the segment. Balancing gives $\epsilon^*\propto\epsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/3}$ and best error $\propto\epsilon_{\rm mach}^{2/3}$.

Their Remark 3.2: fp32 $\epsilon^*\sim10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$, errors $\sim10^{-5}$; bf16 $\epsilon^*\sim10^{-1}$, errors $\sim10^{-2}$.

Identity: the gradient central difference is exactly the average of $H(w+tv)v$ over $t\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]$. A large $\epsilon$ gives curvature averaged along a segment, not a noisy $Hv$. (Their Thm 3.3 is the triangular-kernel version for the second difference of $L$.)

§1 · Measured

On a real network you get the V

The best $\epsilon$ moves with precision: sweep it against an exact $Hv$.

Toy MLP: best $\epsilon\approx1.5\times10^{-2}$ in fp32 (error $3\times10^{-5}$); $0.2$ with bf16-rounded gradients (error $3\times10^{-3}$); autodiff $Hv$ in fp32 at $2\times10^{-7}$ (the horizontal line); fp64 finite differences reach $4\times10^{-11}$ at $\epsilon\approx2\times10^{-5}$ (not plotted). The toy agrees with Remark 3.2.

$\epsilon$ is a step along a unit vector in $\mathbb R^P$: each weight moves by about $\epsilon/\sqrt P$ (about $4\times10^{-4}$ here; about $10^{-8}$ for $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ at 160M parameters). Step sizes are not comparable across models. With bf16 weights, a $10^{-8}$ perturbation would round away, so it has to live in higher precision. The bf16 curve rounds only the two gradients.

§1 · Takeaway

$Hv$ costs a few gradients: exact by autodiff, or two gradients and a well-chosen $\epsilon$.

Everything that follows uses only this black box.

§2 · What products buy

You have a function that returns $Hv$. What can you compute with it?

Three answers in this section: a trace (cheap), a solve (expensive), the top eigenvalues (Lanczos). Start with: how would you estimate the trace of a matrix with $10^{18}$ entries from 10 products? Hint if needed: random vectors.

§2 · Hutchinson

A trace from ten products

$$\operatorname{tr}H=\mathbb E_v\big[v^\top Hv\big]\quad\text{for }\mathbb E[vv^\top]=I$$

Hutchinson: random $\pm1$ probes $v$, one $Hv$ each.

For i.i.d. $\pm1$ probes, $\operatorname{Var}(v^\top Hv)=2(\|H\|_F^2-\sum_jH_{jj}^2)$; Gaussian probes give $2\|H\|_F^2$. PyHessian (Yao et al. 2020) packages power iteration, Hutchinson and SLQ for PyTorch.

§2 · Solves

Solves are the expensive question

  • An IF needs one solve per query.
  • Iterative solvers can need thousands of $Hv$ per solve.
  • That cost is why DA2 uses EK-FAC for IFs.

Koh & Liang (2017) used conjugate gradients and LiSSA (Agarwal, Bullins & Hazan 2017). Grosse et al. (2023, §2.2.1): CG is full-batch; LiSSA "often" needs thousands of iterations, each at least a gradient. CG's count depends on conditioning and tolerance. EK-FAC came from optimization (George et al. 2018); Grosse et al. applied it to IFs.

Picked up in: DA2 stop 1.

§2 · Eigenvalues

How do you find the top eigenvalues from $Hv$ alone?

Most people say power iteration. Good start; it finds the largest-magnitude eigenpair, with error shrinking like $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k$, slowly when the top eigenvalues are close, as outliers often are.

§2 · Krylov

Repeated multiplication tilts $v$ toward the top

Keep all of $v, Hv, H^2v,\dots$: their span is the Krylov subspace.

2×2 example with eigenvalues 3 and 1: the angle to $u_1$ goes 60°, 30°, 11°. Power iteration keeps only the last vector; Lanczos keeps the whole span $\mathcal K_m=\operatorname{span}\{v,Hv,\dots,H^{m-1}v\}$ and gets much more out of the same $m$ products.

§2 · Lanczos

$m$ products compress $H$ to a tridiagonal matrix

$$T_m=Q_m^\top H Q_m=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1&\beta_1&\\ \beta_1&\alpha_2&\ddots\\ &\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}$$

$H$ restricted to the Krylov basis $Q_m$; its eigenvalues, the Ritz values $\theta_k$, approximate $H$'s.

$Q_m=[q_1,\dots,q_m]$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal K_m$, built by the three-term recurrence $Hq_k=\beta_{k-1}q_{k-1}+\alpha_kq_k+\beta_kq_{k+1}$ from $q_1=v$. Each new $Hq_k$ is orthogonalized against only the two previous vectors; symmetry makes the rest vanish in exact arithmetic. Cost: $m$ HVPs plus $O(mP)$ vector work.

§2 · A 4×4 example

A 4×4 example: watch the Ritz values

$$\begin{aligned} m=1:&\quad 0.55\\ \htmlClass{fragment}{m=2:}&\quad\htmlClass{fragment}{0.30,\ \ 4.82}\\ \htmlClass{fragment}{m=3:}&\quad\htmlClass{fragment}{0.23,\ \ 0.81,\ \ 5.00}\\ \htmlClass{fragment}{m=4:}&\quad\htmlClass{fragment}{0.2,\ \ 0.5,\ \ 1,\ \ 5} \end{aligned}$$

True eigenvalues $0.2,\ 0.5,\ 1,\ 5$: the extremes arrive first.

$H=R\,\mathrm{diag}(0.2,0.5,1,5)\,R^\top$ with a fixed random rotation $R$, $v=(1,1,1,1)/2$; numbers from da/checks/L2_checks.py. At $m=4=P$ Lanczos is exact.

Why outside-in: Ritz values are optimal over polynomials of degree $m-1$, and a polynomial can single out an isolated eigenvalue far more easily than one in a cluster. The Kaniel–Paige bounds (refined by Saad) make this precise with Chebyshev polynomials.

§2 · On a network

Outliers converge fast; the bulk is slow

Toy MLP: the five outliers are done by about $m=12$.

Dotted lines: the top five eigenvalues of the toy Hessian. This asymmetry is the whole story of what SLQ can and cannot see.

Code you have seen: TD 2.2 §2.5's top_eig_lanczos calls scipy's eigsh (implicitly restarted Lanczos) on double-backward HVPs.

The eigenpairs route to IFs: Schioppa et al. (2022) get top eigenpairs by Arnoldi (the non-symmetric version) and compute IFs in that subspace, up to a 300M-parameter ViT.

§2 · Floating point

Without reorthogonalization: ghosts

Lost orthogonality creates spurious Ritz values and copies of converged ones.

Same probe, no reorthogonalization: by $m=60$ there are four copies of $\lambda_{\max}$, and stray Ritz values between the outliers. The $q_k$ lose orthogonality once a Ritz value converges.

§2 · The memory wall

Reorthogonalizing needs every basis vector

$$\text{one vector}=P\ \text{numbers}:\quad P=10^{11}\ \text{in bf16}\ \Rightarrow\ 200\ \text{GB}$$

Keeping $m$ of them is out of reach at LLM scale, so $m$ stays small.

Full reorthogonalization also costs $O(m^2P)$ extra work. Without it, ghosts appear once Ritz values converge, which is why the 120B run uses $m=10$ (§5).

§2 · Takeaway

Lanczos finds the outliers in a few steps; the bulk stays a blur.

Hold on to "the bulk stays a blur": it is what the density estimate in §3 can and cannot fix.

§3 · The whole spectrum

Lanczos found the outliers. Can the same $T_m$ give the whole spectrum?

Yes, as a distribution, not eigenvalue by eigenvalue. Three steps.

§3 · Step 1

What a single probe sees

$$v^\top f(H)\,v=\sum_j\,(v\cdot u_j)^2\,f(\lambda_j)$$

Each eigenvalue, weighted by the probe's overlap with its eigenvector.

$(\lambda_j,u_j)$: eigenpairs of $H$; $v$ a unit probe. Expand $v$ in the eigenbasis and apply $f(H)$.

§3 · Step 2

That sum is an integral against sticks

$v^\top f(H)v=\int f\,d\mu_v$: a stick at each $\lambda_j$, height $(v\cdot u_j)^2$.

A small synthetic spectrum (twelve bulk eigenvalues, three outliers) and one random probe. The heights sum to 1 for a unit probe.

§3 · Step 3

Lanczos gives the Gauss rule for those sticks

$$e_1^\top f(T_m)\,e_1=\sum_{k=1}^m\tau_k^2\,f(\theta_k)$$

Nodes $\theta_k$: the Ritz values; weights $\tau_k^2$ from $T_m$'s eigenvectors; exact to degree $2m-1$.

Golub–Welsch: nodes are the Ritz values $\theta_k$; weights $\tau_k^2=(e_1^\top y_k)^2$, the squared first components of $T_m$'s eigenvectors $y_k$. Equivalently $e_1^\top T_m^ke_1=v^\top H^kv$ for $k\le2m-1$: $T_m$ matches $2m$ moments of $\mu_v$.

Reference: Golub & Meurant, Matrices, Moments and Quadrature; Wikipedia's Gaussian quadrature page. Physicists: this is the recursion method, with $\alpha_k,\beta_k$ the continued-fraction coefficients of a local density of states.

(Room for the instructor's framing.)

§3 · A tiny example

Three sticks, two nodes

Two nodes reproduce moments 0 to 3 of the three sticks exactly.

Atoms at 0, 1, 4 with weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2. $T_2$ gives nodes 0.249 and 3.790 with weights 0.760 and 0.240. Moments 0–3: 1, 1.1, 3.5, 13.1 for both; moment 4: 51.5 against 49.6. Checked in da/checks/L2_checks.py.

§3 · Stochastic Lanczos quadrature

Average the rules over a few random probes

$$\hat\rho(s)=\frac1{n_v}\sum_{\text{probes}}\ \sum_{k=1}^m\tau_k^2\,\delta(s-\theta_k)$$

Averaging $n_v$ random probes estimates $\rho$; finite $m$ adds quadrature error.

For $v=z/\sqrt P$ with i.i.d. $\pm1$ entries, $\mathbb E[(v\cdot u_j)^2]=1/P$, so $\mathbb E[\mu_v]=\rho$ exactly. Smooth with a Gaussian of width $\sigma$ to plot. Ubaru, Chen & Saad (2017).

Two error sources: quadrature ($m$) and Monte Carlo ($n_v$). Moments up to degree $2m-1$ are exact in expectation; their error is all Monte Carlo. In the toy, moments 1, 2, 4 are off by $+1.7\%$, $-2.3\%$, $-5.4\%$ at every $m$ from 10 to 90 (same probes).

For probes uniform on the sphere, the number of probes in Chen, Trogdon & Ubaru's (2021) Wasserstein bound falls roughly like $\log P/P$ (never below one): huge models need few probes. Ghorbani et al. (2019) used $m=90$ with Gaussian probes; Granziol & Juarev $m=10$ at 120B. Code: google/spectral-density, PyHessian.

§3 · What SLQ sees

Ten steps place the outliers; the bulk needs more

Toy MLP: at $m=10$ the outliers are right and the bulk is lumpy.

Ten probes. With $m=30$ or $90$ the SLQ curve lies on the exact one (not shown). Fraction of eigenvalues with $|s|<\sigma$: 0.891 exactly, 0.890 by SLQ at $m=90$, 0.78 at $m=10$. The mass near zero, where an IF's damping acts, is where the rule is coarsest.

The mean eigenvalue has no quadrature error and is unbiased, but check its spread across probes: for a spectrum dominated by a few outliers, ten probes can leave tens of percent.

Picked up in: DA1 §6 (filters act eigenvalue by eigenvalue).

§3 · Takeaway

A few probes times a few Lanczos steps give a spectral density: sharp outliers, blurry bulk.

SLQ tells you how much mass sits near zero, not its fine structure.

§4 · Classifiers

A bulk and a few outliers

  • A bulk of eigenvalues near zero.
  • About $C$ large outliers, $C$ the number of classes.
  • The outliers come from class structure in $G$.

Sagun et al. (2017): on $k$-cluster data the number of outliers above the gap equals the number of classes. Ghorbani et al. (2019): SLQ up to ImageNet (ResNet-18, Inception-V3 with 27M parameters); without BatchNorm, outliers appear fast and the gradient concentrates in their span; with it, both almost vanish; large negative eigenvalues early in training vanish within 1% of steps. Papyan (2018, JMLR 2020): about $C$ outliers from class means in $G$, a weaker $C(C-1)$ layer below; the bulk from within-class variation and $H-G$.

Papers report $C$, $C-1$ or about $C^2$, depending on what they count. Our toy ($C=5$), relative to the largest: 1, 0.97, 0.68, 0.42, 0.35, then 0.23.

Picked up in: TD 2.6 §6.3 and §6.4.

§4 · Language models

For a language model, $C$ is the vocabulary

$$C=|\text{vocabulary}|\approx10^4\text{–}10^5$$

"A few outliers" may be tens of thousands; $m=10$ cannot resolve them.

Transformers are also uneven by block: Zhang et al. (NeurIPS 2024) ran SLQ per parameter block and found very different spectra across blocks in transformers, not in CNNs (a Claude-suggested extra). The LLM spectra in §5 are among the first direct looks.

§4 · Takeaway

Classifiers: a bulk plus about $C$ outliers. Language models: still an open case.

Which is why the 120B measurement is interesting.

§5 · Granziol & Juarev (2026)

How do you get $Hv$ from a 120B model sharded across GPUs?

Under FSDP (ZeRO-3) each GPU stores a slice of every parameter tensor. The authors' diagnosis: naive HVPs force expensive all-gathers. arXiv 2602.00816.

§5 · The move

Finite differences, one shard at a time

Two ordinary gradient passes: a Lanczos step costs about two training steps.

§3.1, Algorithm 1. The only extra communication is scalar all-reduces for norms and dot products; $T_{Hv}\approx2T_{\rm grad}$. The gain is compatibility with the training stack, not FLOPs.

Cost (§5.1): Qwen-0.6B on one node, 1 → 8 GPUs gives 5.74× per HVP (Table 3); Lanczos bookkeeping adds 1.3–1.8% (Table 2); on 32B, Table 5 reports about 4× over HessFormer (89.6 s at batch 32 against 22.2 s at batch 80). HessFormer (Granziol 2025), the previous record: Pearlmutter HVPs on one 8-GPU node, SLQ on a 70B DeepSeek distilled model.

Reading the paper: "exact" means the true $H$, not a structured approximation; Figs 2–8 plot Ritz values against weights ("Gamma", "Ritz weights"), our $\theta_k$ and $\tau_k^2$; "smoothing parameter $\epsilon$" is the finite-difference step; their $\theta$ is the parameters and Pearlmutter's method is called "reverse-over-forward"; their loss is a dataset average; §4's Thm 4.1 assumes unbiased, independent finite-difference errors, while Thm 3.1 gives an $O(\epsilon^2)$ bias, so read §4 as heuristic.

§5 · Finding 1

The step size can fake a spectrum

  • $\epsilon=10^{-5}$ or $10^{-2}$: no separated outliers.
  • $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ or $10^{-3}$: clear outliers.
  • Which is right? Only an exact-$Hv$ check can say.

Pythia-160M, §7.1, Figs 3–6; the bad ones "resemble a random matrix". The authors attribute the failures to round-off and to averaging along the segment (Thm 3.1). But the "good" $\epsilon$ is the one that shows the expected outliers, and the check from slide 14 (finite difference against an exact HVP on a probe) is not reported, though it would be cheap at 160M.

Their Remark 3.2 puts fp32 $\epsilon^*$ at $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$, yet their sweet spot is $10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$: constants and $\sqrt P$ move the optimum. fp32/bf16 labels in their figures refer to Lanczos-vector storage; bf16 storage is enough. 30× more data leaves the spectrum unchanged at fixed $\epsilon$ (Fig 7). They report Pythia-1.4B more stable in $\epsilon$ (Fig 8: $3\times10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$): one pair of models.

§5 · Finding 2

A 120B model shows large negative curvature

  • gpt-oss-120b: ten Lanczos steps, no $\epsilon$ sweep.
  • Real curvature, or an artefact of $\epsilon$ or Lanczos?
  • The authors call it "only indicative".

§7, Fig 2: wikitext-2, sequence length 64, $\epsilon=10^{-4}$; the authors call it "only indicative". Why 10: HessFormer's paper says stay below 20 iterations without reorthogonalization, and often uses 10; reorthogonalizing would need 240 GB per bf16 basis vector at 120B.

Three readings, side by side. The authors': the data, as tokenized, has not been memorised. Another: a pretrained checkpoint need not be near a minimum of this particular loss. A numerical one: no $\epsilon$ sweep, 10 iterations, no reorthogonalization. Negative eigenvalues live in $H-G$ only, since $G\succeq0$.

No probe count, hardware or code is given for this run; HessFormer's code is public.

§5 · Finding 3

Per-layer (block-diagonal) curvature can be wrong by $O(1)$

$$\frac{\|(Hv)_b-H_{bb}v_b\|}{\|(Hv)_b\|}\ \approx\ 1$$

$b$: one transformer layer. Dropping cross-layer blocks loses about as much as it keeps.

Per transformer layer $b$, unit Gaussian $v$, true Hessian (§6). Qwen-0.6B, 28 layers: $0.94\pm0.06$, cosine $0.31\pm0.12$ (Table 6). DeepSeek-Coder 1.3B after fine-tuning, 24 layers: $1.21\pm0.15$, cosine $0.00\pm0.13$ (Table 7). Predicting zero gives exactly 1. For DeepSeek-Coder they report a higher final-block cosine (§6.2).

Caveat that changes a conclusion: this is $H$ with random probes; EK-FAC approximates $G$, and an IF applies $(G+\lambda I)^{-1}$ to gradients, which may live elsewhere. A warning, not a verdict. Other direction: EK-FAC's blocks are single weight matrices, finer than a layer, so the test is if anything generous to the block assumption.

At 120B the primitive still works; its knobs still need checking.

§6 · Back to attribution

What does the spectrum tell you about influence?

Attribution wants the spectrum of $G$, not $H$. $Gv$ is the same primitive (slide 11), so everything applies; the 2026 paper measured $H$.

§6 · The IF in G's eigenbasis

In $G$'s eigenbasis $(s_k,u_k)$, each direction gets its own weight

$$\mathcal I_i=-\sum_k\frac{(u_k\cdot\nabla\phi)\,(u_k\cdot\nabla L_i)}{s_k+\lambda}$$

Far below $\lambda$: a scaled gradient dot product. Near or above: curvature-weighted.

$(s_k,u_k)$: eigenpairs of $G$, as in DA1 §6. Directions with $s_k\ll\lambda$ contribute $-\frac1\lambda(u_k\cdot\nabla\phi)(u_k\cdot\nabla L_i)$: the TracIn-like part. At $s_k=0.9\lambda$, replacing the weight by $1/\lambda$ is already a 90% error.

Which directions matter for a given pair depends on both projections and on the weights; the density gives neither projection. TD 2.6 §6.4 finds the gradient concentrated in the top Hessian subspace, where the weights are smallest, so concentration alone does not say which part dominates.

§6 · Where λ sits

Where $\lambda$ sits decides which directions count as curvature

Left of $\lambda$: a scaled gradient dot product. Right of it: curvature-weighted.

Toy $G$ (mean loss), log axis: the bulk spans about seven decades. Grosse et al. set $\lambda=0.1\times$ the mean EK-FAC eigenvalue, layer by layer; on the toy's exact $G$ that puts 78% of eigenvalues below $\lambda$. A $\lambda$ between bulk and outliers puts 99.7% below.

At scale both are computable: SLQ sees the outliers well, and the mean $\operatorname{tr}G/P$ comes from Hutchinson on $Gv$ (check its spread). Where $0.1\times$mean falls inside the bulk is what SLQ resolves least well. DA2's Code 1 sweeps $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}$ times the mean eigenvalue; DA1 §5's exercise uses the median Hessian eigenvalue.

$\lambda$ also has a meaning: DA1 §4's proximal penalty. Choosing it from the spectrum is a statement about where you trust the quadratic model; choosing it from the proximal target is a statement about the question.

§6 · Auditing

Check the approximation, not just the answer

  • EK-FAC assumes block-diagonal curvature.
  • Dropping cross-layer blocks already costs $O(1)$ in $Hv$.
  • Check against exact products: $Gv$ for EK-FAC, $Hv$ for Hessians.

EK-FAC: blocks plus a Kronecker-factored eigenbasis with refitted eigenvalues. DA2 Code 1 measures the IF-level error on a 147-parameter net: rank correlation with the exact damped GN IF is 0.65–0.73 for block-diagonal $G$ and 0.26–0.45 for K-FAC and EK-FAC, across $\lambda$ from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}$ times the mean eigenvalue. So the Kronecker step costs more than the block step there. Both are measurements on specific models, not theorems.

The spectrum shows what your damping and your approximation are doing.

Summary

Three things to keep

  • $v\mapsto Hv$ costs a few gradients.
  • Lanczos and SLQ: outliers fast, a density from few probes.
  • Check step sizes and approximations against exact products.

Bulk plus outliers is the picture to remember for classifiers; for language models it is open.
