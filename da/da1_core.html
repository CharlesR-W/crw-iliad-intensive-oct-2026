1. The question

“Which training examples caused this behaviour?” This section treats that as causal analysis of a training run and shows two ways the simplest counterfactual answer goes wrong. It sets up why later sections are careful to say which question a method answers.

Missed causes and ambiguous effects: counterfactuals pose challenges for interpreting neural networks, Mueller (2024) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●○○○

Written about interpreting forward passes, not training data, but its two failure modes are the ones below: redundant causes are missed (§4) and counterfactual dependence does not chain (§3).

Data Shapley: equitable valuation of data for machine learning, Ghorbani and Zou (ICML 2019) Importance ●●○○○ Length ●●○○○

Shapley values for training points, made computable by sampling permutations with early truncation, or by a one-pass gradient approximation (§3).

A training run as a causal system. Mechanistic interpretability analyses a forward pass: inputs flow through layers to an output, and one asks which components caused it. Data attribution does the same for a training run: data flow through optimizer steps to the final weights, and one asks which examples caused some behaviour of the final model. Two differences matter. A forward pass costs milliseconds, so interpretability can test a causal claim by ablating many times. Retraining costs as much as training, so data attribution can rarely afford even one ablation, and most of its methods are ways to predict retraining without doing it. And the conceptual difficulties of causal reasoning come along unchanged.

Counterfactual attribution. The simplest test of causation is counterfactual dependence, the starting point of Lewis (1973): A caused B if, had A not occurred, B would not have occurred. For training data: example i caused a behaviour if removing i and retraining would change it. This is the leave-one-out (LOO) counterfactual; its graded version asks how much the behaviour changes. It fails in two standard ways.

Overdetermination. A bakery buys flour, butter, yeast and sugar, each from a standard or a premium supplier. All-premium croissants come out exceptionally fluffy. Unknown to the bakery, there are two independent mechanisms for that fluffiness: premium butter makes more steam, premium yeast makes more gas, and either alone is enough. Downgrading only the butter changes nothing; nor does downgrading only the yeast. LOO concludes that neither caused the fluffiness, though both did. The training analogue: a behaviour that many examples each suffice to teach survives the removal of any one of them.

Non-transitivity. A boulder rolls toward a hiker (A); she sees it and ducks (B); she survives (C). Had the boulder not rolled she would not have ducked, so B depends on A. Had she not ducked she would have died, so C depends on B. But C does not depend on A: with or without the boulder, she survives. Dependence does not chain. (The example is Ned Hall’s, as told by Hitchcock 2001.) The training analogue: an example can teach an intermediate feature that a capability then relies on, without the example passing an end-to-end LOO test for the capability.

Shapley values. One response to overdetermination is to average an example’s effect over all the training subsets it could join. Treat the n examples as players, and let v(S) be the behaviour of a model trained on the subset S\subseteq[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}. The Shapley value of example i is

\varphi_i=\frac1n\sum_{S\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}}\binom{n-1}{|S|}^{-1}\big[v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)\big],

the marginal effect of adding i, averaged over subsets of each size and then over the sizes. In the bakery, v(S)=1 if S (the premium ingredients) contains butter or yeast and 0 otherwise. LOO gives every ingredient 0; Shapley gives butter and yeast \tfrac12 each, and flour and sugar 0. Three things to keep in mind:

  • Shapley values attribute over finite coalitions. They are not the derivative at the full training set that the rest of this notebook computes.
  • Exact evaluation needs v on exponentially many subsets, each a retraining. Practical estimators sample subsets or permutations instead.
  • They still split credit between sufficient causes (half each), where one could argue each deserves all of it.

The rest of the day works with something more local and much cheaper than either LOO or Shapley: the derivative of the behaviour with respect to how heavily one example is weighted.

2. Targets and conventions

Before any formula, fix what is being estimated. This section sets the notation used all day, DA2 included, and separates three questions that all get called “the influence of example i”.

Setup. A training set \{z_i\}_{i=1}^n with z_i=(x_i,y_i); model parameters w\in\mathbb R^p; per-example losses L_i(w). Give example i a weight \beta_i and train on the summed weighted loss

L(w;\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\beta_i\,L_i(w).

\beta=\mathbf 1 is the actual training run. The behaviour to attribute is a scalar observable \phi(w) of the trained parameters: the loss or a logit on a test input, the average loss on a task, and so on. We also call \phi (or its test input) the query.

What “the trained parameters” are is a modelling choice. Write \pi(\beta) for it. The object of study is the composite

\beta\;\xrightarrow{\ \pi\ }\;\pi(\beta)\;\xrightarrow{\ \phi\ }\;\phi\big(\pi(\beta)\big),

and the methods of the day differ mainly in what they take \pi to be (table below).

Conventions (used everywhere on the day):

  1. The influence of example i on \phi is the upweighting derivative \mathcal I_i=\frac{\partial\,\phi(\pi(\beta))}{\partial\beta_i}\Big|_{\beta=\mathbf 1}.
  2. Deletion of i means \beta_i:1\to0. To first order it changes \phi by -\mathcal I_i.
  3. Sign. If \phi is a test loss, an example that helps (upweighting it lowers the loss) has negative influence. Koh and Liang’s \mathcal I_{\rm up,loss} has this sign; TracIn, TRAK and SOURCE (methods met in §5 and DA2) report helpful examples as positive.
  4. Koh and Liang’s \mathcal I_{\rm up} is n times ours (Extension below).
  5. Normalization. For SGD written on a mean loss we show the 1/n, or the minibatch’s 1/b, explicitly: w_{t+1}=w_t-\frac{\eta}{b}\sum_{i\in B_t}\beta_i\nabla L_i(w_t), with step size \eta and minibatch B_t of size b. Curvatures and damping constants (§4) are in summed-loss units: the Hessian of L(w;\mathbf 1) is n times that of the mean loss.

Three targets. A derivative and a finite change are different questions.

  • Local derivative: \mathcal I_i, the effect of an infinitesimal reweighting of i.
  • Leave-one-out: \phi(\pi(\mathbf 1-e_i))-\phi(\pi(\mathbf 1)), removing i entirely (e_i is the i-th unit vector). It equals -\mathcal I_i only to first order.
  • Subset deletion: \phi(\pi(\mathbf 1_S)) for a large random subset S (half the data, say), where \mathbf 1_S keeps the examples in S and deletes the rest. Its first-order prediction is \phi(\pi(\mathbf 1))-\sum_{j\notin S}\mathcal I_j.

A method’s target is the question it is defined to answer: a choice of \pi and of one of these three. Its estimator is how it computes the answer. §5 shows that the two need separate checks.

Extension: converting to Koh and Liang’s convention

Why is the influence the same for a summed and a mean loss, and how does Koh and Liang’s \varepsilon relate to \beta? Notation as above.

Exercise 2.1 — Converting conventions. Let \pi(\beta) be an argmin of L(\cdot\,;\beta) (assume it is unique and smooth in \beta). Koh and Liang instead upweight by \varepsilon on the mean loss: \hat\theta_\varepsilon=\arg\min_w\frac1n\sum_jL_j(w)+\varepsilon L_i(w), with \mathcal I_{\rm up}=d\hat\theta_\varepsilon/d\varepsilon at \varepsilon=0.

  1. Show that \mathcal I_i is unchanged if L(w;\beta) is replaced by the mean \frac1n\sum_j\beta_jL_j(w).
  2. Show that their parameter response is \mathcal I_{\rm up}=n\,\partial\pi/\partial\beta_i, and that their removal, \varepsilon=-1/n, gives the same first-order prediction of the parameter change as our \beta_i:1\to0.
Solution
  1. For any c>0, cL(\cdot\,;\beta) and L(\cdot\,;\beta) have the same minimizer. So \pi(\beta) is the same function of \beta for the summed and the mean objective, and so is its derivative. What the normalization does change is the units of curvature and damping (the Hessian scales by 1/n).
  2. \frac1n\sum_jL_j+\varepsilon L_i=\frac1n\sum_j\beta_jL_j with \beta=\mathbf 1+n\varepsilon e_i. So \hat\theta_\varepsilon=\pi(\mathbf 1+n\varepsilon e_i) and, by the chain rule, d\hat\theta_\varepsilon/d\varepsilon=n\,\partial\pi/\partial\beta_i. Removal at \varepsilon=-1/n is \beta_i=0. Their first-order prediction of the parameter change is -\tfrac1n\mathcal I_{\rm up}=-\partial\pi/\partial\beta_i, the same as ours. Koh and Liang’s \varepsilon simply measures the perturbation in units of 1/n.

3. The classical influence function

How does the trained model move if one example is reweighted? The implicit function theorem turns this into one linear solve, -H^{-1}\nabla L_i. In linear regression we can compare it with exact deletion; its accuracy is set by leverage. (The derivation is Koh and Liang §2.)

Influence on a mean. Robust statistics asked this question first (Hampel 1974). Move a fraction \varepsilon of a distribution’s mass to a point z. The mean \mu becomes (1-\varepsilon)\mu+\varepsilon z, whose derivative at \varepsilon=0 is z-\mu. A point’s influence on the mean is its distance from the mean.

The derivation. Notation: L(w;\beta)=\sum_j\beta_jL_j(w); the trained parameters w^* satisfy \nabla_wL(w^*;\mathbf 1)=0; H=\nabla_w^2L(w^*;\mathbf 1) is the Hessian; g_i=\nabla L_i(w^*) is example i’s gradient and g_\phi=\nabla\phi(w^*) the observable’s.

Suppose H is invertible. The implicit function theorem, applied to the stationarity equation \nabla_wL(w;\beta)=0, then gives a unique smooth branch w^*(\beta) of stationary points through w^*, for \beta near \mathbf 1. Differentiate the identity \nabla_wL(w^*(\beta);\beta)=0 with respect to \beta_i. The w-dependence gives H\,\partial w^*/\partial\beta_i, and the explicit \beta_i-dependence gives \nabla L_i(w^*)=g_i. So

H\,\frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\beta_i}+g_i=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial w^*}{\partial\beta_i}\Big|_{\beta=\mathbf 1}=-H^{-1}g_i, \qquad \boxed{\;\mathcal I_i=-\,g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i\;}

by the chain rule. The theorem gives only a local branch of stationary points. Calling it “the retrained model” also needs w^* to be a strict minimum (H positive definite) and retraining to land on this branch rather than in another basin. Both hold automatically for a strictly convex loss.

Three factors. Read -g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i from right to left:

  • g_i is how example i pulls on the parameters (its descent direction is -g_i);
  • H^{-1} turns that pull into a displacement: flat directions (small eigenvalues of H) move a lot, stiff directions little;
  • g_\phi projects the displacement onto what the observable cares about.

Deleting i changes \phi by about -\mathcal I_i=g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i.

Exercise 3.1 — Linear regression: influence versus exact deletion. Take inputs x_i\in\mathbb R^p, targets y_i\in\mathbb R, losses L_i(w)=\frac12(y_i-w^\top x_i)^2, and A=\sum_ix_ix_i^\top, assumed invertible. Write r_j=y_j-x_j^\top w^* for the residual of example j and h_j=x_j^\top A^{-1}x_j for its leverage.

  1. Show that w^*=A^{-1}\sum_ix_iy_i and H=A.
  2. Show that \partial w^*/\partial\beta_j=A^{-1}x_jr_j, so the influence function predicts that deleting j moves the parameters by -A^{-1}x_jr_j.
  3. Show that exact deletion gives w^*_{(-j)}-w^*=-\frac{A^{-1}x_j\,r_j}{1-h_j}, provided h_j<1.
  4. Leverages satisfy 0\le h_j\le1 and \sum_jh_j=p (proof in the solution). What goes wrong when h_j=1?
  5. Exact deletion is the influence-function prediction times 1/(1-h_j). When is the prediction accurate, and when does it fail badly?
Hint

For 3, write w^*_{(-j)}=w^*+\delta and subtract the full normal equations Aw^*=\sum_ix_iy_i from the ones without j. You should find A\delta=x_j\,c for a scalar c; then find c by taking the inner product with A^{-1}x_j. For 4, look at (A-x_jx_j^\top)A^{-1}x_j.

Solution

  1. \nabla_wL(w;\mathbf 1)=-\sum_ix_i(y_i-x_i^\top w)=Aw-\sum_ix_iy_i. Setting it to zero gives w^*=A^{-1}\sum_ix_iy_i. Differentiating once more, H=A.

  2. g_j=\nabla L_j(w^*)=-x_j(y_j-x_j^\top w^*)=-x_jr_j, so \partial w^*/\partial\beta_j=-H^{-1}g_j=A^{-1}x_jr_j. Upweighting j moves the fit toward j: along A^{-1}x_j, in proportion to the residual. Deletion is predicted to move it by -A^{-1}x_jr_j.

  3. Without example j the normal equations are (A-x_jx_j^\top)\,w_{(-j)}=\sum_ix_iy_i-x_jy_j. Put w_{(-j)}=w^*+\delta and use Aw^*=\sum_ix_iy_i: A\delta-x_jx_j^\top w^*-x_jx_j^\top\delta=-x_jy_j \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\delta=x_j\big(x_j^\top\delta-r_j\big)=:x_j\,c . So \delta=A^{-1}x_j\,c. Then x_j^\top\delta=h_jc, and the definition of c gives c=h_jc-r_j, that is, c=-r_j/(1-h_j). Hence w^*_{(-j)}-w^*=-\frac{A^{-1}x_jr_j}{1-h_j}.

  4. The bounds. Let X stack the x_i^\top as rows. The “hat matrix” P=XA^{-1}X^\top is symmetric and P^2=XA^{-1}(X^\top X)A^{-1}X^\top=P, so it is an orthogonal projection. Its diagonal entries satisfy P_{jj}=\sum_kP_{jk}^2\ge P_{jj}^2, so 0\le h_j=P_{jj}\le1. Its trace is its rank: \sum_jh_j=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}X^\top X)=p. So the average leverage is p/n.

    At h_j=1: (A-x_jx_j^\top)A^{-1}x_j=x_j(1-h_j)=0, so the design without j is singular. Some direction in parameter space is pinned down by example j alone, and deleting j leaves it undetermined. The deletion formula requires h_j<1.

  5. The influence function keeps only the leading term of 1/(1-h_j)=1+h_j+h_j^2+\cdots.

    • h_j\to0 (a typical point, when p\ll n): the correction is O(h_j) and the prediction is essentially exact.
    • h_j\to1 (a point alone in some direction of input space): the true deletion effect is arbitrarily larger than predicted.

    The influence function is least reliable for exactly the points one most wants to ask about: those the fit could not reproduce without them. This happens in a strictly convex, fully converged model; nothing here is about neural networks.

Leverage as a distance. With \hat\Sigma=A/n, the inputs’ uncentered second-moment matrix, h_j=\frac1n\,x_j^\top\hat\Sigma^{-1}x_j: a squared length of x_j in the metric set by the data. Directions the data sample rarely have small eigenvalues of \hat\Sigma, so inputs pointing that way have large leverage. If the inputs include a constant feature (an intercept), h_j is \frac1n plus \frac1n times the squared Mahalanobis distance of the other features from their mean.

Extension: the whole response curve

What does the trained model do for every value of one example’s weight, not just its derivative at \beta_j=1? In linear regression this has a closed form, and expanding it shows exactly which terms the influence function drops.

Notation as in Exercise 3.1: A=\sum_ix_ix_i^\top, residual r_j, leverage h_j=x_j^\top A^{-1}x_j. Let w^*(\beta_j) minimize \sum_{i\ne j}L_i(w)+\beta_jL_j(w), with the other weights held at 1.

Exercise 3.2 — Sherman–Morrison and the geometric series.

  1. Using the Sherman–Morrison formula (M+uv^\top)^{-1}=M^{-1}-\dfrac{M^{-1}uv^\top M^{-1}}{1+v^\top M^{-1}u}, show that w^*(\beta_j)-w^*=-\frac{(1-\beta_j)\,A^{-1}x_jr_j}{1-(1-\beta_j)\,h_j}.
  2. Expand in powers of (1-\beta_j): w^*(\beta_j)-w^*=-A^{-1}x_jr_j\sum_{k\ge1}(1-\beta_j)^k\,h_j^{\,k-1}. Identify the k=1 term with the influence function, and say what suppresses the others.
  3. At \beta_j=0, which suppression is left?
Solution
  1. The normal equations are \big(A-(1-\beta_j)x_jx_j^\top\big)w=\sum_ix_iy_i-(1-\beta_j)x_jy_j. Sherman–Morrison with u=-(1-\beta_j)x_j, v=x_j gives, with q_j=A^{-1}x_j, \big(A-(1-\beta_j)x_jx_j^\top\big)^{-1}=A^{-1}+\frac{(1-\beta_j)\,q_jq_j^\top}{1-(1-\beta_j)h_j}. Apply this to the right-hand side, using A^{-1}\sum_ix_iy_i=w^* and q_j^\top\sum_ix_iy_i=x_j^\top w^*. Write t=1-\beta_j and d_j=1-t\,h_j. Then w^*(\beta_j)=w^*-t\,q_jy_j+\frac{t\,q_j\big(x_j^\top w^*-t\,h_jy_j\big)}{d_j} =w^*+\frac{t\,q_j}{d_j}\Big[-y_j(1-t\,h_j)+x_j^\top w^*-t\,h_jy_j\Big] =w^*-\frac{t\,q_jr_j}{d_j}. The response is a rational function of \beta_j because the matrix being inverted is linear in \beta_j.
  2. For |(1-\beta_j)h_j|<1, \dfrac{1}{1-(1-\beta_j)h_j}=\sum_{k\ge0}(1-\beta_j)^kh_j^k. Multiplying by (1-\beta_j) gives the series.
    • The k=1 term is -(1-\beta_j)A^{-1}x_jr_j=(\beta_j-1)\,\partial w^*/\partial\beta_j: the influence-function prediction, as it must be, since the IF is the linear coefficient.
    • The k-th term carries (1-\beta_j)^k h_j^{k-1}: it is suppressed both by the size of the perturbation and by the leverage.
  3. At \beta_j=0 the perturbation factor is 1, and only the powers of h_j remain. The series becomes 1+h_j+h_j^2+\cdots=1/(1-h_j), recovering Exercise 3.1. The gap between the influence function and LOO is the sum of all the higher-order Taylor terms, which linear regression happens to sum in closed form. For other models the higher-order terms are still there, with no closed form.

Extension: what the implicit function theorem needs

Which assumptions does the derivation of -H^{-1}g_i actually use, and which are only needed to call the result “retraining”? Notation: L(w;\beta)=\sum_j\beta_jL_j(w), w^* a stationary point of L(\cdot\,;\mathbf 1), H its Hessian there, g_i=\nabla L_i(w^*).

The implicit function theorem: if F(w,\beta) is C^1, F(w^*,\mathbf 1)=0 and \partial F/\partial w is invertible at (w^*,\mathbf 1), then near \beta=\mathbf 1 there is a unique C^1 map \beta\mapsto w(\beta) near w^* with F(w(\beta),\beta)=0.

Exercise 3.3 — Local branch versus argmin.

  1. Apply the theorem to F(w,\beta)=\nabla_wL(w;\beta). Which hypothesis is the invertibility of H?
  2. Is “w^* is the unique global minimum” needed for the derivative -H^{-1}g_i to exist? What is it needed for?
  3. Take one parameter w\in\mathbb R and three examples: L_1(w)=(w^2-1)^2, L_2(w)=0.10\,w and L_3(w)=-0.11\,w. Where is the global minimum at \beta=\mathbf 1? What happens to the argmin if \beta_3 drops to 0.8? What does the influence function at the original minimum predict?
Solution
  1. \partial F/\partial w=\nabla_w^2L(w;\beta), which at (w^*,\mathbf 1) is H. Its invertibility is exactly the theorem’s main hypothesis. (F is C^1 if each L_j is C^2.)
  2. No. The theorem’s uniqueness is local: it gives the unique branch of stationary points near w^*, and -H^{-1}g_i is the derivative of that branch. Global minimality is not used. It is what you need to identify the branch with “the argmin of the reweighted loss” (together with H\succ0, so the branch consists of minima). Without it, the argmin can jump to another basin as \beta changes, and is then not even continuous in \beta.
  3. L(w;\mathbf 1)=(w^2-1)^2-0.01\,w has two wells, near w=\pm1; the tilt -0.01\,w makes the one near +1 slightly lower, so it is the global minimum. With \beta_3=0.8 the tilt is 0.10\,w-0.088\,w=+0.012\,w, and the global minimum jumps to the well near -1. The influence function at w^*\approx1 (where H\approx8) predicts a small shift within the right-hand well, \Delta w\approx-H^{-1}g_3\,\Delta\beta_3\approx-(-0.11/8)(-0.2)\approx-0.003, which is correct for the local branch. Retraining from a random initialization may land in either well; retraining from w^* stays on the branch.

4. What breaks for neural networks, and the standard fixes

§3 assumed the trained model sits at an isolated, strict minimum. A neural network checkpoint usually does not. Three standard modifications turn -g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i into the formula used in practice, -g_\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i, and each one changes the question being answered. Bae et al. §§1–4 (keystone, above) is the full argument; read it after the session if you want it.

Claude-suggested extras.

Influence functions in deep learning are fragile, Basu, Pope and Feizi (ICLR 2021) Importance ●●○○○ Length ●●○○○

Compares influence estimates with leave-one-out retraining across depth, width and weight decay: fairly accurate for shallow networks, often wrong for deep ones, and on ImageNet even the retraining ground truth is noisy (abstract, §§4–5).

Notation: L(w;\beta)=\sum_j\beta_jL_j(w); a checkpoint w^s, which need not be stationary (when it is, we write w^*); at w^s, the Hessian H=\nabla^2_wL(w^s;\mathbf 1), the gradients g_i=\nabla L_i(w^s) and g_\phi=\nabla\phi(w^s).

1. Degenerate minima: damping. Networks have continuous families of minimizers, from rescaling symmetries, unused units, or simply more parameters than constraints. Along such a family the loss is flat, so H has zero eigenvalues and H^{-1} does not exist. The standard repair replaces H^{-1} by (H+\lambda I)^{-1} with \lambda>0. At a stationary point w^* this is not just a numerical patch: -g_\phi^\top(H+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i is the exact influence for the proximal objective

L(w;\beta)+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|w-w^*\|^2 ,

whose Hessian at w^* is H+\lambda I. The proximal term pulls the response toward w^*, and at finite \lambda it shrinks the response. If g_i has no component along the flat directions (it lies in the range of H), the response has a finite limit as \lambda\to0, the pseudoinverse answer -H^+g_i. If it does have such a component, the response along it grows like 1/\lambda. Degenerate minima are a main subject of the SLT day, which also uses the a\cdot b model below.

2. Negative curvature: Gauss–Newton. Away from a minimum, H can have negative eigenvalues. Then H+\lambda I is singular for some \lambda>0, and nearby values give huge responses of either sign. The standard fix is the Gauss–Newton matrix. Write f_i(w)\in\mathbb R^C for the network’s outputs on example i, \ell_i(f_i) for the loss as a function of them, J_i=\partial f_i/\partial w (C\times p) and H_{y,i}=\nabla_f^2\ell_i (C\times C). Differentiating L(w;\mathbf 1)=\sum_i\ell_i(f_i(w)) twice,

H=\underbrace{\sum_iJ_i^\top H_{y,i}\,J_i}_{G}\;+\;\sum_i\sum_{c=1}^C\frac{\partial\ell_i}{\partial f_{ic}}\,\nabla_w^2f_{ic}.

G is positive semidefinite whenever each \ell_i is convex in the outputs (squared error, softmax cross-entropy), so G+\lambda I is positive definite for every \lambda>0. G drops the second term. That term vanishes when the output gradients \partial\ell_i/\partial f_i do (squared error at interpolation), or when the model is linear in its parameters. It need not vanish at a minimum: with one parameter, f(w)=w^2, target -1 and squared error, L=\frac12(w^2+1)^2 has a strict minimum at w=0 where H=2 but G=0. So even at a converged minimum, the Gauss–Newton influence need not equal the Hessian influence. TD §6.1 has more on the decomposition.

3. Non-convergence: the PBRF. Checkpoints are rarely stationary, so the implicit-function derivation has nothing to start from. Bae et al. show that the formula people compute at a checkpoint w^s is nonetheless the exact answer to a different question. The proximal Bregman response function (PBRF) is the minimizer of

Q(w;\beta)=\sum_{j=1}^nD_j\big(f_j(w),f_j(w^s)\big)+\sum_{j=1}^n(\beta_j-1)\,L_j(w)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w-w^s\|^2 .

Here D_j(f,f^s)=\ell_j(f)-\ell_j(f^s)-\nabla\ell_j(f^s)^\top(f-f^s) is the Bregman divergence of the output loss: zero at f=f^s and growing as the outputs move away (for squared error, \frac12\|f-f^s\|^2). At \beta=\mathbf 1 only the first and last terms remain, and both are minimized at w^s. So the checkpoint is the optimum by construction, whether or not training converged. As a function of f, D_j has zero gradient and Hessian H_{y,j} at f^s, so its Hessian in w at w^s is J_j^\top H_{y,j}J_j. The Hessian of Q at w^s is therefore G+\lambda I, and the implicit function theorem gives

\frac{\partial\,\phi(\text{PBRF}(\beta))}{\partial\beta_i}\Big|_{\beta=\mathbf 1}=-\,g_\phi^\top(G+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i .

This is the damped Gauss–Newton influence function, the formula DA2 stop 1 computes at scale. In words: how would \phi change if i were reweighted, while moving the outputs on the training set and moving the parameters were both penalized? In practice one solves (G+\lambda I)u=g_\phi once per query and takes -u^\top g_i for every training example; DA2 stop 1 is about doing that solve at the scale of a language model.

This is not a repaired estimate of LOO retraining. Bae et al. split the gap between the influence function and LOO retraining into five parts:

  • warm-start gap: the influence function describes retraining started from w^s, not from a fresh initialization;
  • proximity gap: moving the parameters is penalized;
  • non-convergence gap: w^s is not a minimum, so the Bregman term stands in for the loss;
  • linearization error: the first-order step;
  • solver error: the approximate linear solve.

The first three are changes of question, and for neural networks they dominate (their Fig. 4). The influence function answers the PBRF question accurately; that question just differs from retraining.

Code: parameterization dependence of the damped IF. Does the damped IF’s answer depend on which of many equivalent minimizers training reached? For an observable that changes along the valley of minimizers, yes, even as \lambda\to0.

The a\cdot b model has parameters w=(a,b) and predicts the product ab. Its losses are L_i=\frac12(z_i-ab)^2 for numbers z_1,\dots,z_n with mean \bar z\ne0, so the minimizers form the hyperbola ab=\bar z (the valley). Take two observables: \phi=ab, which is what the model computes and is constant along the valley, and \phi=a^2, which changes along it. At a minimizer (a^*,b^*), write v=(b^*,a^*), the direction across the valley. Then H=n\,vv^\top and g_i=-(z_i-\bar z)\,v (Exercise 4.1), so (H+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i=g_i/(n\|v\|^2+\lambda). Writing \mathcal I_\lambda(z_i,\phi)=-g_\phi^\top(H+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i for the damped influence of z_i on \phi,

\mathcal I_\lambda(z_i,ab)=\frac{(z_i-\bar z)\,\|v\|^2}{n\|v\|^2+\lambda}, \qquad \mathcal I_\lambda(z_i,a^2)=\frac{2a^*b^*\,(z_i-\bar z)}{n\|v\|^2+\lambda}.

Extension: the a\cdot b model by hand

Where does the plot’s formula come from, and how does the pseudoinverse compare with exact deletion? Setup as in the code: parameters w=(a,b), losses L_i(w)=\frac12(z_i-ab)^2 summed over i=1,\dots,n, \bar z=\frac1n\sum_iz_i\ne0, a minimizer (a^*,b^*) with a^*b^*=\bar z, and v=(b^*,a^*).

Exercise 4.1 — Influence in a degenerate model.

  1. Show that the Hessian at (a^*,b^*) is H=n\,vv^\top. Show that its null space is the tangent to the hyperbola of minimizers.
  2. For \phi=ab, compute the influence with the pseudoinverse H^+ (invert only the nonzero eigenvalue), and compare it with the exact LOO change in \phi.
  3. Compute the damped influence -g_\phi^\top(H+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i for \phi=ab and \phi=a^2. Describe the dependence on \lambda, on the minimizer, and the \lambda\to0 limit.
  4. Show that gradient flow on any loss of the form \sum_jc_j\ell(z_j,ab) conserves a^2-b^2. Use this to show that warm-started gradient-flow retraining has the same \lambda\to0 derivative of a^2 as in 3.
Solution
  1. \partial^2L_i/\partial a^2=b^2, \partial^2L_i/\partial b^2=a^2, \partial^2L_i/\partial a\partial b=2ab-z_i. Summing at a minimizer, the off-diagonal is \sum_i(2a^*b^*-z_i)=n(2\bar z-\bar z)=n\,a^*b^*, so H=n\begin{pmatrix}b^{*2}&a^*b^*\\a^*b^*&a^{*2}\end{pmatrix}=n\,vv^\top . It has rank one, with eigenvalue n\|v\|^2 on v and 0 on v_\perp=(a^*,-b^*). The hyperbola ab=\bar z has normal \nabla(ab)=(b,a)=v, so its tangent is v_\perp: the null space is the direction along the minimizers, generated by the rescaling (a,b)\mapsto(\alpha a,b/\alpha).
  2. g_i=\nabla L_i(w^*)=-(z_i-\bar z)\,v and g_\phi=\nabla(ab)=v. With H^+=vv^\top/(n\|v\|^4), \mathcal I=-v^\top H^+\big(-(z_i-\bar z)v\big)=\frac{z_i-\bar z}{n}, independent of the minimizer. Deleting z_i changes the mean to (n\bar z-z_i)/(n-1), and every new minimizer has ab equal to it, so the exact change is -(z_i-\bar z)/(n-1). The ratio of exact to predicted is n/(n-1)=1/(1-h) with h=1/n: each point has leverage 1/n in this one-effective-parameter model, as in Exercise 3.1.
  3. (H+\lambda I)^{-1}v=v/(n\|v\|^2+\lambda), because v is an eigenvector. So for any \phi, \mathcal I_\lambda(z_i,\phi)=\frac{(z_i-\bar z)\,g_\phi^\top v}{n\|v\|^2+\lambda}.
    • For \phi=ab, g_\phi^\top v=\|v\|^2. The result depends on the minimizer for \lambda>0, and tends to (z_i-\bar z)/n (the pseudoinverse answer) as \lambda\to0.
    • For \phi=a^2, g_\phi=(2a^*,0) and g_\phi^\top v=2a^*b^*. The limit is 2\bar z(z_i-\bar z)/\big(n(a^{*2}+b^{*2})\big): finite, but generally minimizer-dependent: for \bar z>0, compare the balanced minimizer a^*=b^*=\sqrt{\bar z} with (\bar z,1).
    • In both cases the dependence on \lambda is a plateau for \lambda\ll n\|v\|^2, then decay as 1/\lambda. Nothing diverges as \lambda\to0, because g_i has no component along the null direction.
  4. For a loss depending on u=ab, \dot a=-\partial_u\mathcal L\,b and \dot b=-\partial_u\mathcal L\,a, so \frac{d}{dt}(a^2-b^2)=2a\dot a-2b\dot b=0. Warm-started retraining therefore ends at the point of the new hyperbola ab=\bar z' with the same a^2-b^2=c. There a^2=\big(c+\sqrt{c^2+4u^2}\big)/2, so \frac{d(a^2)}{du}=\frac{2u}{\sqrt{c^2+4u^2}}=\frac{2ab}{a^2+b^2}. With du/d\beta_i=(z_i-\bar z)/n (from 2), this is 2a^*b^*(z_i-\bar z)/\big(n\|v\|^2\big), the \lambda\to0 limit of 3. Geometrically, gradient flow moves along \nabla u=v, which to first order is also the direction to the nearest point of the new hyperbola.

Extension: a proximity gap on a tiny MLP

How different are the proximal question and plain retraining for a real (tiny) network? Setup: a one-hidden-layer tanh network with 8 units (25 parameters), fit to n=20 noisy points of \sin 2x. It is trained to a minimum w^* by Newton’s method, with a tiny ridge term so that the minimum is isolated. Every retrained point is checked to be a minimum, not a saddle. The observable \phi is the squared error at a test input. For each training point i, the cell computes three deletion responses:

  • the damped-IF prediction -\mathcal I_i=g_\phi^\top(H+\lambda I)^{-1}g_i, with \lambda at 1% of the top Hessian eigenvalue;
  • proximal retraining: minimize L(w;\mathbf 1-e_i)+\frac\lambda2\|w-w^*\|^2, the damped IF’s own target (made finite);
  • plain retraining: minimize L(w;\mathbf 1-e_i), started from w^*.

All three are evaluated at the same deletion, so the difference between the last two is the proximity gap. This needs torch; it takes about 25 s.

Code (90 lines) 
import torch
from torch.func import grad, hessian, jacrev, vmap

torch.set_default_dtype(torch.float64)
torch.manual_seed(0)
t0 = time.time()
n_mlp, width = 20, 8
Xt = torch.linspace(-2, 2, n_mlp)[:, None]
Yt = torch.sin(2 * Xt[:, 0]) + 0.2 * torch.randn(n_mlp)
xq, yq = torch.tensor([[0.5]]), torch.sin(torch.tensor([1.0]))
shapes = [(width, 1), (width,), (1, width), (1,)]
sizes = [int(np.prod(s)) for s in shapes]
P = sum(sizes)


def net(th, x):
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = [q.reshape(s) for q, s in zip(torch.split(th, sizes), shapes)]
    return (torch.tanh(x @ W1.T + b1) @ W2.T + b2).squeeze(-1)


ridge = 1e-3                                      # makes the minimum isolated


def objective(th, beta, lam, center):             # sum_i beta_i L_i + ridge + proximal term
    return ((beta * 0.5 * (net(th, Xt) - Yt) ** 2).sum() + 0.5 * ridge * (th ** 2).sum()
            + 0.5 * lam * ((th - center) ** 2).sum())


v_obj = vmap(objective, in_dims=(0, 0, None, None))
v_grad = vmap(grad(objective), in_dims=(0, 0, None, None))
v_hess = vmap(hessian(objective), in_dims=(0, 0, None, None))


def newton(TH, B, lam, center, iters=100):
    # Newton with |eigenvalues| (floored at mu), one problem per row; a step is accepted only if
    # it lowers the objective. Rows that end at a saddle are pushed along the negative-curvature
    # direction and restarted, so every returned point is a minimum.
    for attempt in range(4):
        mu = torch.full((len(TH),), 1e-3)
        val = v_obj(TH, B, lam, center)
        for _ in range(iters):
            g = v_grad(TH, B, lam, center)
            if g.norm(dim=1).max() < 1e-7:
                break
            ev, V = torch.linalg.eigh(v_hess(TH, B, lam, center))
            d = torch.maximum(ev.abs(), mu[:, None])
            step = (V @ ((V.transpose(1, 2) @ g[..., None])[..., 0] / d)[..., None])[..., 0]
            new = TH - step
            nv = v_obj(new, B, lam, center)
            ok = nv < val
            TH, val = torch.where(ok[:, None], new, TH), torch.where(ok, nv, val)
            mu = torch.where(ok, (mu / 3).clamp(min=1e-12), mu * 10)
        ev, V = torch.linalg.eigh(v_hess(TH, B, lam, center))
        saddle = ev[:, 0] < 0
        if not saddle.any():
            break
        kick = 0.05 * V[:, :, 0]                  # escape along the most negative direction
        better = v_obj(TH + kick, B, lam, center) < v_obj(TH - kick, B, lam, center)
        TH = torch.where(saddle[:, None], TH + torch.where(better[:, None], kick, -kick), TH)
    g = v_grad(TH, B, lam, center)
    return TH, g.norm(dim=1).max().item(), ev[:, 0].min().item()

Fits a linear map from the subset indicator \mathbf 1_S to the output of a model trained on S, using hundreds of thousands of retrainings (300,000 CIFAR-10 models at 50% subsets): the expensive target that the LDS is built on. Read §§1–2 and §4.1.

Target versus estimator. A method has a target, the question it is defined to answer: a derivative of some \pi, a deletion, or a subset outcome (§2). It has an estimator: the computation. Errors come from either. The damped IF can compute the PBRF derivative (§4) exactly and still predict LOO retraining poorly. That is a mismatch of target, not a numerical error. So every check should say which question it tests.

Three checks, from least to most demanding. Notation: \mathcal I_i is a method’s score for example i on observable \phi, in the sign convention of §2.

  1. Local finite differences of the method’s own target. Reweight one example by \pm\varepsilon, recompute \pi, and difference \phi. This tests the estimator only.
  2. Leave-one-out. Delete each example, retrain, and compare the change in \phi with -\mathcal I_i. This tests one finite deletion.
  3. Subset deletion and the linear datamodeling score (LDS). Draw m random subsets S_1,\dots,S_m, each a fraction \alpha of the data (\alpha=\frac12 is standard). Retrain on each and record \phi(S_k). Predict each outcome by the summed scores \hat\phi(S_k)=\sum_{i\in S_k}\mathcal I_i: to first order, \phi(S)-\phi(\text{all})=-\sum_{j\notin S}\mathcal I_j, which is \hat\phi(S) up to a constant. The LDS is the Spearman rank correlation between \{\phi(S_k)\} and \{\hat\phi(S_k)\}, averaged over observables (test points). TRAK’s protocol averages each \phi(S_k) over several training seeds.

Two properties of the LDS matter when reading results:

  • It tests ranking, not calibration. Multiplying every score by 10, or applying any increasing function to the predictions, leaves it unchanged.
  • Deleting half the data is far from \beta=\mathbf 1. The LDS rewards estimators that extrapolate well, and these need not be the ones that differentiate exactly.

One model or the distribution? For a nonconvex network, retraining with a different seed changes \phi even on identical data. A target can be single-model (the same seed and data order: this run) or distributional (averaged over seeds). Unrolling targets the first. Datamodels and TRAK’s LDS protocol target the second. The two can disagree: the effect of deleting one example can vary from seed to seed by more than its average, and then a same-seed answer says little about the distributional one.

Gradient-similarity methods have their own estimand. The gradient dot product -g_\phi^\top g_i is the influence function with H replaced by the identity. TracIn uses the training trajectory instead. Write \ell(w,z) for the loss of example z at parameters w, z_q for the test example, and w_t, \eta_t and B_t for the iterate, step size and minibatch (of size b) at step t:

\mathrm{TracIn}(z_i,z_q)=\sum_{t:\,i\in B_t}\frac{\eta_t}{b}\,\nabla\ell(w_t,z_q)\cdot\nabla\ell(w_t,z_i).

This is the first-order decrease of the test loss caused by z_i’s share of each step it was in; summed over all examples, it adds up (to first order in each step) to the decrease \ell(w_0,z_q)-\ell(w_T,z_q) along the actual run. That is a different question from what would change if z_i were missing. (TracIn calls helpful examples positive; its practical version, TracInCP, is in DA2 stop 2.)

def fit(beta, w0=None, lam=0.0, center=None):
    # Newton's method for sum_i beta_i ell_i(w) + kappa/2 |w|^2  [+ lam/2 |w - center|^2]
    w = np.zeros(d) if w0 is None else w0.copy()
    for _ in range(100):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ w))
        g = X.T @ (beta * (p - y)) + kappa * w
        Hw = (X.T * (beta * p * (1 - p))) @ X + kappa * np.eye(d)
        if lam:
            g, Hw = g + lam * (w - center), Hw + lam * np.eye(d)
        step = np.linalg.solve(Hw, g)
        w = w - step
        if np.abs(step).max() < 1e-14 * (1 + np.abs(w).max()):
            break
    return w


def phi(w):
    # the observables: test loss at each of the nq held-out points (a vector)
    m = Xq @ w
    return np.logaddexp(0, m) - yq * m

Solution

The numbers below are from the page’s run (seed 1); your values will match if you did not change the setup.

  1. The exact IF matches the local derivative of retraining to about 3\times10^{-7}, the accuracy of the finite differences. The damped IF is off by about 50% there, because \lambda is as large as a typical eigenvalue of H. But it matches its own target, the derivative of proximal retraining, to the same 3\times10^{-7} (the last printed line). So check 1 confirms both estimators. The damped IF’s error is a difference of target, not a defect, and §4 explains why one would choose that target for a network.
  2. Exercise 3.1: exact deletion is the first-order prediction times 1/(1-h_j) in linear regression. More generally, the higher-order terms of the response add to the first-order term. For logistic regression the factor is not exactly 1/(1-h_j), but the same mechanism makes deletions larger than predicted (slopes of about 1.4 for the exact IF here). The near-perfect Spearman correlation shows that the ranking survives. The damped IF’s fitted slope is larger still (about 1.9).
  3. On LOO the exact IF is clearly best and the damped IF clearly worse (Spearman about 0.96 against 0.82 here). On half-subsets the two are about equal (LDS about 0.83 for both). The checks measure different things, so a ranking of methods by one need not transfer to the other. Choose the check that matches the question you care about.
  4. A high LDS says the summed scores order subsets by their outcome well. It says nothing about the size of the effect: here the fitted calibration slopes of actual on predicted outcomes range from about 12 to 35, far from 1. To claim “deleting these examples changes the loss by about this much”, you need a calibration check like the slopes, not the LDS. How large the miscalibration is depends on the setup: with a stronger ridge (\kappa=1 in the setup cell), the two influence functions’ slopes fall to about 3 and their LDS rises to about 0.97.
  5. Replacing H^{-1} by the identity weights each direction by the raw size of the gradients, not by how much the parameters respond. Along a low-variance feature direction, with scale \sigma, the gradients are of order \sigma and the curvature of order \sigma^2. The IF’s g_\phi^\top H^{-1}g_i is then of order one there, while the dot product is of order \sigma^2 and effectively ignores that direction. Here the Hessian’s eigenvalues span a factor of about 360, from the feature scales and from the logistic weights p_i(1-p_i). If H were a multiple of the identity, the dot product would be proportional to the IF and rank identically. So similarity methods look like influence functions exactly when the curvature is close to isotropic in the directions that matter.

6. A lens for the methods, and where to go next

DA2’s methods answer different questions, but their practical estimators often share one shape: a curvature matrix, something that tames its small eigenvalues, a training gradient and a query gradient. This section lays that shape out as a table of spectral filters, says what the table hides, and hands you to the DA2 tour.

Spectral filters. Take a symmetric curvature matrix M with eigenpairs (s_k,u_k), and expand the training gradient g_i=\sum_kc_ku_k. Many estimators predict a parameter response \partial w/\partial\beta_i of the form

\frac{\partial w}{\partial\beta_i}=-\sum_kF(s_k)\,c_k\,u_k=-F(M)\,g_i ,

and differ in the filter F and in which matrix M is. The classical IF is F(s)=1/s with M=H. In summed-loss units (§2):

Method Filter F(s) s is an eigenvalue of Holds when
classical IF 1/s H, invertible §3’s assumptions
damped Hessian IF 1/(s+\lambda) H by definition; at a stationary point it is the proximal response (§4)
PBRF 1/(s+\lambda) G, a different matrix from H at the checkpoint (§4)
frozen-curvature GD, K steps of size \eta \big[1-(1-\eta s)^K\big]/s H curvature and gradient constant over the K steps
SOURCE, one segment \big(1-e^{-\eta Ks}\big)/s the segment’s average H unpreconditioned SGD, after its stationarity, independence and exponential approximations
local Bayesian IF \approx1/(s+\gamma) H Gaussian leading order at a stationary minimum
Bayesian linear regression 1/(s+\sigma_{\rm noise}^2/\tau^2) X^\top X exact, for the posterior mean
Notes on the rows
  • GD rows: \eta is the step size on the summed loss (for a mean-loss step size, use \eta/n).
  • SOURCE (DA2 stop 2) splits training into segments and treats curvature and gradient as constant within each.
  • Local Bayesian IF: \gamma is the localization strength of the local posterior p_\beta of §2.
  • Bayesian linear regression: \tau^2 is the prior variance and \sigma^2_{\rm noise} the observation noise. The gradients are the unscaled squared-error ones, -x_jr_j. In likelihood-Hessian units, X^\top X/\sigma_{\rm noise}^2, the damping is \tau^{-2}.

Reading the table.

  • Every row except the classical IF is finite at s=0: F(0)=1/\lambda, \eta K, 1/\gamma or \tau^2/\sigma_{\rm noise}^2. This is the sense in which each “regularizes the small eigenvalues”: flat directions get a bounded response.
  • The shapes are similar; the meanings are not. For the damped IF, \lambda is a proximal penalty. For GD, \eta K is training time, so influence already depends on how long you train: directions with s\ll1/(\eta K) have not yet responded fully. For the Bayesian IF, \gamma is a localization strength. For Bayesian linear regression, the damping is a prior.
  • The SOURCE filter (and the GD filter, for \eta s well below 2) crosses over from \eta K to 1/s near s\approx1/(\eta K). It is not ridge: at the crossover it is (1-e^{-1})\,\eta K\approx0.63\,\eta K, against 0.5\,\eta K for ridge with \lambda=1/(\eta K).
Where the GD row comes from

Run GD on L(w;\beta) with constant Hessian H and constant gradient g_i, and let r_t=\partial w_t/\partial\beta_i. One step, w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\nabla_wL(w_t;\beta), gives r_{t+1}=(I-\eta H)\,r_t-\eta g_i, with r_0=0. Summing the geometric series, r_K=-\eta\sum_{k<K}(I-\eta H)^kg_i=-\big[I-(I-\eta H)^K\big]H^{-1}g_i on the range of H, and -\eta K\,g_i on its null space. If 0<\eta s<2, the filter tends to 1/s as K\to\infty; it is close to 1/s once |1-\eta s|^K\ll1.

What the table hides.

  • General unrolling has gradients and curvature that change along the run, and step Jacobians that do not commute. No single filter describes it (DA2 stop 2).
  • Multi-segment SOURCE propagates each segment’s response through every later segment.
  • The Bayesian IF beyond leading order sees non-quadratic structure, which is the reason to use it (DA2 stop 3).
  • Datamodels have no such form: they are fitted, not derived.
  • Negative curvature (s<0): the classical IF follows a saddle, not a minimum; the damped filter diverges at s=-\lambda; the GD and SOURCE filters grow with training time. That is one reason §4 replaces H by G, which has no negative eigenvalues.
