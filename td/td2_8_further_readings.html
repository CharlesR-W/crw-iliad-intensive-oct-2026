1. Dropout as a geometric-mean ensemble

Dropout trains a randomly thinned subnetwork at each step; at test time it runs the full network once, with each weight scaled by its keep probability. Why does that one pass stand in for averaging all the subnetworks? For a logistic unit it is exactly their normalized geometric mean, which is close to their arithmetic mean.

Understanding dropout, Baldi & Sadowski (NeurIPS 2013) Importance ●●●●● Length ●●○○○

The exact geometric-mean identity for logistic units, bounds on its gap to the arithmetic mean, recursive approximations for deep sigmoidal networks, and, for a linear unit, dropout’s adaptive weight-decay term (largest at p=\tfrac12). §3–4 are the averaging results.

Also: Srivastava et al., Dropout (JMLR 2014), the standard reference. Dropout as an adaptive L^2 penalty is derived in the Mysteries day’s MDL1 §4, exercise 4.4.

Optional: annotated derivations and walkthrough

Setup. One logistic unit, \sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x}), with inputs I_i and weights w_i. Dropout gates each input with \delta_i\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_i), independently, so the input sum is S=\sum_i\delta_iw_iI_i and the output is O=\sigma(S). Index the 2^n subnetworks by k, with probabilities P_k, sums S_k and outputs O_k. The arithmetic mean is E=\sum_kP_kO_k; the weighted geometric means are G=\prod_kO_k^{P_k} and G'=\prod_k(1-O_k)^{P_k}; the normalized weighted geometric mean is \mathrm{NWGM}=G/(G+G'), a probability that treats the output and its complement symmetrically. The test-time rule computes \sigma\big(\sum_ip_iw_iI_i\big)=\sigma(\mathbb E S).

Exercise 1.1 — The averaging identity. Show that \mathrm{NWGM}=\sigma(\mathbb E S) exactly, for any probabilities P_k (they need not come from independent gates).

Hint

Divide numerator and denominator by G, and use (1-\sigma(x))/\sigma(x)=e^{-x}.

Solution

Divide through by G:

\mathrm{NWGM}=\frac{1}{1+G'/G}=\frac{1}{1+\prod_k\big(\tfrac{1-O_k}{O_k}\big)^{P_k}}.

For the logistic function (1-\sigma(x))/\sigma(x)=e^{-x}, so each factor is e^{-P_kS_k}, and the product is \exp\big(-\sum_kP_kS_k\big)=e^{-\mathbb ES}. Hence \mathrm{NWGM}=1/(1+e^{-\mathbb ES})=\sigma(\mathbb ES). Only \sum_kP_k=1 was used, so the identity holds for any distribution over subnetworks. With independent Bernoulli gates, \mathbb ES=\sum_ip_iw_iI_i, which is what the weight-scaled network computes. The same argument works for a softmax output layer; the paper notes (citing its companion paper) that only constant and logistic functions satisfy \mathrm{NWGM}(f(S))=f(\mathbb ES).

Exercise 1.2 — How far is the geometric mean from the arithmetic one? Suppose the spread of S across subnetworks is small: S=\mu+\varepsilon with \mu=\mathbb ES, \mathbb E\varepsilon=0 and \mathrm{Var}\,S=s^2 small. Write V=\mathrm{Var}\,O. To leading order in s^2, show that

E-\mathrm{NWGM}\;\approx\;\tfrac12\sigma''(\mu)\,s^2\;\approx\;\frac{V\,(1-2E)}{2E(1-E)}.

So the gap is second order in the spread, and to this order vanishes at E=\tfrac12.

Hint

Use Exercise 1.1 for the NWGM. Taylor-expand \mathbb E\,\sigma(\mu+\varepsilon) to second order; for V, first order is enough. The derivatives are \sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma) and \sigma''=\sigma(1-\sigma)(1-2\sigma).

Solution

By Exercise 1.1, \mathrm{NWGM}=\sigma(\mu). Expanding,

E=\mathbb E\,\sigma(\mu+\varepsilon)=\sigma(\mu)+\sigma'(\mu)\,\mathbb E\varepsilon+\tfrac12\sigma''(\mu)\,\mathbb E\varepsilon^2+O(\mathbb E|\varepsilon|^3) =\sigma(\mu)+\tfrac12\sigma''(\mu)s^2+\dots,

so E-\mathrm{NWGM}\approx\tfrac12\sigma''(\mu)s^2. For the variance, O\approx\sigma(\mu)+\sigma'(\mu)\varepsilon gives V\approx\sigma'(\mu)^2s^2=\sigma(\mu)^2(1-\sigma(\mu))^2s^2. Eliminate s^2:

E-\mathrm{NWGM}\approx\tfrac12\,\sigma(1-\sigma)(1-2\sigma)\cdot\frac{V}{\sigma^2(1-\sigma)^2} =\frac{V(1-2\sigma)}{2\sigma(1-\sigma)},

and replacing \sigma(\mu) by E changes this only at higher order.

Numerical check. Exact enumeration of all 2^{12} subnetworks of a 12-input unit. The last column is the paper’s own second-order estimate of the gap, its eq. (20), V(1-2E)/(1-2V); in this small-spread regime it is smaller than the exact gap by about the factor 1/(2E(1-E))\ge2. The paper’s conclusion, that the NWGM is close to E, is unaffected.

Code (26 lines) 
sigma = lambda s: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-s))
n = 12
masks = np.array(list(itertools.product([0, 1], repeat=n)))   # all 2**12 subnetworks


def means(w, I, p, bias=0.0):
    # Arithmetic mean E, variance V, NWGM and sigma(E[S]) over every dropout mask, exactly.
    P = np.prod(np.where(masks == 1, p, 1 - p), axis=1)        # probability of each mask
    S = masks @ (w * I) + bias                                 # input sum of each subnetwork
    O = sigma(S)
    E = P @ O
    V = P @ (O - E) ** 2
    G, Gc = np.exp(P @ np.log(O)), np.exp(P @ np.log(1 - O))
    return E, V, G / (G + Gc), sigma(P @ S)


# Exercise 1.1: the identity is exact, even with large weights and unequal keep probabilities.
E, V, nwgm, sig_ES = means(rng.normal(0, 1, n), rng.normal(0, 1, n), rng.uniform(0.3, 0.9, n))
print(f"NWGM = {nwgm:.12f}   sigma(E[S]) = {sig_ES:.12f}   arithmetic mean E = {E:.6f}\n")

# Exercise 1.2: small weights (small spread), with a bias to move E away from 1/2.

2. Layer cushion, noise robustness and basin size

In what precise sense is a trained network robust to noise, and does training make it so? Arora et al. measure how strongly noise injected at one layer is attenuated by the layers above it, on the training data. Their key quantity, the layer cushion, is small at random initialization and grows during training, most in the higher layers.

Stronger generalization bounds for deep nets via a compression approach, Arora, Ge, Neyshabur & Zhang (ICML 2018) Importance ●●●●● Length ●●○○○

Defines noise sensitivity, layer cushion and related noise-stability properties, uses them to compress trained networks and bound the compressed network’s generalization error, and measures the cushion growing during training. §3 has the definitions; §6 the measurements.

Computing nonvacuous generalization bounds for deep (stochastic) neural networks with many more parameters than training data, Dziugaite & Roy (UAI 2017) Importance ●●●○○ Length ●●●○○

The weight-noise version: optimize a PAC-Bayes bound over a Gaussian centred near the SGD solution, getting nonvacuous bounds on binary MNIST; the weight noise the training loss tolerates is a (parametrization-dependent) proxy for basin size.

This is a quantitative replacement for “flat minima generalize”, whose raw flatness depends on how the network is parametrized (td2_4 §4.2; the Mysteries day’s MDL1 §4, under “Why flatness could matter, and why sharpness needs a metric”). A network that attenuates injected noise can be compressed a lot, since compression error acts like injected noise, and the compressed network gets a generalization bound the authors report as orders of magnitude better than parameter counting. td2_6 §6.5 covers when the basin is chosen.

Optional: annotated derivations and walkthrough

Notation. Layer i computes x^i=A_i\phi(x^{i-1}), with weight matrix A_i, elementwise activation \phi (ReLU in the paper) and input x^0. \lVert A\rVert_F is the Frobenius norm, \lVert A\rVert_2 the spectral norm, and \eta\sim\mathcal N(0,I) a standard Gaussian noise vector (not a learning rate here), so \mathbb E[\eta\eta^\top]=I.

Definitions (from the paper). The noise sensitivity of a map M at input x is the expected squared noise-to-signal ratio of the output under isotropic input noise scaled by \lVert x\rVert:

\psi(M,x)=\mathbb E_{\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,I)}\frac{\lVert M(x+\eta\lVert x\rVert)-M(x)\rVert^2}{\lVert M(x)\rVert^2}.

The layer cushion \mu_i is the largest \mu such that \mu\lVert A_i\rVert_F\lVert\phi(x^{i-1})\rVert\le\lVert A_i\phi(x^{i-1})\rVert for every training input. The stable rank of A is \lVert A\rVert_F^2/\lVert A\rVert_2^2.

Exercise 2.1 — Cushion is inverse noise sensitivity.

  1. For a linear map M, show \psi(M,x)=\lVert M\rVert_F^2\lVert x\rVert^2/\lVert Mx\rVert^2. Conclude that 1/\mu_i^2 is the largest noise sensitivity of A_i over the training inputs, that \psi is at least the stable rank, and so \mu_i\le1/\sqrt{\text{stable rank}}. When is the bound attained?
  2. Take x\sim\mathcal N(0,I_h) independent of A\in\mathbb R^{h'\times h}. Show \mathbb E\big[\lVert Ax\rVert^2/\lVert x\rVert^2\big]=\lVert A\rVert_F^2/h, so a typical input has cushion about 1/\sqrt h. What must happen during training for the cushion to grow well above that?
Solution

1. M(x+\eta\lVert x\rVert)-Mx=\lVert x\rVert M\eta, and \mathbb E\lVert M\eta\rVert^2=\mathbb E\,\mathrm{tr}(M\eta\eta^\top M^\top)=\mathrm{tr}(MM^\top)=\lVert M\rVert_F^2. So \psi=\lVert M\rVert_F^2\lVert x\rVert^2/\lVert Mx\rVert^2. The cushion condition reads \lVert A_i\phi\rVert^2/(\lVert A_i\rVert_F^2\lVert\phi\rVert^2)\ge\mu^2 for every training input, that is 1/\psi(A_i,\phi)\ge\mu^2, so the largest such \mu has 1/\mu_i^2=\max_x\psi(A_i,\phi(x^{i-1})) (the paper’s Proposition 3.1 and the remark after its Definition 4). Since \lVert Mx\rVert\le\lVert M\rVert_2\lVert x\rVert, \psi\ge\lVert M\rVert_F^2/\lVert M\rVert_2^2, the stable rank, and hence \mu_i\le\lVert A_i\rVert_2/\lVert A_i\rVert_F. Equality needs every training input to lie in the top right singular subspace of A_i. A large cushion therefore needs both a spectrum dominated by a few singular values (low stable rank) and inputs aligned with those directions.

2. x/\lVert x\rVert is uniform on the sphere, so \mathbb E\big[(x/\lVert x\rVert)(x/\lVert x\rVert)^\top\big]=I/h and \mathbb E\big[\lVert Ax\rVert^2/\lVert x\rVert^2\big]=\mathrm{tr}(A^\top A)/h=\lVert A\rVert_F^2/h. The typical cushion is about 1/\sqrt h; it concentrates there when A’s stable rank is large, and for a very low-rank A it fluctuates by order one around that value. For the cushion to grow well above 1/\sqrt h, training has to line up each layer’s inputs on the training data with that layer’s large singular directions: weights and the representations they act on must align. Noise spreads over all directions, while aligned signal passes preferentially. The paper’s measurements (VGG-19 on CIFAR-10) show this growth relative to a random network.

Numerical check of both parts, on a 256\times256 layer with singular values 1/k and on a random Gaussian layer.

3. Geometry-adaptive harmonic bases

What function does a trained image denoiser, the network inside a diffusion model, compute once it has enough data to stop memorizing? Locally, it shrinks the noisy image along an image-dependent basis of harmonics that oscillate along contours and in smooth regions, and these bases appear even on data for which they are suboptimal. It is an inductive-bias result like the spectral bias of td2_7 §7.3, read off from the network’s Jacobian.

Generalization in diffusion models arises from geometry-adaptive harmonic representations, Kadkhodaie, Guth, Simoncelli & Mallat (ICLR 2024) Importance ●●●●● Length ●●○○○

Denoisers trained on disjoint halves of a face dataset memorize when data are few and converge to nearly the same score when data are many (about 10^5 images); the Jacobian-eigenvector analysis then shows near-optimal, geometry-adaptive shrinkage, which persists on data where it is suboptimal. §2 for the transition, §3 for the analysis.

Also: Mohan et al., bias-free denoising CNNs (ICLR 2020) (why the Jacobian reading works); Kamb & Ganguli, an analytic theory of creativity (2024) (locality and equivariance alone predict trained diffusion models’ outputs); Belrose et al., statistics of increasing complexity (2024) (td2_7’s “low-order statistics first”, measured on real networks).

Optional: annotated derivations and walkthrough

The optimal denoiser. A clean image x\in\mathbb R^d is observed as y=x+\sigma z, with z\sim\mathcal N(0,I) and noise level \sigma. Write p_\sigma for the density of y, and \nabla f(y) for the Jacobian of a map f at y. The MSE-optimal denoiser is f^\star(y)=\mathbb E[x\mid y]=y+\sigma^2\nabla\log p_\sigma(y) (the Miyasawa or Tweedie identity, which is why a denoiser gives the score). Its Jacobian is \nabla f^\star(y)=\sigma^{-2}\,\mathrm{Cov}[x\mid y], symmetric and positive semidefinite, and the optimal error is \mathrm{MSE}=\mathbb E\,\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}[x\mid y]=\sigma^2\,\mathbb E\,\mathrm{tr}\,\nabla f^\star(y). Exercise 3.1 checks the last two for Gaussian data.

What a bias-free network computes. The paper’s denoisers are ReLU networks with no additive biases, so they are positively homogeneous and f(y)=\nabla f(y)\,y wherever f is differentiable. Idealizing the Jacobian as symmetric positive semidefinite (approximately true for trained networks), with \nabla f(y)=\sum_k\lambda_k(y)e_k(y)e_k(y)^\top,

f(y)=\sum_k\lambda_k(y)\,\langle y,e_k(y)\rangle\,e_k(y):

shrinkage by factors \lambda_k(y) along a basis e_k(y), both of which can depend on y. The exercise works out the case where they cannot.

Exercise 3.1 — The Gaussian case: shrinkage in a fixed basis. Let x\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma) in \mathbb R^d, with eigendecomposition \Sigma=\sum_k\lambda_kv_kv_k^\top, observed as y=x+\sigma z. Here \lambda_k are the data variances, not the shrinkage factors \lambda_k(y).

  1. Show f^\star(y)=\Sigma(\Sigma+\sigma^2I)^{-1}y. What are the basis and the shrinkage factors, and do they depend on y?
  2. Check \nabla f^\star=\sigma^{-2}\mathrm{Cov}[x\mid y] directly.
  3. Show \mathrm{MSE}=\sigma^2\sum_k\lambda_k/(\lambda_k+\sigma^2). Read \mathrm{tr}\,\nabla f^\star as an effective number of dimensions kept, and compare the MSE with d\sigma^2, the error of returning y unchanged.
Hint

For jointly Gaussian (x,y), \mathbb E[x\mid y]=\mathrm{Cov}(x,y)\,\mathrm{Cov}(y)^{-1}y and \mathrm{Cov}[x\mid y]=\mathrm{Cov}(x)-\mathrm{Cov}(x,y)\mathrm{Cov}(y)^{-1}\mathrm{Cov}(y,x). Here \mathrm{Cov}(x,y)=\Sigma and \mathrm{Cov}(y)=\Sigma+\sigma^2I.

Solution

1. With \mathrm{Cov}(x,y)=\Sigma and \mathrm{Cov}(y)=\Sigma+\sigma^2I, f^\star(y)=\Sigma(\Sigma+\sigma^2I)^{-1}y. In the eigenbasis of \Sigma,

f^\star(y)=\sum_k\frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_k+\sigma^2}\,\langle y,v_k\rangle\,v_k:

shrinkage in the fixed basis v_k (the data’s principal components), with factors \lambda_k/(\lambda_k+\sigma^2) that keep high-variance directions and suppress those below the noise level. Neither depends on y: the Gaussian denoiser is linear. This is Wiener filtering; for stationary images with periodic boundaries the v_k are Fourier modes.

2. \mathrm{Cov}[x\mid y]=\Sigma-\Sigma(\Sigma+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\Sigma =\Sigma(\Sigma+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\big[(\Sigma+\sigma^2I)-\Sigma\big] =\sigma^2\,\Sigma(\Sigma+\sigma^2I)^{-1} (all these matrices commute). Dividing by \sigma^2 gives \Sigma(\Sigma+\sigma^2I)^{-1}=\nabla f^\star.

3. \mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}[x\mid y]=\sigma^2\sum_k\lambda_k/(\lambda_k+\sigma^2)=\sigma^2\,\mathrm{tr}\,\nabla f^\star. Each factor is between 0 and 1, roughly 1 for \lambda_k\gg\sigma^2 and 0 for \lambda_k\ll\sigma^2, so \mathrm{tr}\,\nabla f^\star counts the directions whose signal exceeds the noise, each costing about \sigma^2. Returning y costs d\sigma^2, and \mathrm{MSE}/(d\sigma^2)=\mathrm{tr}\,\nabla f^\star/d. A small error therefore needs \mathrm{tr}\,\nabla f^\star\ll d: few effective dimensions kept (the paper’s eq. (10) argument).

The paper’s point is the contrast: for the Gaussian the best basis is fixed, while a trained network’s basis e_k(y) moves with the image, following its edges and smooth regions.

Numerical check by Monte Carlo, on 64-dimensional Gaussian data with variances 1/k^2 and noise level \sigma=0.1.

4. Critical periods and loss of plasticity

Does it matter when a network sees something? Yes. A deficit early in training, such as blurred images for the first epochs, lowers final accuracy in a way later training does not repair (a critical period), and a network trained on a long sequence of tasks gradually loses the ability to learn new ones (loss of plasticity). Early training builds something later training cannot easily undo.

Loss of plasticity in deep continual learning, Dohare, Hernandez-Garcia, Lan, Rahman, Mahmood & Sutton (Nature 2024; preprint arXiv:2306.13812) Importance ●●●●● Length ●●○○○

On continual ImageNet variants and in RL, standard networks steadily lose the ability to learn, across architectures and optimizers; “continual backprop”, which keeps reinitializing a few little-used units, maintains plasticity.

Critical learning periods in deep networks, Achille, Rovere & Soatto (ICLR 2019) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●●○○

Early blur leaves a permanent deficit, while deficits that keep low-level statistics (vertical flips) do not; the Fisher information of the weights rises fast early in training and then falls.

Two hooks to this day: critical periods already appear in deep linear networks, where the mode equations of td1_linear_networks §1 make them tractable (Kleinman et al.), and plasticity loss is tied to changes in loss-landscape curvature (Lyle et al.; td2_6). The Mysteries day lists critical periods among its strange facts in MDL4 §4.

Also: Kleinman, Achille & Soatto, critical periods in deep linear networks (ICLR 2024); Lyle et al., understanding plasticity (ICML 2023) (plasticity loss tracks curvature, often without saturated units); Ash & Adams, on warm-starting (NeurIPS 2020) (the everyday version, and “shrink and perturb”).

5. Fine-tuning and RL update dynamics

Post-training moves a pretrained model a small distance; in which directions? Measured in weights, RL fine-tuning of language models changes few parameters (as counted in bfloat16), yet each update matrix has full numerical rank, is dominated by one singular direction, and avoids the pretrained matrices’ “principal weights”, where supervised fine-tuning (SFT) does not.

Reinforcement learning finetunes small subnetworks in large language models, Mukherjee, Yuan, Hakkani-Tür & Peng (NeurIPS 2025) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●○○○

Across seven RL algorithms and ten models, RL changes only 5–30% of parameters (counting bfloat16 changes up to 10^{-5} as none); the changed set is consistent across seeds and algorithms, fine-tuning it alone reproduces the full result, and each matrix’s update has nearly full numerical rank.

The path not taken: RLVR provably learns off the principals, Zhu et al. (2025) Importance ●●●●○ Length ●●●○○

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) lands less often than chance on the principal weights (the largest entries of a low-rank SVD reconstruction of each pretrained matrix, a proxy for high curvature) and barely changes the singular spectrum, while SFT does the opposite. The “provably” refers to bounds for small, KL-constrained steps.

The question this day adds, from td2_6 §6.4: how does the update sit relative to the curvature (the Hessian or Gauss–Newton eigenbasis)? None of these papers measures that directly at language-model scale, and Exercise 5.2 shows why a weight-matrix proxy for it needs care. The Mysteries day’s MDL4 §11 reads Mukherjee et al. and Shenfeld et al. as a pair, including what “unchanged” means in bfloat16.

Also: Cai et al., predictability of RL dynamics (2025) (the top singular component of each update recovers about 99% of the accuracy); Schulman and Thinking Machines Lab, LoRA without regret (2025) (rank-1 LoRA suffices for policy-gradient RL); Shenfeld, Pari & Agrawal, RL’s Razor (2025) (forgetting tracks the KL divergence to the base model); Malladi et al., a kernel view of fine-tuning (ICML 2023) (prompt-based fine-tuning as kernel regression with the pretrained tangent kernel); CRW, some interesting papers on RLVR (blog, 2026) (a short, informal survey of these papers and more).

Optional: annotated derivations and walkthrough

Notation. \Delta W is the change in one weight matrix from fine-tuning. Its numerical rank counts singular values above a small tolerance; its stable rank is \lVert\Delta W\rVert_F^2/\lVert\Delta W\rVert_2^2 (Frobenius over spectral norm, squared). Cai et al. keep only the top singular component of each update, rescaled to the update’s norm, and recover about 99% of the trained model’s accuracy.

Exercise 5.1 — Full rank and dominated by one direction. Let \Delta W=uv^\top+\epsilon N with unit vectors u,v\in\mathbb R^n, a dense Gaussian N with entries of variance 1/n, and small \epsilon. What are its rank, numerical rank and stable rank? Which of these do Mukherjee et al. and Cai et al. measure?

Solution

Almost surely every singular value of \Delta W is nonzero, so its rank is n, and its numerical rank is also n unless \epsilon is tiny. But \lVert\epsilon N\rVert_F^2\approx\epsilon^2n and \lVert\epsilon N\rVert_2\approx2\epsilon, so the top singular value is about 1 and the rest are at most about 2\epsilon. The stable rank is about 1+\epsilon^2n, close to 1 when \epsilon\ll1/\sqrt n. Mukherjee et al. report numerical rank (full); Cai et al. measure how much of the accuracy the top singular direction alone recovers (about 99%). Both can be true of the same update.

Numerical check of Exercise 5.1, plus the measurement issue behind the sparsity numbers: a dense update much smaller than the bfloat16 spacing of the weights is mostly invisible when both checkpoints are rounded to bfloat16. Shenfeld et al. found that in their setting the sparsity came from bfloat16 and vanished in float32, at the same performance.

def to_bf16(a):
    # Round finite float32 values to the nearest bfloat16 (ties to even); return as float32.
    b = np.asarray(a, dtype=np.float32).view(np.uint32).astype(np.uint64)
    b = (b + 0x7FFF + ((b >> 16) & 1)) >> 16
    return (b.astype(np.uint32) << 16).view(np.float32)

Exercise 5.2 — Principal directions are not curvature directions. Take a single linear layer f(x)=Wx with loss L(W)=\tfrac12\mathbb E\lVert Wx-y\rVert^2 and input second moment \Sigma_x=\mathbb E[xx^\top].

  1. Show that L(W+\Delta W)=L(W)+\langle\nabla L,\Delta W\rangle+\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(\Delta W\,\Sigma_x\Delta W^\top) exactly. What are the eigen-directions of this curvature, and their eigenvalues?
  2. Let W=\sum_i s_iu_iv_i^\top be the SVD. Give a case where every highest-curvature update is orthogonal (in the Frobenius inner product) to W’s top singular direction u_1v_1^\top. When are “along W’s principal directions” and “along high curvature” close to the same thing?
Hint

For part 2, \langle ab^\top,u_1v_1^\top\rangle_F=(a\cdot u_1)(b\cdot v_1). Compare the condition you need with the layer cushion of §2.

Solution

1. With residual r=Wx-y, \tfrac12\mathbb E\lVert r+\Delta Wx\rVert^2=L(W)+\mathbb E\langle r,\Delta Wx\rangle+\tfrac12\mathbb E\lVert\Delta Wx\rVert^2, and \mathbb E\lVert\Delta Wx\rVert^2=\mathbb E\,\mathrm{tr}(\Delta Wxx^\top\Delta W^\top)=\mathrm{tr}(\Delta W\Sigma_x\Delta W^\top). There are no higher terms because the loss is quadratic in W. The curvature acts as \Delta W\mapsto\Delta W\Sigma_x (on \mathrm{vec}\,\Delta W it is \Sigma_x\otimes I). Its eigen-directions are \Delta W=ac_k^\top for any output vector a and eigenvector c_k of \Sigma_x, with eigenvalue \lambda_k(\Sigma_x): the curvature of an update depends only on how its right factor meets the input distribution, not on W.

2. The highest-curvature updates are ac_1^\top. Their overlap with u_1v_1^\top is (a\cdot u_1)(c_1\cdot v_1), zero for every a when the top input direction c_1 is orthogonal to W’s top right singular vector v_1. For example, W=\mathrm{diag}(1,0.1) and \Sigma_x=\mathrm{diag}(0.1,1): the principal direction is e_1e_1^\top, every highest-curvature update is ae_2^\top, and an update along u_1v_1^\top has low curvature. The two notions agree when W’s top right singular vectors line up with the top eigenvectors of \Sigma_x. A large layer cushion (§2) suggests such alignment but does not establish it: the cushion is a worst case over individual training inputs, while \Sigma_x weights them by frequency.

For a hidden layer the curvature also has a left factor. In the Kronecker-factored approximation of Martens and Grosse, the layer’s Fisher block is approximately \mathbb E[aa^\top]\otimes\mathbb E[gg^\top], with a the layer’s input and g the gradient with respect to its preactivations, computed with targets sampled from the model’s own predictive distribution (with training labels one gets the empirical Fisher, which can differ). For squared error and softmax cross-entropy this Fisher equals the Gauss–Newton matrix. So whether a weight matrix’s principal directions are its curvature directions is an empirical question about the trained network, and a proxy like Zhu et al.’s principal weights has to be argued for rather than assumed.
