Notation on the board (matches NB2): loss $L$, gradient $g=\nabla L$, Hessian $H$, sharpness $S=\lambda_{\max}(H)$, step $\eta$, batch size $B$, per-example gradient covariance $\Sigma$. Time is $t=k\eta$, so gradient flow is $\dot\theta=-\nabla L$.

Symbol clashes to warn about once: $\Sigma$ is the target correlation in NB1 and the noise covariance here; $\lambda$ is a Hessian eigenvalue in td2_1/td2_2, the NTK in the catapult, and the weight decay in td2_5; $P$ is Adam's preconditioner in td2_2–td2_5 and the parameter count in td2_6.

Two kinds of tools, one picture

1. Small-step expansions. Treat training as a flow plus corrections, or as a stochastic differential equation. The expansion parameter is $\eta S$. These give the scaling rules.

2. Where they break. Real training sits at $\eta S\approx2$, exactly where "small step" fails, and different tools take over.

Keep this valley on the board. The bounce across the walls is the top eigenvector; progress is the drift along the floor. Segments on the learning rate, the schedule, curvature and what is learned first all come back to it.

Every NB2 notebook stands alone. Each opens with its readings (every paper, rated, with a line on what it is for), then the derivations and code follow section by section, so students can read the papers, do the derivations, or both. Nobody is expected to finish.

The figure: a quadratic with curvature ratio 50, gradient descent at $\eta S=1.98$. The iterate bounces across the sharp direction at nearly constant amplitude while sliding down the flat one. In a real network the walls move (progressive sharpening), which is the edge-of-stability story two slides after the SDE.

§1 · Learning rate as a step size

Gradient descent follows a modified flow that penalizes the gradient norm

$$\dot\theta=-\nabla\Big(L+\tfrac{\eta}{4}\|\nabla L\|^2\Big)$$
  • On a quadratic, GD converges iff $\eta S<2$.
  • GD's gradient doesn't shrink along a step, so each step overshoots by $-\tfrac{\eta^2}{2}Hg$. As a drift, that is the penalty: implicit gradient regularization.
  • Asymptotic series. Degrades smoothly as $\eta S$ grows; no flow at all once $\eta\lambda\ge1$ in some direction.
  • Momentum: effective step $\eta/(1-\beta)$, edge $2(1+\beta)/\eta$.

$L=\tfrac12(ab-1)^2$ from $(2,0.1)$. Gradient flow conserves $a^2-b^2$; GD shrinks it, and the IGR flow (dashed) accounts for 81%, 42% and 16% of the shift at $\eta S\approx0.2$, $0.8$, $1.4$.

td2_1_flows §1.1–1.3 (Barrett & Dherin), §1.5 momentum (Goh's Distill article)

Limits, stated in td2_1 itself: the penalty vanishes at critical points, so it doesn't help escape minima. It matters when minima form a family (here the hyperbola $ab=1$, where the sharpness is $\sqrt{D^2+4}$ with $D=a^2-b^2$). "GD ends at flatter minima" is Barrett and Dherin's prediction with experiments, not a theorem.

Momentum in one sentence: at equal effective learning rate, heavy ball's implicit penalty is $(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)$ times larger, valid only at much smaller curvatures.

Picked up in: td2_1 §1.2 (the code after Exercise 1.2.d is this figure), §1.5 for momentum.

§2 · Batch size

To first order, SGD is gradient flow plus noise of strength $\eta/B$

$$d\theta=-\nabla L\,dt+\sqrt{\tfrac{\eta}{B}}\,\Sigma^{1/2}dW$$
  • Holding $\eta/B$ fixed holds the SDE fixed: the linear scaling rule.
  • In a basin the floor is $\tfrac{\eta}{4B}\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma$, with no $H$ in it.
  • A one-step Taylor model gives the critical batch $B_{\rm noise}=\mathrm{tr}(H\Sigma)/(g^\top Hg)$, past which larger batches buy almost nothing.
  • Adam: temperature $\eta/\sqrt B$, hence a square-root rule.

Diagonal quadratic, Gaussian gradient noise. Same $\eta/B$, same floor, until $\eta h$ stops being small in the top mode ($\eta=0.8$ sits above the line).

td2_2_sgd_sde §2.1–2.6 (Li, Malladi & Arora; McCandlish et al.; Malladi et al.), §2.7 validity

The order-2 stochastic modified equation adds IGR's drift $\tfrac\eta4\|\nabla L\|^2$, which depends on $\eta$, not $\eta/B$, so the linear scaling rule is a first-order statement. It fails once $B$ approaches $B_{\rm noise}$, when $\eta h$ isn't small in the top directions (the orange run), and at the edge of stability. $\Delta L_{\rm opt}(B)=\Delta L_{\max}/(1+B_{\rm noise}/B)$.

Adam's rule, stated only: under $B\to\kappa B$, take $\eta\to\sqrt\kappa\eta$, $1-\beta_i\to\kappa(1-\beta_i)$, $\epsilon\to\epsilon/\sqrt\kappa$. Needs noise-dominated gradients.

Competing accounts, present evenly. What does the noise buy? One view: the temperature $\eta/B$ is the important parameter. Smith, Dherin, Barrett and De: the finite-$\eta$ drift corrections matter more for generalization (§2.7 item 4). Is the noise Gaussian? Şimşekli et al. argue heavy tails; Li, Malladi and Arora find matched-covariance Gaussian noise reaches the same CIFAR-10 accuracy (§2.7 item 7). "Is the Taylor expansion even valid?" §2.7 collects nine answers; weak-order bounds hold on a fixed horizon as $\eta\to0$ and nothing quantitative follows for a given $\eta$. Testable with SVAG (§2.5).

Picked up in: td2_2 §2.2 (the SDE), §2.3 (the floor), critical batch, scaling rules, validity.

§3 · Where the expansions break: the edge of stability

Sharpness rises to $2/\eta$ and stays there while the loss keeps falling

$$\nabla L(\bar\theta+xu)=\nabla L(\bar\theta)+xSu+\tfrac12x^2\nabla S+O(x^3)$$

Tanh MLP, 256 random labels, full-batch GD. All three runs follow the same curve in $t=k\eta$ until each hits its own $2/\eta$. The $x^2$ term survives the period-2 average: self-stabilization.

td2_3_edge_of_stability §3.1–3.4 (Cohen et al.; Damian, Nichani & Lee; central flows, Rod Flow)

This is exactly where the expansion of the previous slide fails. The bounce across the valley is the oscillation along $u$; the $\tfrac12x^2\nabla S$ push moves the iterate toward lower walls.

The central flow models the time-averaged trajectory: $dw/dt=-[\nabla L+\tfrac12\sigma^2\nabla S]$, with $\sigma^2$ fixed by requiring $S$ to stay at $2/\eta$; its time-averaged loss is $L+\sigma^2/\eta$. State it, don't derive it (Exercise 3.3). Heuristic, and Cohen et al. state its limits. Rod Flow tracks the midpoint and half-length of consecutive iterates; principal flow resums the backward-error terms built from $H$ and captures the onset but not the stabilization (§3.4).

Open question to name: why progressive sharpening happens is only partly understood. td2_3 takes it as given.

Under SGD the quantity at $2/\eta$ is a batch sharpness and the full-batch $S$ settles lower (§3.6). Adam holds a preconditioned sharpness at $38/\eta$ at $\beta_1=0.9$ (§3.5).

Show, if time: eos_toy_and_catapult.html, panel 1, preset "EoS from below". Picked up in: td2_3 §3.1 (this figure).

§3 · Where the expansions break: the catapult

A large step can lower the curvature in one shot

Width-$n$ two-layer net, one input, exact maps. $\eta\lambda_0<2$ lazy; $2<\eta\lambda_0<4$ the loss spikes, the curvature drops, training converges somewhere flatter; $\eta\lambda_0>4$ diverges.

  • Contrast with the edge of stability: self-stabilization is a sustained hover, the catapult a one-shot drop with nothing pushing the curvature back up.
  • The $4/\lambda_0$ edge is specific to this model; ReLU nets in the paper tolerate up to about $12/\lambda_0$.

td2_3_edge_of_stability §3.7 (Lewkowycz et al.)

Compressible to one sentence. The maps: with $b=\eta^2f^2/n$, $f\to f(1-\eta\lambda+b)$ and $\eta\lambda\to\eta\lambda+b(\eta\lambda-4)$. At $\eta\lambda_0=3$ the loss peaks near $n/2$ and the curvature lands at $\eta\lambda\approx0.24$.

Show, if time: the same interactive page, panel 2, presets lazy, catapult, divergent. Picked up in: td2_3 §3.7.

§4 · Schedule

Both ends of a schedule have a mechanism, and each end has more than one

Warmup. Edge-of-stability account: starting at the target $\eta$ from a sharp initialization catapults or diverges; ramping lets $S$ follow $2/\eta(t)$ down. Adam account: early $\hat v$ is estimated from few samples, so the adaptive step has high variance.

Decay. Three pictures of one observation, that the loss drops when $\eta$ is cut: the fluctuation floor $\tfrac{\eta}{4B}\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma$; Wen et al.'s river valley, where noise bounces the iterate between the hillsides and hides progress along the river; an edge-of-stability analogue, where the measured loss is $L+\sigma^2/\eta$.

Same quadratic. The dashed line is the exact expectation, not a fit. The target drops tenfold at once; the loss follows mode by mode at rates $2\eta'h_j$.

td2_3_edge_of_stability §3.8 (Kalra & Barkeshli; Wen et al.) · td2_2_sgd_sde §2.3

What changes immediately at a cut is the target; what sets the speed is where the noise sits. Noise in sharp modes drops within a few $1/(2\eta'h_{\max})$ steps and looks immediate; noise in flat modes drops slowly.

The edge-of-stability analogue for decay is labelled in td2_3 as the notebook's own and not a claim of either paper. Exercise 3.8(a) tests warmup in the toy from two starting sharpnesses and asks when it helps and whether it can hurt.

Show, if time: eos_toy_and_catapult.html, panel 1, presets "sharp start, no warmup" and "sharp start, warmup". Picked up in: td2_2 §2.8, td2_3 §3.3 (Code 2.3.B is this figure).

§5 · Optimizer

An optimizer has to decide what counts as a small step. That decision is a norm.

$$\Delta=\arg\min_{\|\Delta\|\le\eta}\langle g,\Delta\rangle=-\eta\,d^\star(g),\qquad d^\star(g)\in\arg\max_{\|d\|\le1}\langle g,d\rangle$$
  • Euclidean: GD. $\ell_\infty$: sign descent (Adam with its averages off). Fisher norm: natural gradient. Spectral norm on a weight matrix: $UV^\top$, which Muon approximates by Newton–Schulz.
  • Every optimizer is steepest descent under some norm, so the dictionary explains nothing on its own. A norm earns its place by parametrization invariance or by step sizes that transfer across width.

td2_4_optimizer_geometry §4.0–4.4 (Bernstein & Newhouse; Martens; Jordan et al.)

The parable, labelled as an analogy as td2_4 §4.0 does: between point masses with disjoint supports, KL is infinite and Jensen–Shannon constant, so neither gives a gradient; Wasserstein-1 is $|\theta|$ because only it uses the geometry of the space. The parameter-space version is only the question "in which geometry should a step be measured?" The GAN theorems imply nothing about optimizers.

Natural gradient is invariant to reparametrization to first order. Raw sharpness is not: the Hessian is a quadratic form and its eigenvalues need a metric. This links to the MDL day's flatness discussion (Dinh et al.).

Adam's edge: with a frozen preconditioner, Adam at $\beta_1=0.9$ is stable iff $\eta\lambda_{\max}(P^{-1}H)<38$ (td2_3 §3.5). "Adam is sign descent" is exact only with the averages off (§4.3.D gives the failures). The squared-penalty form of the problem points the same way as the trust-region form, with a step $\|g\|_*$ times longer.

Show, if time: steepest_descent_balls.html, and try $g_2\approx0$ to see why sign descent is sensitive to small gradient components. Picked up in: td2_4 §4.1, §4.2 natural gradient, §4.3 Adam, §4.4 Muon.

§6 · Width

Which step sizes transfer across width? A spectral condition answers, and gives muP

$$\|W_l\|_2=\Theta\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{n_l}{n_{l-1}}}\Big),\quad\|\Delta W_l\|_2=\Theta\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{n_l}{n_{l-1}}}\Big)$$
  • Gradient updates are low-rank and aligned with the incoming features, so their spectral norm measures their effect on the activations.
  • For low-rank $G$, $\|\mathrm{sign}(G)\|_2=\Theta(\sqrt{n_ln_{l-1}})$, so Adam needs $\eta_l=\Theta(1/\text{fan-in})$: muP's rule. Muon's $\|UV^\top\|_2=1$ gives $\eta_l=\Theta(\sqrt{n_l/n_{l-1}})$ directly.
  • A dense random sign matrix would give only $\Theta(\sqrt n)$: the structure of the gradient sets the exponent.

$\|\mathrm{sign}(G)\|_2$ against width, $G$ of rank 1, 4, 16 and dense Gaussian. Dotted: $\mathbb E|w_1|\,n$ and $2\sqrt n$.

td2_4_optimizer_geometry §4.5 (Yang, Simon & Bernstein), §4.6 the modular norm

Claim boundary: the derivation needs updates that are low-rank or aligned with incoming features. That muP transfers in large transformers is an empirical finding consistent with this calculation, not implied by it.

Link back to L1: the parametrization decides whether a wide network learns features or stays lazy (Tensor Programs IV is in NB1 §7).

Picked up in: td2_4 §4.5 (§4.5.D is this figure; §4.5.C the derivation), §4.6 for the modular norm and the depth scaling it suggests.

§7 · Normalization and weight decay

Behind a normalization layer the loss sees only a direction. Weight decay sets the step it takes.

$$\langle\nabla L(w),w\rangle=0,\ \ \eta_{\rm eff}\approx\frac{\eta}{\|w\|^2};\qquad\cos\theta^*=1-\eta\lambda$$
  • The gradient is tangent, so a step lengthens $w$ by Pythagoras; without decay the norm only grows and the effective step decays like $1/\sqrt k$.
  • Weight decay pulls radially in. At equilibrium each block rotates by a fixed angle $\theta^*\approx\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}$ per step, whatever its gradient scale.
  • On scale-invariant weights, weight decay is a learning-rate schedule, not a regularizer of the function. Li & Arora: exactly equivalent to an exponentially increasing $\eta$.

Noisy scale-invariant toy, SGD. Four settings share $\eta\lambda=10^{-3}$ and end at the same angle; the norm relaxes on a $1/(4\eta\lambda)$ timescale, often a sizeable part of a schedule.

td2_5_normalization_weight_decay §5.1–5.2 (Kosson et al.; Li & Arora; D'Angelo et al.)

Scope: in a BatchNorm convnet almost every weight is scale-invariant; in a pre-LN transformer few matrices are exactly so, and the analysis is a model there (§5.1). Here $\lambda$ is the weight decay. The exact form is $\eta_{\rm eff}=\eta/((1-\eta\lambda)r^2)$.

Competing accounts of weight decay: the effective-learning-rate account above, versus D'Angelo et al., who argue weight decay is never useful as an explicit regularizer and whose vision mechanism is about keeping SGD's noise alive. Parameters that aren't scale-invariant can still be regularized. §5.2 and its readings give both.

Picked up in: td2_5 §5.1, §5.2 (the five-setting cell is this figure). Short route through td2_5: 30 to 45 min.

§8 · What the knobs act on: curvature

The Hessian is a bulk at zero plus about $C$ outliers. The gradient lives in the outliers; progress mostly doesn't.

GELU MLP, 1229 parameters, $C=5$ Gaussian classes, cross-entropy, stopped at loss 0.24. Full eigh.

  • $H$ = Gauss–Newton + residual. The Gauss–Newton part shares its nonzero spectrum with the loss-weighted tangent kernel, so progressive sharpening with square loss is the kernel moving.
  • The outliers are tied to class means of gradients.
  • A step along a direction of curvature $\lambda$ gains $\Delta L=-\eta g^2(1-\eta\lambda/2)$: at the edge the gain cancels. The top subspace is the bounce; the drift along the floor lives in the bulk.
  • The basin is chosen early, when the kernel moves fastest.

td2_6_curvature §6.1–6.5 (Sagun et al.; Papyan; Song, Ahn & Yun; Fort et al.; Frankle et al.)

Claim boundary, as td2_6 states it: "learning is not in the top subspace" describes a mid-training valley regime. Near interpolation with cross-entropy the notebook's own small network shows the opposite (the top subspace is where the margins grow), and the notebook says it hasn't isolated which difference from Song et al.'s setting matters. Present both.

With cross-entropy the outliers shrink as the margins grow, which is why this figure stops at loss 0.24; trained to interpolation the gap closes. The DA day's L2 picks up how these spectra are measured at scale (Hessian-vector products, Lanczos, SLQ).

Picked up in: td2_6 §6.1 (decomposition), §6.2 (kernel identity), §6.3 (bulk and outliers), §6.4 (the top subspace), §6.5 (when the basin is chosen).

§8 · What the knobs act on: order of learning

Low-order statistics first, then the rest, at a sample cost set by where the signal hides

  • Moments before cumulants. A model linear in its inputs sees only the first two moments, so a network with small pre-activations learns from Gaussian-equivalent statistics first. Train on the data and on its Gaussian clone: the early curves coincide.
  • The price of a hidden direction. A direction visible only in the $k$-th cumulant costs about $d^{\,k-1}$ samples for information exponent $k\ge3$, far fewer if it correlates with lower-order structure.
  • Spectral bias. A fixed kernel fits eigenfunction $k$ at rate $\lambda_k$: low frequencies first.
If L1 was skipped: shallow, $\dot a=s-a$; two-layer from small balanced init, $\dot a=2a(s-a)$, so the strongest structure first, after a plateau.

td2_7_learned_first §7.1–7.3 (Refinetti et al.; Bardone & Goldt; Basri et al.)
