## Content outline (this is the lecture; expand it, don't restructure it without saying why)

**Through-line.** Every estimator in the DA day touches curvature, and no one can form the $P\times P$ matrix. Everything we know about the Hessian of a real network, from "the IF needs damping" to "there are $C$ outliers" to "EK-FAC's blocks are wrong by $O(1)$", comes from one primitive: multiplying the Hessian by a vector. The lecture builds that primitive up from forward- and reverse-mode autodiff, then shows what you can compute from it (solves, top eigenpairs, traces, the whole spectral density by SLQ), what the spectra of trained networks look like, and how the 2026 paper gets the same picture from a 100B-parameter model. It closes by saying what the spectrum tells you about attribution.

### 0. Title and where we are (1 min)
- Title. One slide of context: morning gave the formula; this half hour is about *computing* with the curvature matrix inside it. DA2 after this.

### 1. Why you never form $H$, and what you can ask instead (2 min)
- $P\sim10^8$ to $10^{11}$. $H$ has $P^2$ entries: a 1B model's Hessian would be $10^{18}$ numbers. Even the $C$-class toy spectra in TD were computed on nets with $P\sim10^3$–$10^4$.
- The three questions that only need products $v\mapsto Hv$: (i) solve $(H+\lambda I)x=g$ (what an IF needs); (ii) top-$k$ eigenpairs (sharpness, edge of stability, Arnoldi-IFs); (iii) the spectral density $\rho(\lambda)=\frac1P\sum_j\delta(\lambda-\lambda_j)$ (what SLQ gives).
- Picture: a box labelled "$v\mapsto Hv$" with three arrows out.

### 2. Forward and reverse mode: jvp and vjp (3 min)
- A function $f:\mathbb R^P\to\mathbb R^m$ with Jacobian $J$. **jvp**: $v\mapsto Jv$, one forward pass carrying tangents alongside the primal (forward mode, dual numbers). **vjp**: $u\mapsto J^\top u$, one backward pass (reverse mode). The gradient of a scalar loss is the vjp with $u=1$.
- Cost: each is a small constant times a forward pass, regardless of $P$ or $m$. jvp memory is that of a forward pass; vjp needs the activations stored (the backward pass's memory).
- Names in code: `jax.jvp`, `jax.vjp`; `torch.func.jvp`, `torch.func.vjp` (check the current API names).
- Speaker note: this is the only slide of "autodiff theory"; the point is that both products are cheap and *exact*.

### 3. Pearlmutter's trick: $Hv$ for the price of two gradients (4 min)
- Two equivalent routes, both exact:
  - reverse-over-reverse: $Hv=\nabla_w\big(\nabla L(w)^\top v\big)$, "double backward". Needs the backward graph kept.
  - forward-over-reverse: $Hv=\frac{d}{dr}\nabla L(w+rv)\big|_{r=0}$, a jvp of the gradient function. Pearlmutter (1994), *Fast exact multiplication by the Hessian*, Neural Computation 6(1): the $\mathcal R\{\cdot\}$ operator applied to backprop. Cost about $2$–$3\times$ one gradient; memory about that of a gradient.
- **Finite differences** as the third route: $Hv\approx\frac{\nabla L(w+\epsilon v)-\nabla L(w-\epsilon v)}{2\epsilon}$. Two gradient evaluations, no autodiff plumbing, works with any training loop (that is why the 2026 paper uses it under FSDP). Error $\le\frac{\epsilon^2}{6}\|D^3L\|\,\|v\|^3+O(\epsilon_{\rm mach}\|\nabla L\|/\epsilon)$ (truncation vs round-off), optimum $\epsilon^*\propto\epsilon_{\rm mach}^{1/3}$. In fp32 that gives $\epsilon^*\sim10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ and $\sim10^{-5}$ relative error; in bf16 $\epsilon^*\sim10^{-1}$ and $\sim10^{-2}$ error. Verify these against 2602.00816 §3.1 (Theorem 3.1, Remark 3.2); note that the paper's appendix states a $1/4$ exponent while the theorem gives $1/3$; use $1/3$ for the central difference with the paper's error model and say nothing about the discrepancy on the slide.
- **Gauss–Newton products** for free: $Gv=\sum_iJ_i^\top H_{y,i}J_iv$ is one jvp, one small $m\times m$ multiply, one vjp per example (Schraudolph 2002). This is the matrix DA1 §4 and EK-FAC actually use, and it is PSD, so CG converges.
- Figure (make it): on a small MLP (a few thousand parameters), relative error of the finite-difference $Hv$ against autodiff $Hv$ as a function of $\epsilon$, in float32 and in bfloat16 (emulate bf16 by casting the gradients). Expect the V shape with minima near $10^{-3}$ (fp32) and $10^{-1}$ (bf16). This is the lecture's "trust the primitive" figure.

### 4. First things you can do with $Hv$ (2 min)
- Power iteration for $\lambda_{\max}$: converges like $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^k$; slow when outliers are close, hence Lanczos.
- Hutchinson's trace: $\operatorname{tr}H=\mathbb E[v^\top Hv]$ for $v$ with i.i.d. $\pm1$ (Rademacher) entries; variance $2\big(\|H\|_F^2-\sum_jH_{jj}^2\big)$. One line on PyHessian (Yao, Gholami, Keutzer, Mahoney 2020) as the standard small-model toolbox: top-$k$, trace, density.
- Conjugate gradients and LiSSA (Agarwal, Bullins, Hazan 2017) as the *solve* route for IFs: Koh & Liang 2017 used both; each query costs hundreds to thousands of HVPs. (Point to DA2 stop 1's "why EK-FAC" paragraph; do not derive.)

### 5. Lanczos (4 min)
- Krylov subspace $\mathcal K_m(H,v)=\operatorname{span}\{v,Hv,\dots,H^{m-1}v\}$. Lanczos builds an orthonormal basis $Q_m=[q_1\dots q_m]$ by a three-term recurrence, $Hq_k=\beta_{k-1}q_{k-1}+\alpha_kq_k+\beta_kq_{k+1}$, so that $Q_m^\top HQ_m=T_m$ is $m\times m$ tridiagonal. Cost: $m$ HVPs plus $O(mP)$ vector work.
- Ritz values (eigenvalues of $T_m$) converge to the *extreme* eigenvalues of $H$ first, geometrically in $m$ (Kaniel–Paige–Saad). Outliers are resolved in a handful of iterations; the interior of the bulk is not resolved eigenvalue by eigenvalue, only as a distribution. This asymmetry is the whole story of what SLQ can and cannot see.
- Floating point: the $q_k$ lose orthogonality, and eigenvalues that have converged reappear as *ghost* copies. Fix: full reorthogonalization, which needs the whole basis $Q_m$ in memory ($mP$ numbers: for $P=10^{11}$ in bf16 that is 200 GB per basis vector). This is the memory wall that sets $m$ at LLM scale.
- Arnoldi is the non-symmetric cousin. Schioppa, Zablotskaia, Vilar, Sokolov (AAAI 2022), *Scaling up influence functions*: top-$k$ Hessian eigenpairs by Arnoldi, then IFs in that $k$-dimensional subspace. Mark as a Claude-suggested extra in the readings (CRW has not vetted it) but it belongs on the slide as the "eigenpairs route" to IFs.
- Code pointer: TD `td2_2` computes the top eigenvalue by Lanczos on HVPs (`top_eig_lanczos`), so students have seen the code.

### 6. From Lanczos to the density: stochastic Lanczos quadrature (5 min)
- Target: $\int f(\lambda)\rho(\lambda)\,d\lambda=\frac1P\operatorname{tr}f(H)=\frac1P\,\mathbb E_v[v^\top f(H)v]$ for $v$ with $\mathbb E[vv^\top]=I$.
- For a fixed unit $v$, $v^\top f(H)v=\sum_j(v\cdot u_j)^2f(\lambda_j)=\int f\,d\mu_v$: an integral against the discrete measure $\mu_v$ with nodes $\lambda_j$ and weights $(v\cdot u_j)^2$. Golub–Welsch: the $m$-point Gauss quadrature rule for $\mu_v$ is given exactly by the Lanczos tridiagonal, with nodes the Ritz values $\theta_k$ and weights $\tau_k^2=(e_1^\top y_k)^2$, the squared first components of $T_m$'s eigenvectors. So $v^\top f(H)v\approx e_1^\top f(T_m)e_1=\sum_k\tau_k^2f(\theta_k)$, exact for polynomials of degree $\le2m-1$ (Golub & Meurant, *Matrices, Moments and Quadrature*).
- SLQ (Ubaru, Chen, Saad 2017): average over $n_v$ probes, $\hat\rho(\lambda)=\frac1{n_v}\sum_v\sum_k\tau_k^2\,\delta(\lambda-\theta_k)$, then smooth with a Gaussian of width $\sigma$ for plotting. Two sources of error: quadrature ($m$) and Monte Carlo ($n_v$); Chen, Trogdon, Ubaru (2021) give the modern error analysis. Typical settings in the deep-learning papers: Ghorbani et al. 2019 used $m=90$, $n_v=10$ (check), Granziol & Juarev 2026 used $m$ as small as 10 at 120B.
- What it resolves and what it doesn't: outliers and the bulk's shape, yes; individual bulk eigenvalues, no; and the mass very near zero is where the quadrature is coarsest, which is exactly where the damping $\lambda$ of an IF acts. Say this plainly: SLQ tells you *how much* mass sits near zero, not its fine structure.
- Figure (make it): a small MLP ($P\approx2000$–$5000$) trained a few hundred steps on a $C=5$ class toy problem, exact spectrum by `eigh` vs SLQ with ($m=30$, $n_v=10$) and ($m=90$, $n_v=10$): one panel, log-density, outliers marked. Second panel: the same with $m=10$, showing the outliers are already right and the bulk is coarse. This is the lecture's "what SLQ sees" figure. Verify the SLQ implementation against `eigh` numerically (moments to a few percent) and log it.
- Code pointer: `google/spectral-density` (Ghorbani et al.'s released code, TF/JAX) and PyHessian both implement this.

### 7. What the spectra of trained networks look like (4 min)
- One slide, one picture, three papers CRW already uses in TD/MDL:
  - Sagun, Evci, Güney, Dauphin, Bottou (2017): bulk at zero plus a few outliers; the outlier count tracks the number of classes $C$. (In TD `td2_6`.)
  - Ghorbani, Krishnan, Xiao (ICML 2019): the first ImageNet-scale densities by SLQ. Without BatchNorm, outliers appear early in training and the gradient lives in their span, which slows training; BatchNorm suppresses the outliers. Large negative eigenvalues early that fade. (In MDL4 and TD `td2_6`.)
  - Papyan (2018, 2019; JMLR 2020): the outliers come from the Gauss–Newton term's class-mean structure, $\approx C$ of them, with a further $C(C-1)$ "minibulk"; the residual $H-G$ is bulk. TD `td2_6` derives the simplest exact version of this.
- Consequence for the IF (one line, picked up in §9): the curvature eigenbasis has $\sim C$ directions with eigenvalue $O(1)$ or larger and $P-C$ directions near zero. $(G+\lambda I)^{-1}$ is $\approx\lambda^{-1}$ on almost all of parameter space.
- Transformers: say honestly that the picture above is for classifiers with square or cross-entropy loss and $C$ classes, and that for language models $C$ is the vocabulary, so "$C$ outliers" is not a small number; the LLM-scale spectra below are the first direct look. *(Claude-suggested extra, flag it as such:)* Zhang et al. 2024, *Why transformers need Adam: a Hessian perspective*, blockwise SLQ showing strong block heterogeneity (attention vs MLP vs embedding) in transformers; include only if you can verify the claim from the paper, else drop.

### 8. The 100B-parameter spectrum: Granziol & Juarev 2026 (5 min)
- The obstacle at scale: a Pearlmutter HVP needs the autodiff graph of a sharded model; under FSDP/ZeRO a naive HVP all-gathers the full parameters, and the Krylov basis for reorthogonalization is $m$ full-model copies.
- Their move: the central finite difference computed **shard-locally**. Two gradient passes over the same batch with in-place $\pm\epsilon v$ on each shard, one scalar all-reduce; cost $T_{Hv}\approx2T_{\rm grad}$, so a Lanczos iteration costs about two training steps. Reported single-node scaling on Qwen-0.6B: 1 to 8 GPUs gives $\approx5.7\times$ (about 72% parallel efficiency); Lanczos overhead over the HVP itself is 1–2%. (Verify every number in 2602.00816 §5 and Tables 2–5 before you put it on a slide; drop any you cannot confirm, and do not repeat the paper's "44×" claim, which its own Table 5 does not support.)
- HessFormer (2025) in one line: the same author's single-node distributed SLQ on a 70B DeepSeek model, the previous record.
- Findings to show (verify each against §6–§7 and the figures):
  1. **The finite-difference step is a knob that can fake a spectrum.** On Pythia-160M, $\epsilon=10^{-5}$ and $\epsilon=10^{-2}$ give a random-matrix-looking bulk with no outliers; $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$ give clear outliers. Too small amplifies round-off, too large averages the curvature along the line (their Theorem 3.3 writes the second difference as a triangular-kernel average of the Hessian along $w+tv$). Tie this to the §3 figure.
  2. **Subsampling the data is cheap:** a 30% subset gives essentially the same spectrum at the same $\epsilon$.
  3. **Larger models look more stable in $\epsilon$:** Pythia-1.4B in bf16 gives a consistent spectrum for $10^{-4}\le\epsilon\le10^{-3}$. State it as a single comparison, which is all they show.
  4. **gpt-oss-120b:** the headline spectrum, with large negative eigenvalues, from only 10 Lanczos iterations without reorthogonalization. The authors call it indicative. Say so, and say why 10: memory for the basis.
  5. **Block-diagonal curvature can be wrong by $O(1)$.** They compare $Hv$ restricted to a parameter block against the block-diagonal product (the block's own $H_{bb}v_b$) for Gaussian $v$: on Qwen-0.6B with 28 blocks, relative error $\approx0.94$ and cosine $\approx0.31$; on a 1.3B code model after fine-tuning, relative error $\approx1.2$ and cosine $\approx0$. The off-diagonal blocks carry as much of $Hv$ as the diagonal ones.
- The caveat that changes a conclusion (one line, speaker notes): this test is on $H$ with random probes; EK-FAC approximates $G$ and what matters for an IF is $(G+\lambda I)^{-1}$ applied to *gradients*, which may live in a different subspace. The test is a warning, not a verdict. DA2 stop 1's code cell measures the IF-level error of block-diagonal/K-FAC/EK-FAC on a small net (rank correlation with the exact IF about 0.65–0.75 for block-diagonal and 0.3–0.45 for the Kronecker ones); quote those numbers from the built `da2_methods.html`.
- Scale table (one slide): PyHessian / Ghorbani 2019 at $10^7$–$10^8$ (exact HVP, single GPU); Schioppa 2022 Arnoldi at $\sim10^8$; Grosse et al. 2023 EK-FAC at $5\times10^{10}$ (approximate curvature, no HVPs); HessFormer 2025 at $7\times10^{10}$ (exact, single node); Granziol & Juarev 2026 at $1.2\times10^{11}$ (exact, FD-HVP, multi-node). Verify each entry; a wrong number here is worse than a missing row.

### 9. Back to attribution: what the spectrum tells you (3 min)
- Three uses, each one line, each pointing at a DA section:
  1. **Choosing $\lambda$.** The filter $F(s)=1/(s+\lambda)$ (DA1 §6): $\lambda$ should sit where you want to stop trusting the curvature, between the bulk edge and the outliers; a density plot is the only principled way to see where that is. DA2's code uses $\lambda=0.1\times$ the mean eigenvalue; Grosse et al. choose by validation. (Check what DA1/DA2 actually say and match it.)
  2. **Knowing what the IF is made of.** With $\sim C$ large directions and a bulk at zero, the damped IF is $-\frac1\lambda\nabla\phi^\top\nabla L_i$ (a gradient dot product, TracIn-like) on the bulk plus a curvature-corrected term on the outliers. This is why gradient-similarity baselines are not crazy and why they fail exactly on the outlier directions.
  3. **Auditing the approximation.** EK-FAC assumes block-diagonal plus Kronecker; the 2026 block test says the first assumption already costs $O(1)$ in $Hv$. Our DA2 code says the Kronecker step costs more than the block step at the IF level. Both are measurements on specific models; neither is a theorem.
