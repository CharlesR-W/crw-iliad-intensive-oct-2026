# Training Dynamics reading list: verified links (v1)

How this was checked (2026-10-08): every ID or URL was looked up in the arXiv API, Crossref/DOI, OpenReview, PMLR/NeurIPS/JMLR, or the page itself. Items 1, 4–7, 9–11 and 13–18, plus 12, 22–25, 27, 29, 43 (Baldi–Hornik), 44 and 48, were checked against the papers' full text, not only the abstracts. Anything not confirmed is marked **UNSURE**. Notation: η = learning rate (h in some papers), S = sharpness (top Hessian eigenvalue λ_max).

1. Hairer, Lubich, Wanner, *Geometric Numerical Integration* (2nd ed., Springer 2006). https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-30666-8 — Ch. IX, "Backward Error Analysis and Structure Preservation" (pp. 337–388, https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-30666-8_9), shows that a one-step method Φ_h is, up to any order, the exact time-h flow of a modified ODE ẏ = f + h f₂ + h² f₃ + … . The series usually diverges, but truncating it optimally leaves only an exponentially small error (IX.7). For symplectic methods the modified equation is Hamiltonian, which gives near-conservation of energy over exponentially long times (IX.8). (Chapter title, pages and section titles verified; theorem numbers and constants **UNSURE**, because Springer is paywalled.)
2. Barrett & Dherin, Implicit Gradient Regularization (ICLR 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.11162 — Uses backward error analysis to show that GD with step η follows, to O(η²), gradient flow on the modified loss L̃ = L + (η/4)‖∇L‖². This implicit regularizer biases GD toward flat minima, and adding it explicitly as a regularizer reproduces the effect.
3. Smith, Dherin, Barrett, De, On the origin of implicit regularization in SGD (ICLR 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.12176 — For SGD with random shuffling over one epoch of m minibatches, the mean iterate follows gradient flow on L̃_SGD = L + (η/4m) Σ_k ‖∇L̂_k‖² = L + (η/4)‖∇L‖² + (η/4m) Σ_k ‖∇L̂_k − ∇L‖². In expectation this is L + (η/4)‖∇L‖² + ((N−B)/(N−1))·(η/4B)·tr Cov(per-example gradients), so the noise-driven part scales like η/B.
4. Ghosh, Lyu, Zhang, Wang, Implicit regularization in heavy-ball momentum accelerated SGD (ICLR 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00849 — Shows that heavy-ball GD x_{k+1} = x_k − η∇L + β(x_k − x_{k−1}), once the β^k transients have died out, is O(η²)-close to the rescaled modified flow ẋ = −(1/(1−β)) ∇[L + η(1+β)/(4(1−β)²)·‖∇L‖²]. At the same effective learning rate η/(1−β), this regularizer is (1+β)/(1−β) times stronger than GD's. For SGD with momentum, the batch-averaged regularizer is η(1+β)/(4(1−β)³)‖∇L‖² + η/(4(1−β)²)·E‖∇L_j − ∇L‖². (The 4th author is Rongrong Wang.)
5. Kovachki & Stuart, Continuous time analysis of momentum methods (JMLR 22(17), 2021). https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v22/19-466.html (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04285) — At fixed momentum λ and small η, heavy ball and Nesterov are O(η)-close to the rescaled gradient flow u̇ = −(1−λ)⁻¹∇Φ. They are also O(η)-close to a damped second-order ODE with mass of order η, ηαü + (1−λ)u̇ = −∇Φ. Finally, there is an attractive invariant manifold on which the dynamics are O(η²)-close to the flow of a modified potential Φ + (η/2)c‖∇Φ‖². (This is not a λ→1 limit. Flag: a subagent's symbolic check suggests the printed constant c = λ̄(λ̄ − a + ½) has a sign error and should be λ̄(λ̄ − a − ½), which matches item 4 and Barrett–Dherin at λ = 0; this is our own diagnosis, not a published erratum, so **verify before teaching**.)
6. Cattaneo, Klusowski, Shigida, On the implicit bias of Adam (ICML 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00079 (PMLR https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/cattaneo24a.html) — Backward error analysis of Adam and RMSProp (RMSProp is the β = 0 case). For small ε, Adam is close to sign-like flow on L + (η/2)[(1+β₁)/(1−β₁) − (1+β₂)/(1−β₂)]·‖∇L‖₁ (more exactly, a perturbed one-norm Σ√(∂ᵢL² + ε)). With the usual β₂ > β₁ the coefficient is negative, so Adam *anti*-penalizes the gradient one-norm. For large ε the penalty reverts to a GD-like two-norm penalty.
7. Rosca, Wu, Qin, Dherin, On a continuous time model of GD dynamics and instability in deep learning (TMLR 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01952 — Sums the backward-error-analysis terms built only from ∇L and ∇²L to obtain the "principal flow" θ̇ = Σᵢ [log(1−ηλᵢ)/(ηλᵢ)]·(∇Lᵀuᵢ)uᵢ in the Hessian eigenbasis, which is exact for quadratics. Its coefficient is real for ηλ < 1, complex (oscillatory) for 1 < ηλ < 2, and has positive real part (repelling, like gradient *ascent*) for ηλ > 2. This explains GD's instability and edge-of-stability behaviour, which gradient flow and the IGR flow cannot capture.
8. Li, Tai, E, Stochastic modified equations and adaptive stochastic gradient algorithms (ICML 2017). https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06251 — Proves that SGD is a weak order-1 approximation of dX = −∇f dt + √(ηΣ) dW, and order 2 of dX = −∇(f + (η/4)‖∇f‖²)dt + √(ηΣ) dW. Using optimal control on this SME, it derives adaptive learning-rate (cSGD) and momentum (cMSGD) schedules.
   Follow-up: Li, Tai, E, SME and dynamics of stochastic gradient algorithms I: Mathematical foundations (JMLR 20(40), 2019). https://jmlr.org/papers/v20/17-526.html (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01558) — A rigorous weak-approximation framework for general expectation objectives. It gives order-1 and order-2 SMEs for SGD, an underdamped-Langevin-type SME for momentum SGD, and Nesterov variants.
9. Li, Malladi, Arora, On the validity of modeling SGD with SDEs (NeurIPS 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12470 — Introduces SVAG, a cheap simulation that converges to the Itô SDE at finite η. For scale-invariant nets with weight decay at equilibrium, it proves the SDE and SGD equilibria are *not* C-close if the noise-to-signal ratio N∞/G∞ = E tr Σ / E‖∇L‖² < 1/(C²−1), roughly "the gradient norm must be smaller than its variance". It also proves the linear scaling rule fails once the scale factor κ > C²(1 + N∞/G∞), which gives a predicted critical batch size B* ≈ C²·B·N/G measured from one small-batch run.
10. Malladi, Lyu, Panigrahi, Arora, On the SDEs and scaling rules for adaptive gradient algorithms (NeurIPS 2022). https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10287 — Derives order-1 weak SDEs for RMSprop and Adam, in which one step corresponds to time η², not η. This gives the square-root scaling rule for batch size B → κB: η → √κ·η, 1−β₁ → κ(1−β₁), 1−β₂ → κ(1−β₂), ε → ε/√κ (RMSprop: the same, with its single β).
11. McCandlish, Kaplan, Amodei, OpenAI Dota Team, An empirical model of large-batch training (2018). https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06162 — A quadratic one-step model gives ε_opt(B) = ε_max/(1 + B_noise/B), with ε_max = ‖G‖²/(GᵀHG), B_noise = tr(HΣ)/(GᵀHG), and the Hessian-free proxy B_simple = tr(Σ)/‖G‖², where Σ is the per-example gradient covariance. It also gives the steps/examples tradeoff (S/S_min − 1)(E/E_min − 1) = 1, with critical batch B_crit = E_min/S_min ≈ B_noise.
12. Goh, Why momentum really works (Distill 2017). https://distill.pub/2017/momentum/ — For heavy ball on a convex quadratic, each eigencomponent follows a 2×2 linear recurrence. Stability requires 0 < αλ < 2 + 2β, critical damping is β = (1−√(αλ))², and the optimal α, β improve the convergence rate from (κ−1)/(κ+1) to (√κ−1)/(√κ+1).
13. Cohen, Kaur, Li, Kolter, Talwalkar, Gradient descent on neural networks typically occurs at the edge of stability (ICLR 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00065 — Empirically, under full-batch GD the sharpness rises ("progressive sharpening") until it reaches 2/η. It then hovers at or just above 2/η while the loss decreases non-monotonically over short timescales but steadily over long ones. With momentum the threshold becomes (2+2β)/η for heavy ball.
14. Cohen, Ghorbani, Krishnan, Agarwal, …, Gilmer, Adaptive gradient methods at the edge of stability (2022 preprint; NeurIPS 2023 HeavyTails workshop). https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.14484 — Adaptive methods reach an EoS regime in which the *preconditioned* sharpness λ_max(P⁻¹H) equilibrates at the stability threshold of the optimizer with P held fixed. For Adam's EMA-style momentum this is (2+2β₁)/((1−β₁)η), which equals 38/η at β₁ = 0.9 (RMSProp 2/η). The raw sharpness keeps rising, and the preconditioner adapts to compensate. (Not a NeurIPS 2022 main-track paper.)
15. Cohen, Damian, Talwalkar, Kolter, Lee, Understanding optimization in deep learning with central flows (ICLR 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24206 — Your formula is right up to a factor of η, which is there because they rescale time so that t counts steps. With one unstable eigenvalue the central flow is dw/dt = −η[∇L(w) + ½σ²(t)∇S(w)], with σ² = max(0, 2⟨∇S, −∇L⟩/‖∇S‖²), the unique value that holds dS/dt = 0 at S = 2/η. Equivalently, this is gradient flow with ∇L projected orthogonally to ∇S. In general, dw/dt = −η[∇L + ½∇⟨H, Σ⟩], where Σ ⪰ 0 is supported on the 2/η eigenspace and found from a semidefinite complementarity problem. Σ models the covariance of the oscillation, and the time-averaged loss ≈ L(w(t)) + tr Σ/η. They also derive RMSProp central flows, whose implicit preconditioner solves a convex program.
16. Damian, Nichani, Lee, Self-stabilization: the implicit bias of GD at the edge of stability (ICLR 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15594 — A cubic Taylor expansion along the top eigenvector u gives ∇S = ∇³L(u,u). As oscillation along u grows, it drives the iterate down ∇S, so the sharpness self-stabilizes. They prove that GD at EoS tracks projected GD on the constrained problem min L subject to S(θ) ≤ 2/η (and ∇L·u = 0). Their predicted loss is L(θ†) + x²/η, where x is the oscillation amplitude.
17. Regis & Chewi, Rod Flow: a continuous-time model for gradient descent at the edge of stability (arXiv, Feb 2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01480 — Tracks the midpoint w̄ = (w_{t+1}+w_t)/2 and the half-difference δ ("rod") of consecutive GD iterates. Using exact difference equations plus backward error analysis, it derives explicit ODEs for w̄ and Σ ≈ δδᵀ that need only gradients and Hessian-vector products, with no complementarity problem. These ODEs recover the 2/η threshold on quadratics and give a stable oscillating state (self-stabilization) on quartics. To second order in δ they approximately reduce to the central flow. (No venue found. Related work: Regis & Chewi's Rod Flow for Adam, https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06821.)
18. Andreyev & Beneventano, Edge of Stochastic Stability: revisiting the edge of stability for SGD (arXiv, 2024; cite v5, Dec 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20553 — Under mini-batch SGD, the quantity that rises and plateaus at 2/η (for any batch size) is **Batch Sharpness** = E_B[∇L_Bᵀ H_B ∇L_B / ‖∇L_B‖²]. This is the expected Rayleigh quotient of each mini-batch Hessian along its own mini-batch gradient, with the expectation outside the ratio; earlier arXiv versions wrote this definition incorrectly. Meanwhile the full-batch λ_max settles lower, with a gap that depends on batch size. (Venue **UNSURE**.)
   Follow-up: **Correction:** arXiv 2604.21016 is *not* by Andreyev & Beneventano. It is Liao, Kolomvaki, Kyrillidis, SGD at the edge of stability: the stochastic sharpness gap (Apr 2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21016 — It extends self-stabilization to SGD and derives E[S] = 2/η − ηβσ_u²/(4α) + O(·), with α = −⟨∇L, ∇S⟩, β = ‖∇S⊥‖², and σ_u² = Var_B⟨g_B, u⟩ ∝ 1/b. So the full-batch sharpness sits below 2/η by a gap that shrinks with batch size b. (Its appendix numbers look internally inconsistent; read with care.) A possibly relevant paper by Andreyev, Beneventano et al. is Momentum further constrains sharpness at the edge of stochastic stability (ICML 2026), https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/andreyev26a.html (**UNSURE**, seen only in a search result).
19. Lewkowycz, Bahri, Dyer, Sohl-Dickstein, Gur-Ari, The large learning rate phase of deep learning: the catapult mechanism (2020). https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.02218 — In a solvable wide two-layer model with MSE loss and initial NTK top eigenvalue λ₀, there are three phases: lazy (η < 2/λ₀, kernel constant), catapult (2/λ₀ < η < 4/λ₀: the loss first grows, then the curvature drops until training converges in a flatter region), and divergent (η > η_max). Empirically η_max ≈ 12/λ₀ for ReLU nets, and the best performance is often in the catapult phase.
20. Wen, Li, Wang, Hall, Liang, Ma, Understanding warmup-stable-decay learning rates: a river valley loss landscape perspective (2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05192 — Models the pretraining loss as a "river valley" and argues that the high constant learning rate makes fast progress along the river while oscillating across the steep valley walls, which hides that progress. The decay phase then damps the oscillation, producing WSD's sharp loss drop. The paper proposes WSD-S. (Venue **UNSURE**; arXiv only per its metadata.)
21. Kalra & Barkeshli, Why warmup the learning rate? Underlying mechanisms and improvements (NeurIPS 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09405 — Through experiments with SGD and Adam, shows that warmup's main benefit is letting the network tolerate a larger target η by moving it into better-conditioned (lower-sharpness) regions. Which regime warmup operates in depends on whether training starts in progressive sharpening or sharpness reduction. They propose choosing η_init via the catapult to shorten or eliminate warmup, plus a variance initialization for Adam.
22. Bernstein & Newhouse, Old optimizer, new norm: an anthology (OPT 2024 workshop). https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.20325 — With their EMAs switched off, Adam is sign descent, i.e. steepest descent in ℓ∞ on the flattened weights (a max-of-max operator norm), and Shampoo is steepest descent under the per-layer spectral norm (update UVᵀ). Prodigy is sign descent with an automatically warmed-up step size. The paper argues for assigning operator norms to tensors according to their role.
23. Jordan et al., Muon: an optimizer for hidden layers in neural networks (blog, Dec 8 2024). https://kellerjordan.github.io/posts/muon/ — Muon replaces the SGD-momentum (Nesterov) update of each 2D hidden-layer weight with an approximation of its nearest semi-orthogonal matrix UVᵀ. This is computed by 5 quintic Newton–Schulz iterations X ← aX + b(XXᵀ)X + c(XXᵀ)²X with (a, b, c) = (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315). Embeddings, the output head and scalar parameters use AdamW. The post reports CIFAR-10 and NanoGPT speedrun records. (BibTeX authors: Jordan, Jin, Boza, You, Cesista, Newhouse, Bernstein.)
24. Yang, Simon, Bernstein, A spectral condition for feature learning (2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17813 — Feature learning in every layer requires the spectral norms of the weights and of their updates to scale as ‖W_ℓ‖_*, ‖ΔW_ℓ‖_* = Θ(√(fan_out/fan_in)). This gives an elementary derivation of μP. (Venue **UNSURE**; arXiv only.)
25. Large, Liu, Huh, Bahng, Isola, Bernstein, Scalable optimization in the modular norm (NeurIPS 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14813 — Defines the "modular norm" recursively over module composition and concatenation, using each module's mass and sensitivity. Normalizing updates in this norm makes the learning rate transfer across width and depth. They prove the gradient is Lipschitz in the modular norm, with recursively computable constants, and release the Modula package.
26. Arjovsky & Bottou, Towards principled methods for training GANs (ICLR 2017). https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.04862 — Proves that when the data and generator distributions have (nearly) disjoint low-dimensional supports, a perfect discriminator exists and generator gradients vanish (original loss) or become unstable (−log D loss). It proposes adding noise and using softer distances.
   Arjovsky, Chintala, Bottou, Wasserstein GAN (ICML 2017). https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.07875 (PMLR https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/arjovsky17a.html) — Replaces JS divergence with the Earth-Mover/Wasserstein-1 distance, which is continuous and almost everywhere differentiable in the generator parameters. A weight-clipped Lipschitz critic estimates it through Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, which gives more stable training and meaningful loss curves.
27. Amari, Natural gradient works efficiently in learning (Neural Computation 10(2):251–276, 1998). https://doi.org/10.1162/089976698300017746 — In a Riemannian parameter space, the steepest-descent direction is the natural gradient G⁻¹∇L, with G the Fisher metric for statistical models. Online natural-gradient learning is Fisher-efficient, i.e. asymptotically as good as optimal batch estimation, and may weaken plateaus. Worked examples: perceptrons, blind source separation, deconvolution. (Formula confirmed from secondary sources only, since the publisher page is paywalled; whether the paper explicitly claims reparametrization invariance is **UNSURE**.)
28. Gur-Ari, Roberts, Dyer, Gradient descent happens in a tiny subspace (2018). https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.04754 — After a short transient, the gradient lies mostly in the span of the top-k Hessian eigenvectors, with k = number of classes, and this subspace stays nearly fixed during training. The effect is illustrated in a solvable model.
29. Song, Ahn, Yun, Does SGD really happen in tiny subspaces? (ICLR 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16002 — With P_k projecting onto the top-k Hessian eigenspace, "Dom-SGD" (update −ηP_k g) stops decreasing the training loss, and the loss slowly rises over long runs. "Bulk-SGD" (update −η(I−P_k)g) trains as well as full SGD, so the gradient's alignment with the dominant subspace is spurious. They attribute the alignment to gradient noise in an ill-conditioned valley for small-LR SGD, and to EoS self-stabilization only for GD at EoS. The same pattern holds for GD, SAM, momentum SGD and Adam.
30. Sagun, Evci, Güney, Dauphin, Bottou, Empirical analysis of the Hessian of over-parametrized neural networks (ICLR 2018 workshop). https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04454 — The Hessian spectrum is a bulk concentrated near zero plus a few outliers. Adding parameters only scales the bulk, while changing the data (more clusters, less separable) changes only the outliers. Large- and small-batch solutions can be connected without barriers.
   Papyan, Traces of class/cross-class structure pervade deep learning spectra (JMLR 21, 2020). https://jmlr.org/papers/v21/20-933.html (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.11865) — Shows that the outliers ("spikes") and "bumps" beyond the bulk of deep-net Hessian, Fisher and feature spectra come from a class/cross-class decomposition. It proves that the outlier-to-bulk ratio of the Fisher spectrum predicts misclassification in multinomial logistic regression, and proposes a KFAC correction.
31. Fort, Dziugaite, Paul, Kharaghani, Roy, Ganguli, Deep learning versus kernel learning (NeurIPS 2020). https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.15110 — Many measures of landscape geometry and NTK evolution move together. A chaotic transient in the first 2–3 epochs fixes the final linearly connected basin, while the NTK changes fast and becomes about 3× better than the initial NTK. Afterwards the NTK moves at constant velocity, and kernel regression with it matches full training after 15–45% of training time.
32. Frankle, Dziugaite, Roy, Carbin, Linear mode connectivity and the lottery ticket hypothesis (ICML 2020). https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05671 — Defines instability to SGD noise as the error barrier on the linear path between two copies trained from the same point with different data orders. Networks become stable (linearly mode-connected) early in training, and IMP lottery-ticket subnetworks reach full accuracy only when they are stable. For large-scale settings this requires rewinding to an early iterate rather than initialization.
33. Martens & Grosse, Optimizing neural networks with Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (ICML 2015). https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.05671 (PMLR https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/martens15.html) — Approximates each layer's Fisher block as a Kronecker product A ⊗ G of the second moments of input activations and of back-propagated gradients, and approximates the inverse Fisher as block-diagonal or block-tridiagonal. This gives a cheap, invertible natural-gradient preconditioner that works in stochastic settings.
34. Refinetti, Ingrosso, Goldt, Neural networks trained with SGD learn distributions of increasing complexity (ICML 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11567 — Demonstrates a "distributional simplicity bias": networks first classify using low-order input statistics (mean, covariance) and only later exploit higher-order cumulants. Shown analytically in a solvable model and empirically in deep CNNs and transformers.
35. Bardone & Goldt, Sliding down the stairs: how correlated latent variables accelerate learning (ICML 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08602 — Recovering a direction from the order-p cumulant with online SGD costs ~d^{p−1} samples, as in tensor PCA. The paper derives nearly sharp sample-complexity thresholds showing that correlations between latent variables along directions in different cumulants (e.g. covariance and higher-order cumulants) let a single neuron learn the hard higher-order direction much faster.
36. Rahaman et al., On the spectral bias of neural networks (ICML 2019). https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.08734 — Fourier analysis of ReLU networks, which are continuous piecewise-linear functions, shows that they are biased toward low frequencies: low-frequency components are learned first and are more robust. Learning high frequencies gets easier as the data manifold becomes more complex.
   Xu, Zhang, Luo, Xiao, Ma, Frequency Principle: Fourier analysis sheds light on deep neural networks (Commun. Comput. Phys. 2020). https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.06523 — The "F-Principle": DNNs fit target functions from low to high frequency, the opposite of the high-frequency-first convergence of Jacobi-type solvers. Explained by the regularity of the activation functions. The original F-Principle paper is Xu, Zhang, Xiao, Training behavior of deep neural network in frequency domain (ICONIP 2019), https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.01251.
37. Kadkhodaie, Guth, Simoncelli, Mallat, Generalization in diffusion models arises from geometry-adaptive harmonic representations (ICLR 2024, Outstanding Paper). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02557 — Two denoisers trained on non-overlapping data subsets converge to nearly the same score, so the model transitions from memorization to generalization as training-set size grows. The learned denoiser acts as shrinkage in a "geometry-adaptive harmonic basis" (oscillations along contours and in smooth regions), an inductive bias that is near-optimal for classes where such bases are optimal.
38. Achille, Rovere, Soatto, Critical learning periods in deep networks (ICLR 2019). https://openreview.net/forum?id=BkeStsCcKQ (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08856) — A temporary input deficit such as blur early in training permanently impairs final performance, as in animal critical periods, whereas later or low-level-preserving deficits do not. The Fisher information of the weights rises and then falls during training, which the paper calls a loss of "information plasticity".
   Dohare, Hernandez-Garcia, Lan, Rahman, Mahmood, Sutton, Loss of plasticity in deep continual learning (Nature 632:768–774, 2024). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07711-7 (preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13812) — In continual learning on ImageNet task sequences and in RL, standard deep-learning methods gradually lose plasticity until they learn no better than a shallow network. "Continual backprop", which keeps reinitializing a small fraction of low-utility units, maintains plasticity indefinitely.
39. Saxe, McClelland, Ganguli, Exact solutions to the nonlinear dynamics of learning in deep linear neural networks (ICLR 2014). https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6120 — For deep linear nets with whitened inputs and aligned initialization, gradient flow decouples into independent modes of the input–output correlation SVD. Each mode strength follows an exact sigmoidal trajectory, with a plateau and then a rapid transition, taking time ~ (τ/2s)·ln(s/u₀). With orthogonal (dynamically isometric) initialization, learning speed is independent of depth.
   Saxe, McClelland, Ganguli, A mathematical theory of semantic development in deep neural networks (PNAS 116(23), 2019). https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1820226116 (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.10531) — Applies these exact solutions to hierarchically structured data. Stage-like, coarse-to-fine differentiation of concepts arises because singular modes are learned in order of singular value, and the theory explains semantic illusions, typicality and category coherence.
40. Atanasov, Bordelon, Pehlevan, Neural networks as kernel learners: the silent alignment effect (ICLR 2022). https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00034 — From small initialization with whitened data, the NTK aligns its eigenstructure (gaining a low-rank part) while it is still small and before the loss decreases, and afterwards it only grows in scale. So the trained network equals kernel regression with its *final* NTK. This is derived analytically for linear networks, and the effect weakens for non-whitened data.
41. Advani, Saxe, Sompolinsky, High-dimensional dynamics of generalization error in neural networks (Neural Networks 132:428–446, 2020). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neunet.2020.08.022 (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.03667) — Uses random matrix theory (Marchenko–Pastur) and exact linear solutions to derive training and test error dynamics for P ≈ N. Overtraining is worst when the number of parameters ≈ number of samples, which is an early double-descent peak. Small initial weights are needed for good generalization, and large networks are protected because eigen-directions with near-zero eigenvalues barely move.
42. Saxe, Sodhani, Lewallen, The neural race reduction: dynamics of abstraction in gated networks (ICML 2022). https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10430 — Introduces gated deep linear networks, in which gating lets them compute nonlinear functions, and derives an exact reduction of their learning dynamics, with exact solutions in some cases, to a "race" between pathways. The race favours shared representations and determines systematic generalization.
43. Baldi & Hornik, Neural networks and principal component analysis: learning from examples without local minima (Neural Networks 2(1):53–58, 1989). https://doi.org/10.1016/0893-6080(89)90014-2 — For a linear n–p–n network with squared loss, every critical point projects onto the span of some p eigenvectors of Σ = Σ_YX Σ_XX⁻¹ Σ_XY. Only the top-p subspace is a minimum (unique up to invertible reparametrization, hence global), and all other critical points are saddles. In the autoencoder case this is PCA.
   Kawaguchi, Deep learning without poor local minima (NeurIPS 2016). https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07110 — For deep linear networks of any depth and width with squared loss, every local minimum is global and every other critical point is a saddle. "Bad" saddles, with no negative Hessian eigenvalue, exist only for depth > 3. The same holds for nonlinear nets under strong independence assumptions.
44. Arora, Cohen, Hazan, On the optimization of deep networks: implicit acceleration by overparameterization (ICML 2018). https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06509 (PMLR https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/arora18a.html) — From balanced initialization (W_{j+1}ᵀW_{j+1} = W_jW_jᵀ), the end-to-end matrix W_e = W_N⋯W_1 obeys dW_e/dt = −η Σ_{j=1}^{N} [W_eW_eᵀ]^{(j−1)/N} ∇L(W_e) [W_eᵀW_e]^{(N−j)/N}. Here λ = 0; with weight decay there is an extra −ηλN·W_e term. Vectorized, this is a PSD preconditioner with eigenvalues Σ_j σ_r^{2(N−j)/N} σ_{r'}^{2(j−1)/N} that favours directions already large in W_e. For single-output networks with N > 2 it is provably not the gradient of any function, so no regularizer can reproduce it.
45. Tu, Aranguri, Jacot, Mixed dynamics in linear networks: unifying the lazy and active regimes (NeurIPS 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17580 — Gives one simple formula for the evolution of a linear network's learned matrix that covers the lazy, balanced and mixed regimes. In the mixed regime, singular values below a threshold stay lazy and those above it become balanced/active, and this yields a nearly complete phase diagram in initialization variance and width.
   Kunin, Raventós, Dominé, Chen, Klindt, Saxe, Ganguli, Get rich quick: exact solutions reveal how unbalanced initializations promote rapid feature learning (NeurIPS 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06158 — Exact solutions of a minimal model show that layer-wise initialization scales and learning rates set the lazy/rich regime through conserved quantities. In linear nets, rapid feature learning happens only from balanced initialization, while in piecewise-linear nonlinear nets, upstream-heavy unbalanced initialization accelerates rich learning.
46. Chizat, Oyallon, Bach, On lazy training in differentiable programming (NeurIPS 2019). https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.07956 — "Lazy training" (the model behaving as its linearization, i.e. a kernel method) is caused by a choice of scaling, e.g. output multiplied by α → ∞, not by overparameterization itself. The paper bounds the distance between the lazy and linearized paths, and shows that CNNs trained in the lazy regime perform worse.
47. Roberts, Yaida, Hanin, *The Principles of Deep Learning Theory* (Cambridge UP 2022). https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10165 — An effective-field-theory treatment of finite-width networks. Outputs are nearly Gaussian, with corrections controlled by the depth-to-width ratio L/n, and the book derives the resulting NTK evolution and representation learning order by order in 1/n.
   Yang & Hu, Tensor Programs IV: Feature learning in infinite-width neural networks (ICML 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.14522 — Within a natural class of abc-parametrizations, every infinite-width limit either learns features or is kernel gradient descent, never both. The paper derives the Maximal Update Parametrization (μP), which learns features maximally.
   Bordelon & Pehlevan, Self-consistent dynamical field theory of kernel evolution in wide neural networks (NeurIPS 2022). https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09653 — Describes μP/mean-field infinite-width gradient flow through deterministic order parameters: two-time kernels of activations and gradients in each layer, obtained self-consistently through a saddle point, which recovers Tensor Programs' limit. For deep linear nets these reduce to algebraic matrix equations.
   Mei, Montanari, Nguyen, A mean field view of the landscape of two-layer neural networks (PNAS 2018). https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06561 — In the large-width mean-field scaling, SGD on two-layer networks is described by a nonlinear PDE ("distributional dynamics"), a Wasserstein gradient flow on the distribution of neurons. This is used to prove convergence to near-optimal networks, including a general result for noisy SGD.
48. Baldi & Sadowski, Understanding dropout (NeurIPS 2013). https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2013/hash/71f6278d140af599e06ad9bf1ba03cb0-Abstract.html — For linear networks, scaling the weights by p exactly computes the average over dropout subnetworks. For a logistic unit, the normalized weighted geometric mean equals σ(E[S]) exactly, which gives recursive E ≈ NWGM approximations for deep sigmoidal networks. For a single linear unit, the expected dropout gradient equals the ensemble gradient plus an adaptive weight-decay term ½Σ wᵢ²Iᵢ²Var(δᵢ), largest at p = ½. (Old URL https://papers.nips.cc/paper/4878-understanding-dropout redirects to the same page.)
49. Arora, Ge, Neyshabur, Zhang, Stronger generalization bounds for deep nets via a compression approach (ICML 2018). https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05296 — Proves generalization bounds for the compressed network that are orders of magnitude tighter than parameter counting, using noise-stability properties of trained nets. One of these is the **layer cushion** μᵢ: the largest μ such that μ‖Aᵢ‖_F‖φ(x^{i−1})‖ ≤ ‖Aᵢφ(x^{i−1})‖ for all training x, so 1/μᵢ² is layer i's sensitivity to Gaussian noise. Interlayer cushion, activation contraction and interlayer smoothness are the others.
50. D'Angelo, Andriushchenko, Varre, Flammarion, Why do we need weight decay in modern deep learning? (NeurIPS 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04415 — Weight decay is never useful as an explicit regularizer. In multi-epoch vision training it strengthens SGD's implicit regularization through a "loss stabilization" mechanism (keeping the effective learning rate high), and in ~one-epoch LLM training it balances the bias–variance tradeoff of stochastic optimization, lowering training loss and improving stability. (The first author is D'Angelo, not Andriushchenko.)
51. Mukherjee, Yuan, Hakkani-Tür, Peng, Reinforcement learning finetunes small subnetworks in large language models (NeurIPS 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11711 — RL fine-tuning (PPO, GRPO, DPO, …) updates only 5–30% of parameters, yet these sparse updates are nearly full-rank, spread across nearly all matrices, and overlap across seeds and algorithms. Fine-tuning that subnetwork alone reproduces the full model. The authors conjecture that the sparsity comes mainly from training on near-policy data; KL regularization and gradient clipping have limited impact.
   Zhu et al. (H. Zhu, Z. Zhang, …, Y. Tian, K. S. Tai), The path not taken: RLVR provably learns off the principals (2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08567 — Argues that RLVR's apparent sparsity is a model-conditioned optimization bias, explained by a "Three-Gate Theory" (KL anchor, model geometry, bf16 precision). RLVR updates avoid the principal directions of the weights, preserving the spectrum with little rotation of the principal subspace, whereas SFT targets principal weights and distorts the spectrum. (Venue **UNSURE**.)
   Shenfeld, Pari, Agrawal, RL's Razor: why online reinforcement learning forgets less (2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04259 — Forgetting during fine-tuning is predicted by the forward KL between the fine-tuned and base policies on the new task. On-policy RL is implicitly biased toward KL-minimal solutions among those that solve the task, while SFT can move arbitrarily far, so RL forgets less. (Venue **UNSURE**.)
   Cai et al., On predictability of reinforcement learning dynamics for large language models (2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00553 — RL-induced parameter updates show "rank-1 dominance": the top singular subspace of the update recovers over 99% of reasoning gains. They also show "rank-1 linear dynamics": this subspace evolves linearly during training, so the final update can be extrapolated from early checkpoints (the AlphaRL method). (Venue **UNSURE**.)

52. Li & Arora, An exponential learning rate schedule for deep learning (ICLR 2020). https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.07454 — For scale-invariant nets (normalization layers), GD with weight decay and momentum is equivalent to training with an exponentially increasing learning rate and no weight decay.
53. Kosson, Messmer, Jaggi, Rotational equilibrium: how weight decay balances learning across neural networks (ICML 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17212 — Weight decay drives each scale-invariant weight vector to an equilibrium norm where the expected angular update per step is set by η and λ alone (√(2ηλ) for SGD), for SGD, Adam(W) and Lion; balancing this across layers explains part of AdamW's advantage.
54. Wortsman et al., Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities (ICLR 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14322 — Reproduces two large-model instabilities at small scale (attention-logit growth, mitigated by QK-layernorm; output-logit divergence, mitigated by z-loss) and studies how LR sensitivity scales with model size.
55. Molybog et al., A theory on Adam instability in large-scale machine learning (2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09871 — Attributes loss spikes in large LM training to Adam's update components becoming time-correlated (and the update direction uncorrelated with the true gradient) when second-moment estimates go stale for small-gradient parameters.
56. Zhang, He, Sra, Jadbabaie, Why gradient clipping accelerates training: a theoretical justification for adaptivity (ICLR 2020). https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11881 — Introduces (L₀,L₁)-smoothness ‖∇²f‖ ≤ L₀ + L₁‖∇f‖, observed empirically in LSTMs/CNNs, and proves clipped GD can be arbitrarily faster than fixed-step GD under it.
57. Malladi, Wettig, Yu, Chen, Arora, A kernel-based view of language model fine-tuning (ICML 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05643 — Prompt-based fine-tuning of pretrained LMs is often well described by the eNTK (kernel/lazy) regime, with conditions under which this holds and a kernel analysis of why parameter-efficient methods work.
